AI Lab Atlas: The 15-Week Lab Map

Every lab in the 15-week AI Lab sequence, mapped week by week, with difficulty, task counts, and links to the full walkthrough of each lab.

73 labs474 tasks15 weeks11 categoriesDifficulty: 8 Easy, 21 Medium, 26 Hard, 18 Expert
How the week titles were written. The AI Lab site organizes labs by week number with lab lists, not prose week titles. Every week title and description on this page is derived from the actual day lists in that week (day titles, categories, task counts) and written in plain words by the curriculum team. They are honest labels for what the week teaches, not the site's own titles.

How to read this atlas

The AI Lab is a 15-week, 73-lab sequence. This atlas is the map of the whole sequence. Each week chapter below lists every lab day, links to the lab's full walkthrough on its category page (volumes 17 through 27), and shows the difficulty and task count at a glance.

Weeks map to the category volumes. Most weeks belong to one category, and each category owns one volume: weeks 1 and 2 open on Python data tools and math (volumes 17 and 18), weeks 3 and 4 are classical models (volume 19), and so on through agents in week 15 (volume 27). Click any lab link to jump to that volume and read the full task-by-task walkthrough.

Weeks also pair with the theory spine. Volumes 1 through 15 teach the theory; the lab weeks teach the doing. Work them together. Weeks 1 and 2 pair with Volume 1 math and Volume 2 ML foundations, the neural network weeks pair with the deep learning volumes, and the systems weeks pair with the serving and scaling volumes. The lab week never repeats the theory chapter; it assumes you have read it.

Follow the weeks in order. Each week builds on the last. Week 3 assumes the probability from week 2, week 6 assumes the evaluation habits of week 5, and week 11 assumes the transformers of weeks 9 and 10. Skipping ahead works only for review.

How the lab pages work. On each category page, the tab bar at the top jumps to any lab. Every lab opens with a framing paragraph, then tasks are collapsed one by one: read the task, try the starter code yourself, open the hints only when stuck, and open the solution only after you have an answer or a real failure to compare against. Collapsed solutions are the study discipline; do not read ahead.

How to read this diagram

  1. Read the four boxes left to right. Each box is one phase: the week range at the top, the theme in the middle, the lab count and the category volumes at the bottom.
  2. Phase 1 (weeks 1-2, green) is tools and math: 8 Easy labs. Everything after assumes this fluency.
  3. Phase 2 (weeks 3-6, yellow) is classical models plus evaluation habits: 21 Medium labs. This is where interviewers draw baseline questions.
  4. Phase 3 (weeks 7-11, orange) is the deep learning block: 26 Hard labs, from neural nets through transformers to RAG. The biggest phase and the steepest ramp.
  5. Phase 4 (weeks 12-15, red) is production: 18 Expert labs on MLOps, system design, and agents. Fewer labs, but each is a design problem.
  6. The arrows between boxes are the dependency: you cannot do phase 3 well without phase 1, and phase 4 assumes phase 3.

Difficulty progression

How to read this diagram

  1. The x-axis is the week number, 1 on the left through 15 on the right. The y-axis is the number of labs in that week, from 0 at the bottom to 6 at the top.
  2. Each bar is one week. The number above the bar is the lab count for the week.
  3. The color of a bar is its difficulty level: green for Easy, yellow for Medium, orange for Hard, red for Expert. Every week in this curriculum uses a single difficulty level, so each bar is one color.
  4. Read left to right to see the ramp: two green weeks, four yellow weeks, five orange weeks, four red weeks.

The progression is a staircase with four landings. Weeks 1 and 2 are Easy fundamentals: data tools and the math the models need. Weeks 3 through 6 are Medium: classical models, evaluation habits, and the first neural networks. Weeks 7 through 11 are Hard: the full deep learning and transformer block. Weeks 12 through 15 are Expert: production systems, system design, and agents.

The steepest step is week 7, where Medium becomes Hard at the moment training real networks begins. The peak workload is week 10: six labs and 47 tasks, the week you build and train a small language model. The expert block at the end has the fewest labs per week, but each lab is a design problem rather than a coding exercise, so the effort stays high. If you are short on time, protect weeks 5, 7, and 10; those are the weeks interviewers test most often.

Week 1: Python data tools

Three Easy labs build the working surface for everything after: NumPy arrays, pandas tables, and one exploratory data analysis (EDA) session, which means loading a dataset and probing it with plots and summary statistics before modeling. Every later lab assumes you can load a dataset, slice it, and check shapes without thinking. This week exists so tools never become the bottleneck.

  • NumPy, pandas, and EDA are the assumed background for all 70 later labs.

  • The EDA lab's homework sets the weekly rhythm: practice between lab days.

Week 2: The math you will actually use

Five Easy labs cover exactly the math the models need: linear algebra for features, calculus and gradients (the direction of steepest change, used to train models) for optimization, probability for uncertainty, and statistics for reading results. It sits here because weeks 3 through 5 spend this math every day. The final math lab mixes linear algebra with probability, which is the pairing most interview questions combine.

  • All five labs are Easy; the math is applied, not proved.
  • Gradients and probability pay off immediately in week 3.
  • Pair this week with Volume 1 math and Volume 2 ML foundations.

Week 3: Classical models I

The first real models. Logistic regression teaches gradient-based fitting (adjusting parameters by following gradients downhill) and convexity, linear regression introduces generalized linear models (GLMs), and the week closes with k-nearest neighbors and Naive Bayes for distance-based and probabilistic thinking. Problems are small enough that every step is inspectable, which is why classical models come before neural networks.

  • Difficulty steps to Medium and stays there through week 6.
  • Calibration matters: a model's predicted probabilities should match reality.
  • These five models are the baselines interviewers compare everything against.

Week 4: Classical models II

Decision trees, support vector machines, and ensemble methods finish the classical model zoo, then day 4 is a full lab on classical ML failure modes: the ways these models break in practice. Day 5 is a capstone lab combining the two weeks of models. The failure-modes day is the most interview-relevant day of the classical block.

  • Trees plus ensembles are the workhorse models for tabular data.
  • Failure modes are what interviewers actually probe; know them cold.
  • After this week you have a complete classical toolkit before going deep.

Week 5: Evaluation and debugging

The week that separates people who train models from people who can tell whether a model is good. Feature engineering, an evaluation deep dive, the bias-variance tradeoff (simple models underfit, complex models overfit) with regularization, systematic debugging and error analysis, and a PCA lab. Interviewers live in this week: evaluation and debugging questions appear in almost every ML loop.

  • Six labs, the most of any week so far; pace yourself.
  • Error analysis is the highest-leverage debugging skill in the whole curriculum.
  • Everything here transfers directly to deep learning weeks.

Week 6: Neural networks I

The bridge week. It opens with data preprocessing pipelines and data engineering, which are the unglamorous half of deep learning work, then builds the first neural network and closes with a backpropagation lab. Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes gradients through a network's layers so training is possible. This is the last Medium week; it is the on-ramp to the Hard block.

  • Backpropagation is the single concept the deep learning weeks assume.
  • Preprocessing and data engineering return as interview topics in MLOps.
  • Pair this week with the deep learning theory volumes.

Week 7: Neural networks II

The difficulty steps to Hard and networks stop being black boxes. Activation functions and initialization explain how signals survive through layers, optimization methods explain how training actually converges, and day 3 trains a small network end to end. The week closes with CNN basics and residual networks, the architecture family that made deep vision practical.

  • The Medium-to-Hard step here is the steepest in the curriculum.
  • Training one small network yourself teaches more than reading about ten.
  • Optimization questions are favorites in ML theory rounds.

Week 8: CNNs in practice, then text

Three days on making CNNs work: transfer learning and fine-tuning, augmentation strategies, and a full image-classification lab using a frozen backbone. Then the week pivots: text preprocessing and tokenization, then classical NLP methods. The pivot is deliberate. Week 9 starts sequence modeling, and you cannot model sequences without first turning text into numbers.

  • Transfer learning is the practical answer to "I have no data".
  • Tokenization choices follow you into weeks 9 through 11.
  • One week, two modalities: vision practice, language setup.

Week 9: From sequences to attention

The setup week for transformers. Embeddings give words and items learnable vector representations, RNNs and LSTMs show the older way of modeling sequences (briefly, as history), and day 5 introduces self-attention: the mechanism that lets a model weigh which other positions matter for each position. The vectorized softmax lab in between is pure coding craft.

  • Self-attention on day 5 is the seed of everything in weeks 10 and 11.
  • Understand what RNNs got wrong to understand why attention won.
  • Embeddings return as the core of retrieval in week 11.

Week 10: Transformers

The peak week: six labs, 47 tasks, all Hard. The full transformer architecture, LLM training dynamics, a tokenization deep dive, a lab that builds a tiny autoregressive language model offline, a fine-tuning lab, and attention complexity optimizations. An autoregressive language model generates text one token at a time, each token conditioned on the ones before it. By Friday you have built and trained one.

  • The tiny-LM lab is the single most impressive lab to discuss in interviews.
  • Training dynamics questions dominate post-training interview rounds.

Week 11: Retrieval and RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives a language model access to external documents at query time instead of relying only on its weights. The week builds the full stack: embeddings for retrieval, FAISS (a library for fast approximate nearest-neighbor search over vectors), a RAG architecture lab, a lab on RAG tradeoffs, and prompt engineering. The shift is from training models to building systems that use them.

DayLabDifficultyCategoryTasks

  • RAG architecture plus tradeoffs is a complete interview system-design answer.
  • Prompt engineering is a tool, not a theory; treat it as one.
  • This week feeds both post-training rounds and system design rounds.

Week 12: MLOps and production systems

The first Expert week, and the focus changes from building one good model to running many models reliably. MLOps (machine learning operations) covers pipelines and CI/CD (continuous integration and delivery: automated testing and deployment), model registries and versioning, monitoring and drift detection, scaling, and a deployment lab. Interviewers test whether you think like an engineer who ships.

  • First Expert difficulty; the labs are judgment problems, not coding drills.
  • Drift detection is the MLOps topic interviewers ask about most.
  • Pairs with the serving and scaling theory volumes.

Week 13: System design I

Design interviews begin here. The fundamentals of ML system design, feature store patterns, real-time versus batch inference, and case studies of recommendation, search, and ads systems. There is no single right answer in a design round; the skill being trained is reasoning about tradeoffs at scale and defending choices.

DayLabDifficultyCategoryTasks
Day 1ML System Design FundamentalsExpertSystem Design8
Day 2Feature Store PatternsExpertSystem Design6
Day 3Real-Time vs Batch InferenceExpertSystem Design5
Day 4Case Studies , Recs, Search, AdsExpertSystem Design5
  • Feature stores are the canonical "have you built real systems" topic.
  • Case studies give you reusable story structure for any design question.
  • Do these labs out loud; design rounds are performed, not written.

Week 14: System design II

Harder designs. A full real-time recommendation system, LLM inference system design, a feature store and data pipeline deep dive, and distributed training at scale. This week pairs directly with distributed training and inference-serving interview rounds; the scaling-training lab is the bridge to the GPU and systems volumes.

DayLabDifficultyCategoryTasks
Day 1Design Real-Time Recommendation SystemExpertSystem Design6
Day 3LLM Inference System DesignExpertSystem Design4
Day 4Feature Stores & Data Pipelines (Deep Dive)ExpertSystem Design6
Day 5Scaling Training , Distributed MLExpertSystem Design4
  • Distributed training is where theory meets hardware reality.
  • LLM inference design combines week 10 architecture with week 12 serving.
  • Recommendation design is the most-asked ML system design question.

Week 15: LLM and agentic AI

The capstone. LLMs in production, an agents introduction, multi-agent systems in production, inference-time scaling with verifiers, and self-improving agents. An agent is a system that uses a language model to plan and take actions, not just generate text. Everything from the previous fourteen weeks converges here: transformers from week 10, RAG from week 11, serving from week 12, design from weeks 13 and 14.

  • The finale is forward-looking; expect these topics in senior and research loops.
  • Inference-time scaling is the newest interview topic on this page.
  • Revisit week 5 debugging habits here; agents fail in new ways.
