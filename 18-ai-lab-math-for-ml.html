Math for ML: The Machinery Underneath

Turn mathematical fluency into working code: projections and least squares you can derive, gradients you can check, Bayes updates you can simulate, and statistics you can defend in an interview.

Why this category exists

Volume 1 built the mathematical foundations: vectors, derivatives, probability, and statistics as ideas. This volume is where those ideas become code you can run, numbers you can check, and derivations you can defend out loud. An interviewer does not ask whether you have heard of least squares. They ask you to derive the normal equations on the board, or to explain why your gradient is right, or to compute a posterior without flinching. This volume is the practice ground for exactly that.

Without these skills, the rest of the curriculum is cargo cult. You can call np.linalg.lstsq without knowing what a projection is, and you will, right up until a shape bug or a singular matrix gives you nonsense with no error message. You can write a loss function without ever gradient-checking it, and you will, right up until the model does not learn and you cannot tell whether the bug is in the math or the code. You can quote a p-value without understanding coverage, and you will, right up until someone asks what it means.

The five labs are all rated Easy, and the rating refers to the mechanics: the NumPy is elementary, the simulations are short. The difficulty is in the reasoning. The Linear Algebra lab drills the FAANG gotchas explicitly: assert shapes, never form the explicit inverse. The Calculus lab makes finite differences the arbiter of every gradient you write. The Probability lab centers the classic disease-test interview question and the log-space habit. The Statistics lab attacks p-value misreadings head on. The Math Lab is the capstone: five derivation problems, each paired with a computational check.

Core ideas, worked end to end

Concept 1: Projections, least squares, and PSD matrices

1. What it is, in plain words. A projection finds the closest point to a vector v inside a subspace, for example the line spanned by a vector u. Least squares chooses the weights w that minimize the squared error ||Xw - y||^2. A matrix A is positive semidefinite (PSD) when v^T A v is never negative for any vector v. SVD (singular value decomposition) factors a matrix as X = U S V^T, revealing its principal directions.

2. Why it exists. Least squares is the workhorse fit behind linear regression, and the lab shows it is nothing but projection: Xw must be the projection of y onto the column space of X. The PSD concept matters because covariance matrices and X^T X are PSD, and PSD is what guarantees that a quadratic objective has a single global minimum instead of saddle-shaped traps. SVD is the engine inside PCA, which the lab implements from scratch.

3. How it works, step by step. Step one: the projection of v onto u is (u^T v / u^T u) u, the component of v along u. Step two: the residual v minus the projection is orthogonal to u; orthogonality is the defining property. Step three: for least squares, demand that the residual y - Xw be orthogonal to every column of X, which gives the normal equations X^T X w = X^T y. Step four: solve with np.linalg.solve or lstsq, never by forming the explicit inverse, which is slower and numerically worse. Step five: for PCA, center X, take its SVD, and keep the top-k columns of V as the principal directions.

4. Concrete numbers. Fitting 10 features on 1,000 rows: np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y) returns w in milliseconds. Forming np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) first costs roughly 3x the flops and squares the condition number, so a mildly collinear feature set that solve handles fine can make the inverse path return garbage. The lab has you verify X^T X is PSD by testing v^T A v over many random vectors, turning an abstract definition into a check you can run.

5. The common misunderstanding. Beginners think the normal equations say "multiply by the inverse". The equations say the residual is orthogonal to the column space; the inverse is just one (bad) way to compute the answer. The second misunderstanding is treating SVD as a black box: the lab shows the singular vectors of centered X are exactly the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, which is why PCA via SVD and PCA via eigendecomposition agree.

How to read this diagram

  1. The horizontal line is span(u), the set of all multiples of the vector u.
  2. The blue arrow v is the target we want to reach, but it does not lie in the subspace.
  3. The gold arrow proj_u(v) is the closest point to v inside the subspace, computed as (u^T v / u^T u) u.

  5. Least squares generalizes this: with a matrix X, the best Xw is the projection of y onto the column space of X, so the residual is orthogonal to every column. That orthogonality condition is the normal equations.

7. Interview relevance. "Why does projection show up in linear regression?" and "where does PSD show up in ML?" are listed as interview angles in the labs themselves. The expected answer is the picture above: least squares is projection, and PSD (covariance, kernels, Hessians) is what makes minima well-behaved.

Key takeaways

  • Projection is the closest point in a subspace; least squares is projection of y onto the column space of X.
  • The normal equations come from orthogonality of the residual, not from "multiplying by the inverse".
  • Use solve or lstsq, never the explicit inverse: faster and numerically stabler.
  • X^T X is always PSD; PCA directions are the top singular vectors of centered X.

Concept 2: Gradients, the chain rule, and numerical checking

1. What it is, in plain words. A gradient is the vector of partial derivatives of a scalar loss with respect to each parameter; it points in the direction of steepest increase. The chain rule computes derivatives of composed functions by multiplying the derivatives of the pieces. Finite differences approximate a derivative numerically as (f(w+e) - f(w-e)) / 2e. The Hessian is the matrix of second derivatives; it describes curvature.

2. Why it exists. Every model in this curriculum is trained by following gradients downhill. If your gradient code is wrong, training silently optimizes the wrong objective: loss decreases, nothing learns, and no error is raised. Finite differences exist as the independent arbiter. They are too slow for training but perfect for verification, because they use only function evaluations and cannot share your derivation bug.

3. How it works, step by step. Step one: write the forward computation as a chain of simple ops (affine transform, sigmoid, loss). Step two: differentiate each op and multiply them in reverse order; that is backpropagation by hand. Step three: implement the gradient in vectorized NumPy, watching the two classic bug sites: missing constants like 2/n, and broadcasting shape mismatches. Step four: check it. Perturb each parameter by a small e in both directions, compute the central difference, and compare against your analytic gradient. Step five: for curvature questions, build the Hessian numerically the same way and inspect its eigenvalues; all non-negative means a local minimum.

4. Concrete numbers. For MSE loss L(w) = (1/n) sum (Xw - y)^2, the gradient is (2/n) X^T (Xw - y). Forget the 2/n and your gradient is off by a constant factor; with n = 10,000 that is a 20,000x error in step size, and gradient descent either crawls or explodes. The lab's central-difference check typically agrees with a correct analytic gradient to about 1e-5 relative error with e around 1e-5; agreement worse than 1e-3 means a bug.

5. The common misunderstanding. Beginners trust the analytic gradient because the math "looked right". The lab's position is blunt: most gradient bugs are shape bugs or missing constants, and neither is visible in the derivation. The second misunderstanding is using a one-sided difference (f(w+e) - f(w)) / e, which has error proportional to e, instead of the central difference, whose error is proportional to e squared and is therefore far more accurate for the same e.

How to read this diagram

  1. The blue curve is the loss f(w); the gold dot marks the point w where you check.
  2. The solid green secant line is the finite-difference slope: evaluate f at w+e and w-e, divide the difference by 2e.
  3. The dashed red tangent is the slope your analytic chain-rule code claims.
  4. If they agree to about 1e-5, the analytic gradient is correct. If they disagree, the finite-difference version is the ground truth and the bug is in your derivation or code.
  5. The lab mantra, quoted from the lab itself: if you cannot gradient-check it, you do not really trust it.

7. Interview relevance. The binary cross-entropy gradient (sigmoid(Xw), loss, gradient X^T(p - y)/n) is flagged as the core interview gradient in the lab. "How would you debug an autodiff bug in production?" expects exactly this answer: isolate the op, finite-difference it, binary search the chain.

Key takeaways

  • The gradient points uphill; training steps go the opposite way. Chain rule multiplies the pieces in reverse.
  • The two classic gradient bugs: missing constants (2/n) and broadcasting shape mismatches.
  • Central finite differences are the ground truth for verification; agreement to ~1e-5 means the code is right.
  • The Hessian's eigenvalues describe curvature; the lab builds it numerically to make that concrete.

Concept 3: Bayes' rule, base rates, and log-space computation

1. What it is, in plain words. Bayes' rule updates a belief with evidence: P(D|+) = P(+|D) P(D) / P(+). The prior P(D) is what you believed before the evidence; the posterior P(D|+) is what you believe after. Expectation is the probability-weighted average of a random variable; variance measures its spread. Computing in log-space means working with log probabilities so products become sums.

2. Why it exists. ML is applied belief updating: spam filters, medical tests, and recommendation scores are all posteriors. The base rate matters because rare events stay rare even after positive evidence, and engineers who ignore the prior ship systems that cry wolf. Log-space exists because multiplying hundreds of small probabilities underflows to zero in floating point; adding their logs does not.

3. How it works, step by step. Step one: write down the prior, for example disease prevalence 1%. Step two: write the likelihoods, sensitivity P(+|D) and false positive rate P(+|healthy). Step three: compute the total probability of a positive test across both groups. Step four: divide the true-positive mass by the total positive mass; that ratio is the posterior. Step five: sanity-check by simulation. Sample many synthetic patients, apply the test probabilistically, and confirm the empirical fraction matches the analytic answer. Step six: when chaining many likelihoods (as in Naive Bayes), sum log probabilities instead of multiplying raw ones.

4. Concrete numbers. The lab's disease-test example: prevalence 0.01, sensitivity 0.99, false positive rate 0.05. True positives per 10,000 people: 99. False positives: 495. Posterior: 99 / 594 = 16.7%. A "99% accurate test" leaves a positive patient still 83% likely to be healthy. The simulation check in the lab reproduces 16.7% empirically, which is how you learn to trust both the math and the code.

5. The common misunderstanding. Beginners read "99% sensitive" as "99% chance I am sick if I test positive". That confuses P(+|D) with P(D|+), the exact reversal Bayes' rule exists to fix. The second misunderstanding is multiplying probabilities in a long chain and wondering why everything becomes zero; the lab's gotcha names underflow explicitly and the Naive Bayes mini-task moves to log-odds.

How to read this diagram

  1. Start with 10,000 people and 1% prevalence: 100 sick, 9,900 healthy.
  2. The sick group (red box): 99% sensitivity means 99 test positive.
  3. The healthy group (green box): a 5% false positive rate on 9,900 people yields 495 positive tests, five times more than the true positives.
  4. Bayes rule combines them: P(sick | positive) = 99 / (99 + 495) = 16.7%.
  5. The lesson: the posterior depends on the base rate, not just the test quality. Interviewers ask this to check whether you update on evidence correctly.

7. Interview relevance. The disease-test question is one of the most asked probability questions in ML interviews, and the lab lists "why can a 99% accurate test still have low P(D|+)?" as an interview angle. The expected answer is the base-rate arithmetic above, delivered without hesitation, plus the log-space habit for real implementations.

Key takeaways

  • Bayes' rule: posterior = likelihood times prior, divided by total evidence. Never skip the prior.
  • A rare disease plus a good test still yields mostly false positives; the healthy majority dominates.
  • Verify analytic posteriors with simulation; the two should agree.
  • Multiply many probabilities in log-space; raw products underflow to zero.

Concept 4: Confidence intervals, p-values, and the multiple-comparisons trap

1. What it is, in plain words. A confidence interval (CI) is a range computed from data that captures the true parameter in a stated fraction of repeated experiments. A p-value is the probability of seeing data this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. A permutation test builds the null distribution by shuffling labels instead of assuming a formula. The Bonferroni correction divides the significance threshold by the number of tests run.

2. Why it exists. ML engineers constantly ask "is B really better than A": model comparisons, A/B tests, feature selection. Without uncertainty quantification, every lucky fluctuation looks like a breakthrough and every dashboard slice becomes a false discovery. The lab's coverage simulation exists to make the CI definition visceral, and the permutation test exists because real data rarely satisfies textbook assumptions.

3. How it works, step by step. Step one: estimate from data, for example the sample mean and the unbiased sample variance (dividing by n-1, not n, which corrects the downward bias). Step two: form the interval as mean +/- z times s/sqrt(n), with z about 1.96 for 95%. Step three: verify coverage by simulation: repeat the whole procedure thousands of times on known data and count how often the interval contains the truth. Step four: for comparing two groups, run a permutation test: shuffle the group labels many times, recompute the difference each time, and see where the observed difference falls in that shuffled distribution. Step five: if you ran m tests, compare each p-value against alpha/m, not alpha.

4. Concrete numbers. With n = 100 samples from a standard normal, the 95% CI is roughly mean +/- 0.196. The lab's coverage simulation shows about 95% of such intervals contain the true mean 0, not exactly 95% in any finite run, which teaches that coverage is a long-run property. With m = 20 tests at alpha = 0.05, Bonferroni uses 0.0025 per test; without it, the chance of at least one false positive across 20 tests is about 64%.

5. The common misunderstanding. The lab names the two big ones as gotchas: a CI is for the mean, not for individual outcomes, and a p-value is not P(H0 is true). "95% confident the mean is in here" is wrong; the correct reading is "this procedure captures the true mean 95% of the time". The second trap is p-hacking by slicing: every dashboard slice is a test, and uncorrected slicing manufactures discoveries, which is why the lab pairs the permutation test with Bonferroni.

How to read this diagram

  1. Each horizontal line is one 95% confidence interval from one simulated experiment; the dot is that experiment's sample mean.
  2. The dashed vertical line is the true mean, which the experimenter does not know.
  3. Nineteen of the twenty intervals cross the dashed line; one (red) misses. That is the meaning of 95% coverage: a property of the procedure over repetitions, not a 95% probability about any single interval.
  4. The lab has you run this simulation yourself so the definition becomes a picture, not a sentence.
  5. The trap to avoid: "there is a 95% chance the true mean is in this interval" is wrong. The interval either contains it or not; 95% describes the method.

7. Interview relevance. "Why does a CI have ~95% coverage by design?" and "slicing dashboards can create accidental p-hacking" are the lab's own interview angles. The expected answers are the simulation picture above and the Bonferroni arithmetic, stated in plain sentences.

Key takeaways

  • Use the unbiased variance (divide by n-1); the CI is mean +/- 1.96 s/sqrt(n) for large n.
  • Coverage is a property of the procedure over repetitions, verified by simulation.
  • A p-value is P(data this extreme | H0), not P(H0 is true); it says nothing about practical importance.
  • Divide alpha by the number of tests (Bonferroni) or your dashboard will manufacture discoveries.

Design prompt

  • You tested 20 features and one has p = 0.03. Is it significant? Show the correction.
  • Explain the permutation test to someone who has never seen a t-test.

def proj(u, v):
    denom = dot(u, u)
    if denom == 0:
        raise ValueError('u must be non-zero')
    return (dot(u, v) / denom) * u

def permutation_pvalue(A, B, n_perm=5000, two_sided=True):
    A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float)
    B = np.asarray(B, dtype=float)
    obs = B.mean() - A.mean()
    pooled = np.concatenate([A, B])
    nA = A.size
    diffs = np.empty(n_perm)
    for i in range(n_perm):
        perm = rng.permutation(pooled)
        diffs[i] = perm[nA:].mean() - perm[:nA].mean()
    if two_sided:
        p = np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(obs))
    else:
        p = np.mean(diffs >= obs)
    return float(p)

Q1. Which statement about the normal equations is correct? A: They require forming the matrix inverse explicitly. B: They state that the residual is orthogonal to the column space of X. C: They only apply when X is square. D: They minimize the L1 error.

Answer: B. The normal equations X^T X w = X^T y are the algebraic form of "the residual y - Xw is orthogonal to every column of X".

  • A, wrong: the inverse is one way to solve them, and the numerically worst one; solve or lstsq is preferred.
  • B, right: orthogonality of the residual is the geometric content; everything else is algebra.
  • C, wrong: X is typically tall (n rows, d columns); squareness is not required.
  • D, wrong: least squares minimizes squared (L2) error, not L1.

Q2. Your analytic gradient and your central-difference check disagree by 0.5 on one parameter. List the two most likely bug sites, in the order you would check them.

Answer: first, a missing constant (like 2/n or 1/n) in the analytic gradient; second, a broadcasting shape bug where two arrays aligned on the wrong axis. Both are named as the classic gradient bugs in the lab. Check the constant by comparing magnitudes (a clean ratio like exactly n or 2 suggests the missing factor), then print shapes at every step of the chain.

Q3. A startup claims its model "detects disease with 99% accuracy" on a condition affecting 1 in 1,000 people. What is wrong with this claim?

Answer: a model that always predicts "healthy" scores 99.9% accuracy on this population while detecting nothing. Accuracy without the base rate is meaningless under imbalance. The right questions: what are the sensitivity and false positive rate, and what is P(disease | positive)? With a 5% false positive rate, the posterior is roughly 2%, so 98 of 100 alarms are false.

Q4. You run 20 experiments, one per feature, and the best p-value is 0.03. Your colleague wants to ship that feature. What do you say, with numbers?

Answer: with 20 tests at alpha 0.05, you expect about one false positive by chance alone; the chance of at least one is 1 - 0.95^20, about 64%. Bonferroni requires 0.05/20 = 0.0025 per test, and 0.03 does not clear it. The result is consistent with noise. Ship nothing on this evidence; re-test the feature alone on fresh data if you believe in it.
