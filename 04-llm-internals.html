1. Tokenization from zero

Turning raw text into the integer IDs a neural network can actually read.

Design prompt

  • "Why don't language models just use words as tokens? What breaks?"
  • "Walk me through BPE training on a small corpus. Then encode a new word with the merges you learned."
  • "What is token fertility, and why does it matter for cost and context windows?"

Step 1: the simplest picture

A neural network cannot read text. It reads numbers. So the first thing any language model needs is a fixed dictionary that turns pieces of text into integers. That dictionary is called the vocabulary. Its entries are called tokens. The step that converts text to token IDs is tokenization.

Concrete example: in GPT-2's vocabulary, the string "Hello" is token ID 15496. The model never sees the letters H-e-l-l-o. It sees the integer 15496, looks up a vector for it (Chapter 2), and goes. Everything downstream, attention, loss, sampling, works on these IDs. Get this mapping wrong and nothing else can be right, which is why interviews probe it.

Step 2: the one design decision

Once you accept that text must become integer IDs, one decision remains: what should a token be? Three options exist. Two of them fail, and the failure modes are the whole reason subword tokenization exists.

OptionWhat it meansWhat breaks
WordsEach distinct word is one tokenVocabulary explosion and unknown words. English has hundreds of thousands of words, and new ones appear daily. Every word needs its own vector (Chapter 2), so the embedding table becomes enormous. Worse, any word not seen in training becomes an OOV (out-of-vocabulary) token, usually mapped to a single "unknown" ID. The model goes blind on exactly the rare words that carry meaning. Agglutinative languages and typos make this catastrophic.
CharactersEach character (or byte) is one tokenSequences get too long. A 1,000-word article is roughly 5,000 characters. Attention costs grow with the square of sequence length (Chapter 3), so character models pay roughly 25 times the attention cost of word models for the same text. The model must also re-learn spelling from scratch at every position.
SubwordsFrequent strings become single tokens; rare strings split into piecesNothing structural. Common words like " the" are one token, rare words split into reusable pieces like " token" + "ization". No unknown words ever (any string decomposes into known pieces, down to single bytes). This is what every modern LLM uses.
Key idea: subword tokenization is a compression tradeoff. Frequent strings earn short representations (one token). Rare strings pay with longer ones (many tokens). The tokenizer learns which strings are frequent from training data.

Step 3: how BPE learns its vocabulary

Subwords win, but who decides which strings deserve to be single tokens? BPE (byte pair encoding), the algorithm behind GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, and LLaMA tokenizers, learns the answer from data (borrowed from compression; Sennrich et al., 2016). Two phases: training learns merge rules from a corpus; encoding applies them to new text.

Training, step by step

  1. Start tiny. Split every word in the training corpus into individual characters (plus a special end-of-word marker, often written </w>). The starting vocabulary is just the alphabet plus the marker.
  2. Count every adjacent pair. Walk the whole corpus and count how often each pair of neighboring symbols occurs, weighted by word frequency. ("l","o") might occur 7 times, ("o","w") 7 times, ("e","s") 9 times.
  3. Merge the winner. Take the single most frequent pair and merge it into one new symbol. ("e","s") becomes the new token "es". Add it to the vocabulary. This is one merge rule, and its order is recorded (rule #1, rule #2, ...).
  4. Repeat. Re-split the corpus using the merges learned so far, recount pairs, merge the new winner. Do this a fixed number of times (tens of thousands for real models). Each merge adds exactly one token to the vocabulary.

Notice what this learns: the first merges are always the most frequent letter pairs in the language ("th", "he", "in", "er" for English). Later merges build whole morphemes and common words. The vocabulary ends up as a mix of characters, pieces, and whole frequent words, exactly matching the statistics of the training text.

Encoding new text, step by step

  1. Split the input into characters.
  2. Apply the learned merge rules in the order they were learned, greedily, wherever they match. Rule #1 first, then #2, and so on.
  3. When no more rules apply, look up each resulting piece in the vocabulary and emit its integer ID.

Decoding is trivial: concatenate the token strings. That is why tokenizers are lossless. Every byte sequence can be represented, because in the worst case the text falls back to single bytes.

How to read this diagram

  1. Panel 1 (Initial Tokens): the corpus split into letters. "low" appears 5 times, "lower" 2 times. Each letter is its own tile. This is the starting vocabulary.
  2. Panel 2 (Pair Frequency): every adjacent letter pair is counted across the corpus. The pair "l"+"o" appears in "low" (5 times) and "lower" (2 times), total 7, the highest count. The blue highlight marks the winner.
  3. Panel 3 (Merge): "l" and "o" fuse into one tile, "lo", a brand-new vocabulary entry. "low" is now two tokens ("lo","w") instead of three. "lower" becomes ("lo","w","e","r").

What breaks if a step fails: if pair counting ignored word frequencies, rare words would dominate the merges and the vocabulary would memorize noise. If merges applied out of order at encode time, two identical strings could tokenize differently, and the model would see inconsistent IDs for the same text.

Read the pattern: the most frequent words get absorbed into single tokens first ("newest" after 8 merges, "low" after 9). The rare word "widest" stays fragmented. That is the compression tradeoff in action, and it is exactly what happens at scale: frequent strings earn short codes.

Step 5: what this costs in production

Real vocabularies: GPT-2 has 50,257 tokens, LLaMA models use 32,000, GPT-4's cl100k_base has about 100,000. A larger vocabulary means fewer tokens per sentence, but a bigger embedding table and a bigger final softmax. The tradeoff between vocabulary size and sequence length is picked up in Chapter 2 and Volume 5.

Fertility means the average number of tokens produced per word. It measures how wasteful a tokenizer is on a given language. Illustrative numbers: English text runs about 1.2 to 1.4 tokens per word on modern tokenizers. Code runs higher (indentation and symbols fragment). Many non-English languages run much higher, sometimes several tokens per word, because the tokenizer's merge statistics were learned mostly on English.

Why fertility matters in practice: context windows and API prices are counted in tokens, not words. A 4,096-token window holds roughly 3,000 English words but far fewer words of a high-fertility language. Two users with the same word count can pay very different prices and hit the context limit at very different points. When someone says "the model supports 128k context," always ask: 128k tokens of what?
Common misunderstanding: "tokens are words." They are not. The word "tokenization" itself might split into "token" + "ization". A token can be a whole word, part of a word, a single character, or even part of a UTF-8 byte sequence. Reviewers probe this because engineers who think in words misjudge context usage, misjudge cost, and get confused when the model splits a word in a surprising place (for example, a number like "12345" becoming "12"+"345", which is part of why LLMs fumble arithmetic).
Field relevance: tokenization questions test whether you understand the model's actual input. The strong candidate can trace BPE merges on a toy example without notes and explain fertility in one sentence.

Key takeaways

  • Tokenization maps text to integer IDs. The model only ever sees the IDs.
  • Subword tokenization (BPE) beats words (OOV explosion) and characters (quadratic attention cost).
  • BPE training: count adjacent pairs, merge the most frequent, repeat. Encoding: apply merges greedily in learned order.
  • Fertility (tokens per word) turns token budgets into words and dollars. It varies sharply by language.

2. Embeddings: points in a learned space

What a token ID becomes before the model can think with it.

Chapter 1 turned text into integers and left a question hanging: what can a model actually do with bare integers? This chapter answers it. Every token gets a point in a learned space, and geometry does the rest.

Design prompt

  • "What is an embedding, geometrically? What does distance between two embeddings mean?"
  • "Are embeddings learned or fixed? What would break if you froze them?"
  • "How many parameters live in the embedding table of a 7B model? Show the arithmetic."

Step 1: from integer to point

An embedding is a vector of floating-point numbers that stands in for one token. The model keeps a giant lookup table, the embedding matrix, with one row per vocabulary entry. Token ID 15496 ("Hello") becomes row 15496 of that table, a vector like [0.13, -0.87, 0.44, ...] with one number per model dimension. If the model dimension is 4,096, every token becomes a point in a 4,096-dimensional space.

Geometrically, that is all an embedding is: a point. Tokens whose points land near each other get treated similarly by the rest of the network. "Distance" between embeddings is the model's working definition of similarity. Nothing about this is programmed. The points start random and get pulled into useful positions by gradient descent (Volume 1) during training.

Step 2: why vectors, and why learned ones

An integer ID like 15496 is nearly useless as input to arithmetic: IDs have no notion of similarity (15495 and 15496 are adjacent IDs but unrelated tokens), and a single number carries almost no information. Expanding each ID into a high-dimensional vector buys two things: capacity (4,096 numbers encode far more than one integer) and geometry (similar tokens can sit near each other, so what the model learns about one generalizes to neighbors).

Two designs exist for filling the table:

DesignWhat it meansWhere you see it
FixedVectors chosen up front, never updated. The simplest fixed embedding is one-hot: token i becomes a vector that is 1 at position i and 0 elsewhere.Old pipelines, and frozen word2vec/GloVe vectors reused as features.
LearnedVectors start random and are updated by backpropagation along with every other parameter.Every modern LLM. The embedding table is just another weight matrix the optimizer trains.

Fixed one-hot embeddings fail for the same reason integer IDs fail: every token is equally far from every other token, so there is no similarity to exploit, and the vectors are vocabulary-sized (50,257 dimensions for GPT-2), which is enormous and mostly zeros. Learned dense embeddings fix both: compact (a few thousand dimensions) and geometrically meaningful. If you froze a modern model's embeddings at their random starting values, training would still work, but the model would have to encode all token identity information in later layers, wasting capacity that could have held semantics.

Step 3: the full picture: size, geometry, and limits

The embedding table is often the single largest matrix in a small model. LLaMA-style 7B: vocabulary 32,000 x model width 4,096 = 131,072,000 parameters, about 2% of the model. GPT-3 scale: 50,257 x 12,288 = 617 million parameters. There is also an output embedding (the final layer that maps hidden states back to vocabulary scores, covered in Volume 5). Some models tie the input and output embeddings, reusing one matrix for both directions, which saves parameters and is a favorite field detail.

How to read this diagram

  1. Each labeled point is a token's embedding projected down to 2D so human eyes can see it. The real vectors have thousands of dimensions.
  2. The dashed arrows are vector differences. "queen" minus "king" points in roughly the same direction as "woman" minus "man". That shared direction is what people call the "royal" or gender direction.
  3. Why this is possible at all: tokens that appear in similar contexts get pulled to similar regions during training. "king" and "queen" share contexts (crowns, thrones, reigns), so their points end up near each other, offset along the direction that distinguishes the contexts where they differ.

What this diagram does not prove: that the model "understands" royalty, or that vector arithmetic is reliable reasoning. The analogy works for frequent, cleanly-separated concepts and degrades for rare or tangled ones. It is a property of the geometry, not a reasoning engine.

Common misunderstanding: "the embedding of a word is its meaning, fixed forever." Two traps in one. First, the embedding is context-free: the token "bank" gets the same vector in "river bank" and "bank loan". Meaning in context is built later, by attention (Chapter 3). Second, embeddings are not definitions. They are positions that happened to be useful for predicting text. A token's nearest neighbors are a hint about its distributional behavior, not a dictionary entry.
Field relevance: embedding questions are warm-ups that separate memorizers from understanders. The strong candidate draws the lookup ("row i of a V x d matrix"), does the parameter arithmetic unprompted, and volunteers the context-free limitation before being asked. Tying input and output embeddings is a sharp detail to have ready.

Key takeaways

  • An embedding is a learned vector per token: a point in a high-dimensional space where distance means similarity.
  • Modern LLMs learn embeddings with everything else. Fixed one-hot vectors waste the geometry.
  • The table costs V x d parameters (131M at 7B scale). Tying input and output embeddings saves a copy.
  • Embeddings are context-free. Attention (next chapter) is what puts them in context.

3. Attention, fully worked: every number by hand

The mechanism at the center of every LLM, computed here with pencil-sized matrices.

Chapter 2's embeddings are context-free: the vector for 'bank' is identical in 'river bank' and 'bank loan'. This chapter fixes that gap. Attention lets every token rewrite its vector using every other token's, and this chapter works the whole mechanism by hand, number by number.

Design prompt

  • "Compute one attention head by hand on these matrices. Show Q, K, V, the scores, the mask, the softmax, and the output."
  • "Why divide by the square root of d_k? What breaks if you don't?"
  • "Explain attention as differentiable retrieval. What are the query, the keys, and the values?"
  • "What is the time and memory complexity of attention in sequence length?"

Step 1: the problem and the idea

Embeddings (Chapter 2) are context-free: "bank" is the same vector in "river bank" and "bank loan". But meaning depends on context. In "the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", the word "it" needs information from "animal", not "street". Older architectures handled this with recurrence (read left to right, one step at a time, which cannot be parallelized) or fixed windows.

Attention solves it directly: every token builds a new vector by taking a weighted average of all tokens' vectors, where the weights measure how relevant each token is to the current one. Each token asks a question ("what should I look at?"), compares it against every token's label, and mixes the tokens' contents in proportion to the match. All positions are processed at once, any token can directly reach any other regardless of distance, and the whole thing parallelizes perfectly. The price is quadratic cost in sequence length, which is why long context is expensive (Step 5 works it out).

One gap remains: nothing in these five steps sees word order. Shuffle the tokens and the outputs shuffle identically, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look the same to this mechanism. Chapter 4 closes that gap with positional encodings. Until then, follow the ritual as stated.

Step 2: the five-step ritual

One attention head is five steps. Learn these as a fixed ritual:

  1. Project. Multiply the input matrix X (one row per token) by three learned matrices: Q = XWQ (queries), K = XWK (keys), V = XWV (values). Same input, three different learned views of it.
  2. Score. Compute S = QKT / sqrt(dk). Entry (i, j) is the dot product of token i's query with token j's key: how much token i wants to look at token j. Divide by sqrt(dk) to keep the numbers in a range softmax can handle (below).
  3. Mask (for decoders). Set every score above the diagonal to negative infinity. Token i may only look at tokens 0 through i, never the future. This is the causal mask. Without it, training would let the model peek at the answer it is supposed to predict.
  4. Normalize. Apply softmax to each row. The scores become non-negative weights that sum to 1.
  5. Mix. Output = weights x V. Each token's output is a weighted average of the value vectors, weighted by relevance.

How to read this diagram

  1. Left column: input tokens ("How", "are", "you", "doing", "?") become one embedding matrix. One input, three learned projections.
  2. WK, WQ, WV: the three projection matrices. They are the head's learned parameters. Everything the head "knows" lives here.
  3. Keys x Queries: the score matrix. Each cell is one query-key dot product, one relevance judgment.
  4. Softmax: rows become probability distributions over positions.
  5. Weights x Values: the weighted mix. The output "Hidden states" have the same shape as the input embeddings, so heads stack cleanly.

What breaks if a step fails: skip the mask and the model cheats during training (it sees the future), then fails at generation time when the future is genuinely absent. Skip the sqrt(dk) scaling and the softmax saturates (next section).

Interlude: the two equations, written out

The five steps above are compact algebra. Here are the two equations behind steps 2 and 4, with every symbol named.

softmax(z)i = exp(zi) / Σj exp(zj)
where: z is the score vector (one number per position); zi is the score for position i; exp is the exponential, which makes every term positive; the denominator sums over all positions j, so the outputs are non-negative and sum to 1.

Softmax, formally: Vol 1 §3.2. The probability chapter of Math for ML builds softmax from zero: exponentials, normalization, and why the outputs form a valid probability distribution.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / sqrt(dk)) V
where: Q is the n x dk query matrix (each row asks what one token looks for); K is the n x dk key matrix (each row labels what one token offers); V is the n x dv value matrix (each row holds one token's content); dk is the key and query width, usually 64; QKT is the n x n score matrix from Step 2. Step 3 below is this equation worked end to end on real numbers.
Worked example: softmax by hand. Take row 1 of Step 3's masked scores: [1.4142, 1.4142, -inf]. Exponentiate: exp(1.4142) = 4.1131, twice; exp(-inf) = 0. Denominator: 4.1131 + 4.1131 + 0 = 8.2262. Each of the first two weights: 4.1131 / 8.2262 = 0.5. The last: 0 / 8.2262 = 0. Result: [0.5, 0.5, 0], and the row sums to 1.0, exactly as printed in Step 3.

Step 3: every number by hand

Three tokens, model width 4, one head with key/query dimension 2. Every number below was produced by running the NumPy lab at the end of this volume. Follow along with a calculator.

Inputs. Token embeddings X (3 tokens x 4 dims) and the head's three projection matrices (4x2 each):

X (token embeddings)          W_Q                 W_K                 W_V
  "the" [1, 0, 1, 0]           [1, 0]              [1, 1]              [1, 0]
  "cat" [0, 1, 0, 1]           [0, 1]              [0, 0]              [0, 2]

raw QK^T = [[4, 0, 2],      scaled (/1.4142) = [[2.8284, 0,      1.4142],
              [2, 2, 2],                           [1.4142, 1.4142, 1.4142],
              [3, 1, 2]]                           [2.1213, 0.7071, 1.4142]]

Step 3: causal mask. Above-diagonal cells become negative infinity (token 0 sees only token 0; token 1 sees tokens 0-1; token 2 sees all three).

masked = [[ 2.8284,    -inf,    -inf],

Step 4: softmax per row. Row 1: exp(1.4142) = 4.1131 twice, so [0.5, 0.5, 0]. Row 2: exp(2.1213) = 8.342, exp(0.7071) = 2.028, exp(1.4142) = 4.113; sum = 14.483; weights = [0.576, 0.140, 0.284].

weights = [[1.000, 0.000, 0.000],
           [0.500, 0.500, 0.000],
           [0.576, 0.140, 0.284]]     <- rows sum to 1.000

Step 5: mix values. Output row 2 = 0.576 x [2,1] + 0.140 x [0,2] + 0.284 x [1,2] = [1.152 + 0 + 0.284, 0.576 + 0.280 + 0.568] = [1.436, 1.424].

output = [[2.000, 1.000],
          [1.000, 1.500],
          [1.436, 1.424]]

Read the result: token 0 ("the") can only see itself, so its output is just its own value vector. Token 1 ("cat") splits attention evenly between "the" and itself. Token 2 ("sat") leans mostly on "the" (0.576), a little on "cat" (0.140) and itself (0.284). The head has decided, through its learned projections, that "the" is the most informative context for "sat".

Why divide by sqrt(dk)? The saturation argument, precisely.

A dot product of two random vectors of dimension dk with unit variance has variance dk (variances add across the dk terms). So its typical magnitude grows like sqrt(dk). Feed unscaled scores into softmax and the largest score dominates completely: softmax outputs approach a one-hot vector, and the gradient of softmax with respect to its inputs approaches zero. Learning stalls. Dividing by sqrt(dk) brings the scores back to unit variance, keeping softmax in its sensitive middle range where gradients flow. With dk = 64 (a common head size), unscaled scores would be ~8x too large.

How to read this diagram

  1. Rows are queries, columns are keys. Cell (i, j) is how much token i attends to token j. Read row 2 ("sat"): 0.576 on "the", 0.140 on "cat", 0.284 on itself.
  2. Blue cells are kept; gray cells are masked. The mask is the upper triangle: every cell above the diagonal is negative infinity before softmax, which forces its weight to exactly 0.
  3. Every row sums to 1. Softmax turns each row into a distribution over the visible past only. Row 0 has no past, so it is forced to [1, 0, 0].

What breaks without it: during training the model could peek at future tokens, then fail at generation time when the future is genuinely absent. The mask is what makes training match generation.

Step 4: what the heads actually learn

Map the five steps onto a search engine:

Retrieval conceptAttention equivalentWhat it does
Search queryQuery vectorEncodes "what am I looking for" for this token at this layer.
Index labelsKey vectorsEncode "what I offer" for every token. Matching is a dot product, a learned similarity.
Stored documentsValue vectorsThe actual content retrieved, mixed by match strength.
Ranking + top-kSoftmaxTurns match scores into a probability distribution. But unlike top-k, it is soft: every token contributes something, and the whole thing is differentiable, so gradient descent can tune what gets retrieved.

"Differentiable" is the load-bearing word. A database lookup is discrete: you get document #7 or you do not, and you cannot take a gradient through that choice. Attention replaces the hard choice with a weighted blend, so the retrieval behavior itself can be learned end to end. The head learns what to ask for (WQ), how to label content (WK), and what content to store (WV) all at once.

Step 5: the quadratic bill

The score matrix is n x n for a sequence of n tokens. Time and memory both scale as O(n2 x d). Concrete: n = 4,096, one head, float32. The score matrix holds 4,0962 = 16.8 million numbers = 67 MB. With 32 heads, that is 2.1 GB per layer just for scores, before values, gradients, or optimizer state. Double the context to 8,192 and the bill quadruples. This single fact drives half of LLM systems research: FlashAttention (Volume 6) recomputes instead of storing, and the KV cache (Chapter 8) exists because re-paying this bill on every generated token would be ruinous.

Common misunderstanding: "attention weights explain the model's decision." Treat this claim with suspicion in design reviews. Attention weights show where information flowed, not why the output is what it is. The values being mixed, the later layers, and the residual stream (the shared vector that every sublayer reads from and adds its updates to, detailed in Chapter 6) all shape the result too. Researchers have shown you can often perturb attention weights substantially without changing predictions. Say: "weights are a clue about information routing, not a certificate of reasoning."
Field relevance: this is the single most whiteboarded mechanism in ML design reviews. The strong candidate writes the five steps from memory, hand-computes a 2x2 or 3x3 case without flinching, states the sqrt(dk) saturation argument, and quotes the O(n2) cost with a real number (67 MB per head at 4k context). The retrieval framing is the one-liner that shows understanding rather than memorization: "attention is differentiable retrieval."

Key takeaways

  • One head: project to Q/K/V, score with QKT/sqrt(dk), causal-mask, softmax, mix values.
  • The sqrt(dk) scaling keeps softmax out of saturation so gradients flow.
  • The causal mask is what makes training match generation: no peeking at the future.
  • Attention is differentiable retrieval: learned queries, keys, values, soft matching.
  • Cost is O(n2d) time and O(n2) memory. At 4k context, one head's score matrix is 67 MB.

4. Positional encodings: sinusoids, then RoPE derived deeply

Attention cannot see order. This chapter gives it eyes.

Chapter 3 ended with one unsolved gap: attention ignores word order, so 'dog bites man' and 'man bites dog' look identical to it. This chapter adds the position signal, from simple sinusoids to RoPE derived from scratch.

Design prompt

  • "Why does attention need positional information at all? What happens with no position encoding?"
  • "Write down the sinusoidal encoding formula and explain why the frequencies differ per dimension."
  • "Derive RoPE in 2D. Why does a rotation encode relative position?"

Step 1: why attention is position-blind

Look at the attention formula from Chapter 3: it is a set operation. If you permute the input tokens, the outputs permute identically. Attention has no notion of order at all: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce the same bag of contextualized vectors, just reordered. A positional encoding injects order information so the model can distinguish them.

Without any position signal, the model is a bag-of-words model with extra steps. It can learn which tokens co-occur but not sequences, grammar, or arithmetic. Every position scheme answers one question: how do we tell the model where each token sits, in a way that generalizes to sequences longer than those seen in training?

Step 2: sinusoids, the original answer

The 2017 Transformer paper added a fixed (not learned) vector to each token embedding. For position pos and dimension pair 2i, 2i+1 of a d-dimensional encoding:

PE(pos, 2i)   = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d))
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d))

Each dimension is a wave along the position axis, and the wavelength grows geometrically with dimension index: dimension 0 oscillates fast (wavelength 2 pi), the last dimension oscillates slowly (wavelength 2 pi x 10000). Why this shape? Two reasons. First, every position gets a unique fingerprint, because the combination of frequencies never repeats within any realistic context length. Second, and this is the clever part: shifting position by k corresponds to a linear transformation of the encoding (a rotation in each 2D subspace, by the angle addition formulas). So the model can learn to attend to "three tokens ago" as a fixed linear operation, independent of absolute position.

How to read this diagram

  1. Horizontal axis is token position (0 to 100). Vertical axis is the encoding value added to the embedding.
  2. Each curve is one dimension of the position vector. Dimension 0 completes many cycles; dimension 3 barely completes a fraction of one.
  3. The teaching point: the model gets position at multiple resolutions simultaneously. Fast waves distinguish neighbors; slow waves distinguish "early in the document" from "late". No dimension is wasted: together they form a unique fingerprint per position.

Limitation this picture hints at: the encoding is absolute (it says "position 47", not "3 after position 44"). Relative relationships must be learned by the model from these absolute fingerprints, which works but generalizes poorly to sequences longer than training.

Step 3: RoPE, derived deeply

RoPE (rotary position embedding) is the position scheme in LLaMA, PaLM, GPT-NeoX, and most modern LLMs. Instead of adding a position vector, it rotates the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to their position. The magic: after rotation, the attention score between two tokens depends only on their relative distance, which is exactly the property sinusoids approximated and RoPE delivers exactly.

The 2D derivation

Take one pair of dimensions of a query vector, q = (q1, q2), at position m. RoPE rotates it by angle m x theta:

R(m) q = | cos(m*theta)  -sin(m*theta) | | q1 |
         | sin(m*theta)   cos(m*theta) | | q2 |

Apply the same to a key k at position n, rotated by n x theta. Their dot product:

(R(m) q) . (R(n) k) = q . R(n - m) k

This is the whole trick, and it follows from rotation composition: rotating q by m and k by n, then dotting, equals dotting q against k rotated by (n - m). The absolute positions m and n cancel. Only the distance (n - m) survives. The attention score now natively encodes "how far apart are these tokens", with no absolute position anywhere.

Each dimension pair gets its own theta: thetai = 10000(-2i/d), the same geometric wavelength schedule as the sinusoids. Fast pairs resolve nearby positions precisely; slow pairs carry long-range distance. The full vector is just many independent 2D rotations glued together.

The RoPE symbols, named. R(m): rotation by angle m x theta, applied to the query or key at position m. m and n: absolute token positions. theta (per pair thetai): the base rotation speed, thetai = 10000(-2i/d), where i is the dimension-pair index and d is the head width. (n - m): the relative distance, the only position information that survives into the attention score. Step 4 below rotates q = (3, 4) and k = (1, 2) by hand to show the distance surviving.

How to read this diagram

  1. Horizontal axis is the dimension pair index (0 to 63 for a 128-wide head). Vertical axis is the rotation wavelength in tokens, on a log scale.
  2. The straight line is the geometric schedule. Each pair's theta is 10000(-2i/d), so wavelengths multiply by a constant factor per pair, the same shape as the sinusoidal encoding.
  3. Left pairs are fast, right pairs are slow. Fast pairs distinguish exact nearby offsets; slow pairs encode "far apart" without saturating. Together the 64 pairs give the model position at every scale at once.

Why this matters for extensions: NTK-aware scaling and YaRN (Appendix 4A) both work by reshaping this line, protecting the fast left end while stretching the slow right end.

How to read this diagram

  1. Top panel: the 2D case. Vector (x1, x2) is rotated by m x theta1, where m is the position. Rotation preserves the vector's length (norm), so it changes direction only. Nothing about the token's content is distorted.
  2. Bottom panel: the same rotation applied to every query/key position 1-6. Position 1 rotates a little, position 6 rotates a lot. Each position gets a distinct orientation.
  3. The payoff (not drawn, derived above): when a rotated query dots a rotated key, the two rotations partially cancel, leaving exactly the relative offset. That is why RoPE generalizes to longer sequences better than absolute encodings: "three apart" looks the same everywhere.

What breaks if misapplied: RoPE must be applied to Q and K before the dot product, and never to V (values carry content, not addresses; rotating them would scramble what gets retrieved). A common implementation bug is rotating with the wrong theta schedule, which silently destroys long-range position signal while short-range still works, a nightmare to debug from loss alone.

Common misunderstanding: "RoPE adds position information to the embeddings." It does not add anything. It rotates Q and K inside the attention computation. The token embeddings and the residual stream never contain an explicit position vector. This matters when people try to "read" positions out of hidden states, or when they apply RoPE twice (once at embedding, once in attention), which double-rotates and silently corrupts distances.
Field relevance: RoPE is the position question at modern labs. The strong candidate derives the 2D case on the board, states the relative-distance property as the reason it won, and names the theta schedule. Bonus points for the extrapolation caveat: RoPE trained on 4k context degrades beyond it, which is why long-context extensions (position interpolation, YaRN-style rescaling) exist. Those extensions belong to Volume 8; here, just know the boundary.

Key takeaways

  • Attention is permutation-invariant. Position encodings supply order.
  • Sinusoids: fixed multi-frequency waves; relative shift is a linear operation on the encoding.
  • RoPE: rotate Q/K by position-proportional angles; scores then depend only on relative distance (n - m).
  • Rotation preserves norms; apply to Q and K only, never V; theta follows the 10000^(-2i/d) schedule.

5. Activations and where normalization lives

GELU, SwiGLU, and why the norm moved before the sublayer.

Chapters 1 through 4 built attention over tokens. But attention is not the whole block. This chapter fills in the remaining machinery that lives around it: activation functions and normalization, and exactly where normalization sits in the stack.

Design prompt

  • "What is GELU, and why did transformers switch to it from ReLU?"
  • "Write SwiGLU and explain the gating. How many matrices does the MLP have?"
  • "Pre-norm vs post-norm: draw both, and explain why the field moved to pre-norm."

Step 1: why the MLP needs a nonlinearity

A transformer block (the repeating unit you will assemble in Chapter 6) has two sublayers. Attention mixes information across positions. The second sublayer is a small feed-forward network, the MLP, which transforms each position independently: expand the width (512 to 2048), apply a nonlinearity, project back (2048 to 512). The nonlinearity is the only thing stopping the whole block from collapsing into a single linear map (Volume 1: compositions of linear maps are linear). The choice of nonlinearity affects trainability and, slightly, quality. Two dominate modern LLMs.

Step 2: GELU and SwiGLU, precisely

GELU (Gaussian error linear unit), used in GPT-2 and BERT: GELU(x) = x x Phi(x), where Phi is the standard normal CDF (cumulative distribution function: the S-shaped curve mapping any input to a probability between 0 and 1). In code it is the approximation 0.5 x (1 + tanh(sqrt(2/pi) (x + 0.044715 x^3))).

Intuition: ReLU (rectified linear unit, the older default: x if x is positive, else 0) multiplies x by a hard gate (1 if x > 0 else 0). GELU multiplies x by the probability that x is positive under a Gaussian assumption, a smooth gate. Small negative values pass a little instead of being killed outright, which keeps gradients alive in a region where ReLU is exactly dead.

SwiGLU, used in LLaMA and PaLM: SwiGLU(x) = Swish(xW) elementwise-times (xV), where Swish(z) = z x sigmoid(z) (sigmoid squashes any input into the 0-to-1 range, so it works as a smooth gate). Read it as a gated linear unit: one projection (xV) carries the content, a second projection passed through Swish (xW) acts as a learned per-element gate deciding how much of each content element survives. The MLP now has three matrices (gate, up, down) instead of two, and the gate gives the network multiplicative interactions that a plain activation cannot express.

How to read this diagram

  1. Two projections up, one down. Both branches expand 512 to 2048; the down projection contracts back to 512. That is the three-matrix count from the parameter arithmetic.
  2. The gate branch decides what survives. Swish(xW) is a smooth per-element gate: where it is near zero, that content element is erased; where it is large, the content passes through amplified.
  3. The multiply is the key difference. A GELU MLP applies a fixed curve to one projection. SwiGLU multiplies two learned projections, which expresses multiplicative interactions between features that no fixed curve can.

Reading the numbers off this picture: 3 x 512 x 2048 = 3,145,728 parameters per block, about 3x the attention projections, which is why the MLP dominates the parameter count.

How to read this diagram

  1. Left panel: near zero, GELU and SiLU curve smoothly while ReLU has a sharp kink. The kink is a discontinuity in the derivative; the smooth curves keep gradients flowing for slightly-negative inputs.
  2. Far left and far right: all three agree asymptotically (identity for large positive x, near-zero for large negative x). The differences live in the transition region, which is where most activations actually sit during training.
  3. Right panel: SwiGLU is not a fixed curve applied elementwise. It is the product of two learned projections, so its effective shape adapts during training. The amber curve shows the characteristic gated shape for one setting of the projections.

Step 3: where normalization lives

LayerNorm (Volume 3) normalizes each token's vector to zero mean and unit variance, then applies learned scale and shift. RMSNorm is its cheaper sibling: it skips the mean-centering and divides by the root-mean-square only. Same stabilizing effect, slightly less compute, which is why LLaMA-style models use it. The question is where in the block the norm goes. This chapter settles the two ingredients Chapter 6 needs before it can assemble the block: the MLP's nonlinearity and the norm's placement.

How to read this diagram

  1. Left (post-norm): input flows through attention, gets added to the residual, then normalized. Every block's output is re-normalized, so the stream entering the next block always has controlled scale.
  2. Right (pre-norm): input is normalized first, then attention transforms it, then the raw result is added to the untouched residual. The residual stream itself is never normalized.
  3. The curved arrows are the residual paths. In pre-norm, gradients flowing backward along this path never pass through a normalization, so they propagate cleanly through all layers.
  4. Why the field moved: post-norm trains fine for shallow stacks but becomes unstable as depth grows, because each block's output scale compounds through repeated normalize-add cycles and gradients must fight through every norm. Pre-norm trains stably at 80+ layers with less learning-rate babysitting. The cost: the residual stream's magnitude grows with depth (each block adds to it unnormalized), which the final norm before the output layer handles.

What breaks if you mix them up: a post-norm checkpoint loaded into a pre-norm architecture (or vice versa) produces garbage, because the learned scales assume a specific placement. Architecture strings in model configs exist largely to prevent this.

Common misunderstanding: "normalization placement is a minor style choice." It changes the training dynamics of deep models qualitatively. Post-norm was a real obstacle to training very deep transformers and motivated a line of research (DeepNorm, Admin) before the field converged on pre-norm. In design reviews, "pre-norm trains deeper stacks stably because gradients bypass the norms on the residual path" is the expected sentence.
Field relevance: activation and norm questions test whether you know why the defaults are the defaults. The strong candidate writes the GELU and SwiGLU formulas, explains gating as multiplicative interaction, and draws both norm placements with the gradient-flow argument for pre-norm. RMSNorm's definition (no mean centering) is a sharp detail.

Key takeaways

  • GELU: smooth ReLU-like gate using the Gaussian CDF; keeps small negatives alive.
  • SwiGLU: Swish(xW) times (xV); a learned gate, three MLP matrices, the modern default.
  • RMSNorm: LayerNorm without mean centering; cheaper, equally stabilizing.
  • Pre-norm won because the residual path stays unnormalized and gradients flow through deep stacks.

6. Multi-head attention and the transformer block

Many heads, one block, every tensor shape accounted for.

Chapter 3 worked one attention head; Chapter 5 added the glue of activations and normalization. This chapter assembles the full transformer block: many heads running in parallel, the residual stream carrying everything forward, and the exact order it all runs in.

Design prompt

  • "Why multiple heads instead of one bigger head? What does each head learn?"
  • "Walk me through one transformer block and give me the tensor shape after every operation. Use batch 2, sequence 128, width 512, 8 heads."
  • "Where do the residual connections go, and what breaks without them?"

Step 1: many heads, many relevance patterns

Multi-head attention runs several attention heads (Chapter 3) in parallel on the same input, then concatenates their outputs and projects back to the model width. If the model width is 512 and there are 8 heads, each head works in its own 64-dimensional subspace (512 / 8 = 64). Each head has its own WQ, WK, WV, so each head can learn a different notion of relevance.

One head can only implement one retrieval pattern at a time, because a single softmax forces a single probability distribution over positions. Language needs many patterns simultaneously: in "the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", resolving "it" needs coreference tracking, but the same layer also needs subject-verb agreement, prepositional attachment, and local phrase structure. With 8 heads, head 3 can specialize in coreference while head 6 tracks syntax, and the concatenation carries all of it forward.

Empirically, trained heads do specialize this way: some attend mostly to the previous token, some to syntactic parents, some spread broadly. The expressive gain comes from the multiple independent distributions, not the parameter count.

Step 2: the full block, end to end

A transformer block is the repeating unit of the model: attention sublayer, feed-forward sublayer, normalization, and residual connections around each. Modern decoders (LLaMA-style) use the pre-norm layout: normalize, then sublayer, then add the residual. Reference model for the trace below: batch B=2, sequence S=128, width d=512, heads h=8, head dim dk=64, feed-forward width dff=2048, vocabulary V=32,000.

How to read this diagram

  1. Top to bottom is data flow. The token vectors enter at the top and exit transformed at the bottom, same shape in and out, which is why blocks stack.
  2. Blue RMSNorm boxes come before each sublayer (pre-norm). Normalization first keeps the inputs to attention and MLP well-scaled.
  3. The curved arrows are residual connections. Each sublayer's output is added to its input. The block learns a refinement (delta) to the stream, not a replacement. This is what lets gradients flow through dozens of layers (Volume 3: residual networks).
  4. Attention then MLP. Attention mixes information across positions (tokens talk to each other). The MLP transforms each position independently (per-token computation). The block alternates communication and computation.

  • Shapes: [B,S,512] in and out; scores [B,8,S,S] carry the quadratic cost; MLP holds ~3x attention's parameters.

7. MHA vs MQA vs GQA: the memory tradeoff

Same queries, fewer key-value heads: buying serving memory at a small quality cost.

Chapter 6's full block is expensive at serving time, and the memory bill grows with every attention head. This chapter asks what can be slimmed down: MHA versus MQA versus GQA, the memory tradeoff stated plainly.

Design prompt

  • "What is the difference between MHA, MQA, and GQA? Draw the head wiring for each."
  • "A 70B model with GQA-8 serves at 4k context. Compute its KV cache per token in fp16."
  • "Why does reducing KV heads speed up decoding, not just save memory?"

Step 1: the three wirings

Recall Chapter 6: with h query heads, standard MHA (multi-head attention) also uses h key heads and h value heads, one pair per query head. The variants share key-value heads across groups of query heads:

VariantQuery headsKV headsWiring
MHA (multi-head attention)hhEach query head gets its own K and V heads. The 2017 default.
MQA (multi-query attention)h1All query heads share a single K head and a single V head.
GQA (grouped-query attention)hg (between 1 and h)Query heads are split into g groups; each group shares one K/V head pair. MHA and MQA are its endpoints.

Nothing about the query side changes: every variant keeps all h query heads and their independent attention distributions. Only the keys and values are shared. The Q, K, V projections just get narrower on the K/V side.

Step 2: why: the KV cache bill

During generation, the K and V vectors of all previous tokens are cached (Chapter 8). The cache size per token is:

bytes per token = 2 (K and V) x layers x KV-heads x head-dim x bytes-per-number

KV heads sit directly in this formula. Cutting KV heads from 32 to 8 cuts the cache 8x, and just as importantly cuts the memory bandwidth per decoding step: every generated token must re-read the whole cache, and decoding is bandwidth-bound (Chapter 8). Fewer KV heads means less data moved per token, which means faster generation, not just a smaller cache.

  2. GQA panel: four query heads, KV heads shared across pairs (two groups of two). The lines show which queries read which shared KV head.
  3. MQA panel: four query heads, all lines converging on a single KV head. Minimum memory.
  4. The tradeoff, visually: the query side (blue) never shrinks. Sharing only touches the key-value side (teal), because experiments showed the model's quality lives mostly in having many distinct queries, while keys and values can be shared with little damage.

8. KV cache mechanics: the memory math of serving

What gets cached during generation, what it costs, and why serving lives or dies by it.

Chapters 6 and 7 describe what to compute. But generation would recompute it on every single token unless we store it. This chapter is the storage answer: the KV cache, and the memory math that decides whether serving lives or dies.

Design prompt

  • "What exactly is stored in the KV cache? Why K and V but not Q?"
  • "Compute the KV cache for a 7B MHA model at 4k context in fp16. Now with batch size 32."
  • "Why is decoding memory-bandwidth bound, and how does the KV cache relate?"

Step 1: the cache that makes generation affordable

Generation is autoregressive (the model produces one token, appends it, and runs again): The KV cache stores the key and value vectors of every previous token, at every layer, so each new step only computes Q, K, V for the single new token and reuses the cached K/V for everything before it. Without the cache, generating token number 1,000 would recompute attention over all 1,000 tokens from scratch, turning generation into an O(n2) process per token instead of O(n).

Why K and V but not Q? Look at the attention formula: token i's query only ever dots against keys, and the weights only ever mix values. Once token j is in the past, its query will never be used again (nothing in the future needs token j's question; the future asks its own questions). Its key and value, however, are needed by every future token. So K and V persist, Q is recomputed fresh each step and discarded.

Step 2: the two phases: prefill vs decode

PhaseWhat happensCost shape
PrefillThe user's prompt (say 2,000 tokens) is processed in one parallel forward pass. K and V for all prompt tokens are computed and stored in the cache.Compute-bound: big matrix multiplications over the whole prompt at once. This is the "time to first token".
DecodeOne new token at a time. Compute its Q/K/V, append K/V to the cache, attend over the full cache, sample the next token, repeat.Memory-bandwidth bound: each step re-reads the entire cache plus all weights to produce a single token. The arithmetic is tiny; the data movement dominates.

The bandwidth point deserves emphasis. In decode, generating one token for a 7B model reads ~14 GB of weights (fp16) plus the whole KV cache, to perform a few hundred GFLOPs of math. A GPU that can do 300 TFLOPs/s can only move ~2 TB/s of memory, so the step takes as long as the reading takes, not the math. This is why quantization (fewer bytes per weight) and GQA (fewer bytes per cache token) speed up generation: they shrink what must be read.

How to read this diagram

  1. Q_new (purple bar): the query of the single token being generated now. It is computed fresh and never cached.
  2. K_prev / V_prev (gray blocks): the cache. Keys and values of all previous tokens, sitting in GPU memory from earlier steps.
  3. K_new / V_new (orange and blue strips): the new token's key and value, computed this step. They are appended to the cache (the arrows labeled KV-cache[0:K] and KV-cache[K+1] show the cache growing by one slot).
  4. The data flow: scores = Q_new against [K_prev, K_new]; weights mix [V_prev, V_new]. Next step, today's new vectors are part of "prev".

What breaks without the cache: each decode step would recompute K/V for all previous tokens, making total generation cost quadratic in output length. A 4,000-token generation would cost roughly 2,000x more compute. The cache converts per-token work from O(n) recompute to O(1) amortized append plus an O(n) attention read.

Step 3: the memory math, worked

Cache bytes = 2 x L x Hkv x dh x b x s x B
where: the leading 2 counts K and V (one copy each); L is the layer count; Hkv is the number of key-value heads (all heads for MHA, 1 for MQA, a small group for GQA); dh is the head dimension; b is bytes per value (2 for fp16); s is the sequence length; B is the batch size.

The worked numbers, from the chapter:

KV cache bytes = 2 (K and V) x layers x KV-heads x head-dim x bytes-per-value x seq-len x batch

LLaMA-2 7B, fp16, seq 4096, batch 1:
  2 x 32 layers x 32 KV-heads x 128 x 2 bytes x 4096
  = 0.5 MiB per token x 4096 = 2.0 GiB

Same model, batch 32 (32 concurrent users):
  2.0 GiB x 32 = 64 GiB   <- the cache alone exceeds most single GPUs

LLaMA-2 70B (GQA-8), fp16, seq 4096, batch 1:
  2 x 80 x 8 x 128 x 2 x 4096 = 1.25 GiB
  (MHA would have been 10 GiB: the GQA chapter paying off visibly)

Two consequences practitioners live with. First, the cache caps batch size: every concurrent sequence holds its own cache, so long contexts directly reduce how many users one GPU serves. Second, the cache is why context length is a serving cost, not just a model capability: doubling max context doubles worst-case cache per sequence.

Common misunderstanding: "the KV cache is pure win, free speedup." It trades compute for memory, and memory is the binding constraint in serving. Engineers new to inference routinely size a deployment by parameter count ("70B needs ~140 GB in fp16") and forget the cache, then watch the server OOM at batch size 8 with 4k contexts. Always budget: weights + KV cache x batch x max length + activations headroom.
Field relevance: KV cache questions are the inference design review in miniature. The strong candidate writes the formula, explains why Q is not cached, distinguishes prefill (compute-bound) from decode (bandwidth-bound), and connects GQA to cache size without prompting. "Decoding is bandwidth-bound" is the sentence that every follow-up about quantization, batching, and throughput comes back to.

Key takeaways

  • KV cache stores past K and V per layer; each decode step appends one token's K/V and reuses the rest.
  • Q is never cached: past queries are never needed again.
  • Prefill is compute-bound (time to first token); decode is memory-bandwidth bound (tokens per second).
  • Cache bytes = 2 x layers x KV-heads x head-dim x bytes x seq-len x batch. It caps batch size and makes context length a serving cost.

W_Q = np.array([[1,0],[0,1],[1,0],[0,1]], dtype=float)  # projects dim 4 -> head dim 2
W_K = np.array([[1,1],[0,0],[1,0],[0,1]], dtype=float)
W_V = np.array([[1,0],[0,2],[1,1],[0,0]], dtype=float)

def attention(X, W_Q, W_K, W_V):
    # WHAT: one causal self-attention head, the five Chapter 3 steps.
    # WHY one line per step: each line maps 1:1 onto the math. A fused einsum
    # version would be faster but unreadable; this is a teaching implementation.
    # COMPLEXITY: O(S^2 * d) time, O(S^2) memory, from the score matrix.
    # WHAT BREAKS: per-step notes below; each names the failure mode.
    # 1. project: (3,4) @ (4,2) -> (3,2) for Q, K, V alike.
    Q, K, V = X @ W_Q, X @ W_K, X @ W_V
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]  # head dim, read from the matrix, never hardcoded
    # 2. score + scale. WHY divide by sqrt(d_k): raw dot products grow with
    # dim, which pushes softmax into saturation (Chapter 3). WHAT BREAKS: skip
    # the scale and weights collapse toward one-hot; the w[1] assertion fails
    # because [0.5, 0.5] becomes roughly [0.88, 0.12].
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)
    # 3. causal mask. WHY -inf and not 0: exp(-inf) is exactly 0, killing the
    # weight; masking with 0 would ADD e^0 = 1 of weight to future tokens.
    # WHAT BREAKS: mask with 0 and future information leaks; w[0] is no longer
    # [1, 0, 0] because token 0 starts attending to tokens 1 and 2.
    mask = np.triu(np.ones_like(scores), k=1).astype(bool)  # True above diagonal
    scores = np.where(mask, -np.inf, scores)
    # 4. softmax, computed stably. WHY subtract the row max first: exp()
    # overflows past ~88; the shift cancels out in the division, so the result
    # is mathematically identical but every intermediate stays finite.
    # WHAT BREAKS: without the shift, large scores give inf/inf = nan, and
    # every assertion below fails with nan.
    scores = scores - scores.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    weights = np.exp(scores)
    weights = weights / weights.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)  # rows now sum to 1
    # 5. mix. Each output row is a convex combination of the V rows, weighted
    # by relevance. WHY return the weights too: you need them to eyeball the
    # alignment against the chapter's hand-computed matrices.
    return weights @ V, weights

print("output:\n", np.round(out, 4))

import re
from collections import Counter  # Counter tallies pair frequencies in one pass

# WHAT: the Chapter 1 toy corpus. Words are pre-split into characters, each
# word carries a frequency, and </w> marks end-of-word so merges never cross
# word boundaries.
# WHY this shape: BPE counts pairs over the whole corpus, and the frequencies
# decide the winner. "newest" x6 must outweigh "lowest" x5 for ("e","s") to
# take merge #1 exactly as in Chapter 1.
# WHAT BREAKS: drop </w> and the first merges glue word endings to word
# beginnings, diverging from the expected sequence below.
corpus = {"l o w </w>": 5, "l o w e r </w>": 2,
          "n e w e s t </w>": 6, "w i d e s t </w>": 3}

def pair_counts(corpus):
    # WHAT: count every adjacent symbol pair, weighted by word frequency.
    # WHY Counter: one pass over the corpus, O(total symbols). A plain dict
    # with manual += would work; this is the idiom.
    # WHAT BREAKS: counting 1 per word instead of += freq reorders the merges,
    # because then "newest" and "lowest" weigh the same and ("l","o") wins.
    counts = Counter()
    for word, freq in corpus.items():
        symbols = word.split()
        for a, b in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):  # adjacent pairs via offset zip
            counts[(a, b)] += freq
    return counts

def merge_word(word, bigram):
    # WHAT: rewrite every occurrence of the pair "a b" as "ab", as whole
    # symbols only.
    # WHY the regex guards (?<!\S) and (?!\S): symbols are space-separated,
    # so the pair must match at symbol boundaries. Without them, merging
    # ("t","</w>") would match the t inside the already-merged symbol "est",
    # splitting it apart and corrupting the vocabulary.
    # WHAT BREAKS: a plain str.replace("t </w>", "t</w>") corrupts merged
    # symbols; the merge sequence diverges within two steps.
    return re.sub(r"(?<!\S)" + re.escape(" ".join(bigram)) + r"(?!\S)",
                  "".join(bigram), word)

merges = []
for step in range(10):
    # WHAT: one BPE training step: find the most frequent pair, record it,
    # apply it to the whole corpus.
    # WHY re-apply to the corpus each step: later pair counts depend on
    # earlier merges. ("es","t") only exists after "e"+"s" merged.
    # WHAT BREAKS: skipping the re-application freezes pair counts and the
    # merge sequence diverges after step 1.
    best, count = pair_counts(corpus).most_common(1)[0]
    merges.append((best, count))
    corpus = {merge_word(w, best): f for w, f in corpus.items()}
    print(f"merge #{step+1}: {best[0]} + {best[1]} -> {''.join(best)} (count {count})")

# WHAT: the expected merge table from Chapter 1.

expected = [(("e","s"),9),(("es","t"),9),(("est","</w>"),9),(("l","o"),7),
            (("lo","w"),7),(("n","e"),6),(("ne","w"),6),
            (("new","est</w>"),6),(("low","</w>"),5),(("w","i"),3)]
assert [(m, c) for m, c in merges] == expected, "merge sequence diverged"
print("All 10 merges match Chapter 1.")

# --- encode a new word with the learned merges, in order ---
def encode(word, merges):
    # WHAT: apply the learned merges greedily, lowest rank first, to a fresh
    # word. This is the OOV handling: unseen words decompose into learned pieces.
    # WHY in merge order: BPE encoding IS replaying the training merges in the
    # order learned; any other order is a different tokenizer.
    # WHAT BREAKS: reversing the order (try it) yields a different, worse
    # segmentation and different token IDs downstream.
    word = " ".join(list(word)) + " </w>"  # same char-split + end marker as training
    for (a, b), _ in merges:
        word = merge_word(word, (a, b))
    return word.split()

print("encode('lowest') ->", encode("lowest", merges))  # unseen word: the OOV test
print("encode('newer')  ->", encode("newer", merges))    # partial reuse: the realistic case

Appendix 4A: Long-Context Engineering

Volume 4 derives RoPE from scratch and notes the boundary: a model trained on 4k context degrades beyond it. Volume 8 covers the KV cache. Chapter 1. Stretching RoPE past its training window

1.1 Why extrapolation breaks

RoPE encodes position by rotating each pair of embedding dimensions at a fixed frequency. During training, the model only ever sees positions 0 through L, the training length. The rotation angles for those positions become familiar territory. Position L+5000 is not. The model has never seen those angles, and its attention patterns fall apart on them. Perplexity climbs, and long-range answers degrade first.

The goal of every extension method: make positions beyond L look familiar again, without retraining the model from scratch.

1.2 Position interpolation: the simple fix

If the model knows positions 0 to 4000 and you need 16,000, divide every position index by 4. Position 8000 becomes 2000, which the model has seen. This is position interpolation: map m to m/s, where s is the extension factor.

It works, with fine-tuning, up to about 8x. Its flaw: it compresses every RoPE frequency equally. The high-frequency channels carry fine-grained local position information, which nearby tokens need to tell each other apart. Interpolation blurs them. The model recovers some of this with fine-tuning, but the blurring caps how far you can go.

1.3 NTK-aware scaling: protect the high frequencies

A community researcher known as bloc97 posted a one-line improvement in June 2023. Instead of stretching positions, scale RoPE's base frequency. RoPE's per-dimension frequencies come from a base b, usually 10,000:

theta_d = b^(-2d/D)

NTK-aware scaling replaces b with b' = b * s^(D/(D-2)), where D is the head dimension and s is the extension factor. Low-frequency dimensions get interpolated, high-frequency dimensions stay nearly untouched. The local-resolution channels survive.

The worked numbers, for a LLaMA-style head with D = 128 and b = 10,000:

  • s = 2 gives b' = 20,221
  • s = 4 gives b' = 40,890
  • s = 8 gives b' = 82,685
  • s = 16 gives b' = 167,199

Two practical notes. First, this often works with no fine-tuning at all for 2x to 4x extensions, which is why inference engines adopted it quickly. Second, Code Llama shipped with NTK-aware scaling, manually setting the base to 1,000,000. If you have ever wondered why that config value looks odd, this is why.

1.4 YaRN: NTK-by-parts plus a temperature fix

YaRN, published in late 2023, starts from the observation that NTK-aware scaling is still one blunt rule for all dimensions. Its NTK-by-parts scheme treats each dimension by how many rotations it completes inside the training window. Dimensions that rotate many times (high frequency, short wavelength) are left alone. Dimensions that rotate less than once (low frequency) are fully interpolated. Dimensions in between get a smooth ramp. For the LLaMA family the ramp runs from alpha = 1 to beta = 32 rotations.

YaRN adds a second fix. Longer contexts spread attention over more tokens, which shifts the entropy of the attention distribution. YaRN compensates by dividing the pre-softmax logits by a temperature t, with 1/t = 0.1 * ln(s) + 1. For s = 16, that is 1/t = 1.277, implemented for free by scaling the rotated queries and keys by sqrt(1/t) = 1.13. No attention code changes, no runtime cost.

YaRN works training-free at inference time (Dynamic-YaRN) and reaches its best quality with a short fine-tune of a few hundred steps. It composes with FlashAttention, which is why it became the default extension recipe in serious long-context fine-tuning.

import math

def ntk_aware_theta(theta, scale, dim):
    # NTK-aware base: theta' = theta * scale^(dim / (dim - 2)).
    # Why this exponent: it spreads the interpolation pressure across
    # dimensions so the high-frequency channels (which carry local
    # position resolution) move the least. Change the exponent and the
    # pressure lands on the wrong dimensions: too small and low freqs
    # extrapolate, too large and high freqs get blurred like plain PI.
    return theta * (scale ** (dim / (dim - 2)))


def yarn_temperature(scale):
    # YaRN's entropy fix: 1/t = 0.1 * ln(s) + 1. Longer contexts spread
    # attention mass over more tokens; dividing logits by t re-sharpens
    # the distribution. Implemented by scaling q,k by sqrt(1/t): zero
    # extra compute at inference.
    return 0.1 * math.log(scale) + 1


def precompute_freqs_ntk(dim, end, theta=10000.0, scale=4.0):
    # RoPE frequencies with NTK-aware scaling, pure Python.
    # freqs[d] = 1 / theta'^(2d/dim): the rotation speed of dimension d.
    # cos/sin of (position * freq) are the rotation matrices from Vol 4.
    theta_p = ntk_aware_theta(theta, scale, dim)
    freqs = [1.0 / (theta_p ** (2 * d / dim)) for d in range(dim // 2)]
    # Outer product: positions 0..end-1 against each frequency.
    angles = [[t * f for f in freqs] for t in range(end)]
    cos = [[math.cos(a) for a in row] for row in angles]
    sin = [[math.sin(a) for a in row] for row in angles]
    return cos, sin


print("NTK-aware theta, base 10000, dim 128:")
for s in (2, 4, 8, 16):
    print(f"  scale {s:2d}x -> theta' = {ntk_aware_theta(10000, s, 128):9.1f}")

print("YaRN temperature:")
for s in (8, 16, 32):
    t_inv = yarn_temperature(s)
    print(f"  scale {s:2d}x -> 1/t = {t_inv:.4f}, q,k scale = {math.sqrt(t_inv):.4f}")

How to read this diagram
  1. ntk_aware_theta is the one-line formula. The exponent dim/(dim-2) is slightly above 1, so the base grows a little faster than the scale factor. For dim 128 and scale 4, theta moves from 10,000 to 40,890.
  2. yarn_temperature computes the entropy compensation. At 16x the logits are divided by 1.277, or equivalently q and k are scaled by 1.13 before the dot product.
  3. precompute_freqs_ntk builds the actual rotation tables: frequencies from the scaled base, then cos and sin of position times frequency. This is the same table Volume 4 derives, with theta replaced by theta prime.
  4. The printed tables are the numbers you would put in a model config. If a config shows theta 40,890 on a 4k-trained model serving 16k, it is doing exactly this.

Chapter 2. KV cache eviction policies

2.1 Why you cannot keep everything

Every generated token appends its key and value vectors to the KV cache, and every future token attends to all of them. The cache grows linearly with sequence length, and at long context it dominates GPU memory.

The worked numbers: a 32-layer model with d_model 4096 in fp16 stores 2 (K and V) * 32 * 4096 * 2 bytes = 0.5 MB per token. At 128k tokens that is 64 GB, more than most single GPUs hold, before counting the model weights at all. Something has to give: either buy more memory, or keep fewer tokens.

Eviction keeps a fixed budget of tokens and discards the rest. The research question is which tokens are safe to discard.

2.2 The attention sink discovery

The surprise finding: the first few tokens of any sequence attract huge attention weights no matter what they say. They act as sinks that stabilize the attention distribution. Drop them and the model breaks. Drop middle tokens and often nothing happens.

This single fact shapes every policy below. Whatever else you evict, protect the sinks.

2.3 Three policies

Sliding window. Keep the most recent W tokens, evict everything older. Simple, fast, and wrong for anything that needs long-range recall: the answer buried 50k tokens back is gone. Good for streaming chat where only recent context matters.

StreamingLLM. Keep the sink tokens (typically the first 4) plus a sliding window of recent tokens. The cache stays bounded at sink + window no matter how long the stream grows, so the model can generate coherently forever. The price: middle context is discarded entirely, so retrieval over the full history fails.

H2O (Heavy-Hitter Oracle). Attention scores follow a power law: a small set of tokens, the heavy hitters, receives most of the attention mass across generation steps. H2O keeps a running sum of each token's received attention and, at a fixed budget, retains the top heavy hitters plus recent tokens. With only 20% of the full cache budget, it matches full-cache accuracy across the paper's benchmarks, and it beat StreamingLLM on perplexity in long streaming runs. The cost is bookkeeping: you must track accumulated scores per token per head.

The honest comparison: sliding window is a positional rule, blind to content. StreamingLLM adds sinks to the positional rule. H2O is content-aware, which is why it keeps quality at small budgets, and heavier to implement, which is why it is not everywhere.

def kv_cache_bytes(n_layers, d_model, seq_len, bytes_per=2):
    # KV memory: 2 (K and V) * layers * d_model * seq_len * bytes.
    # fp16 -> 2 bytes; int8 KV quantization halves it (see Vol 8).
    # This is per sequence: multiply by batch size for the real bill.
    return 2 * n_layers * d_model * seq_len * bytes_per


def h2o_evict(scores, budget, n_recent=4, n_sink=4):
    # H2O-style eviction on one attention head, pure Python.
    # scores: accumulated attention received per token so far (the
    # "heavy-hitter" signal). budget: total tokens we may keep.
    # Keeps: the first n_sink tokens (attention sinks, always protected),
    # the last n_recent tokens (local context), and the top heavy hitters
    # by accumulated score for the remaining budget.
    # What breaks if changed: dropping the sinks destabilizes attention
    # (the sink finding); dropping recency kills local coherence; using
    # per-step scores instead of accumulated scores makes the ranking
    # noisy and eviction thrashy.
    n = len(scores)
    if n <= budget:
        return list(range(n))  # Under budget: evict nothing.

    keep = set(range(n_sink))                    # attention sinks
    keep |= set(range(n - n_recent, n))          # recent window
    # Heavy hitters: highest accumulated attention among the rest.
    candidates = [i for i in range(n) if i not in keep]
    candidates.sort(key=lambda i: scores[i], reverse=True)
    keep |= set(candidates[:budget - len(keep)])
    return sorted(keep)


# The memory bill from the chapter: 32 layers, d_model 4096, fp16, 128k.
print(f"full 128k cache: {kv_cache_bytes(32, 4096, 131072) / 1024**3:.0f} GB")
print(f"H2O at 20% budget: {kv_cache_bytes(32, 4096, 131072) / 1024**3 * 0.2:.0f} GB")

# Tiny eviction demo: 20 tokens, budget 8.
import random
random.seed(0)
accum = [random.paretovariate(2.0) for _ in range(20)]  # power-law-ish scores
print("kept positions:", h2o_evict(accum, budget=8))

How to read this diagram
  1. kv_cache_bytes is the memory arithmetic. The factor of 2 for K and V is the line people forget. The per-sequence note is the line that saves you in capacity planning, because batch size multiplies everything.
  2. h2o_evict takes accumulated attention scores, not per-step ones. Accumulation is what makes "heavy hitter" a stable property instead of a noisy one.
  3. Sinks are protected unconditionally (the first 4 indices), then the recent window, then the top scorers fill the remaining budget. The demo prints which of 20 positions survive an 8-token budget.
  4. The printed memory numbers show the payoff: 64 GB full cache versus about 13 GB at H2O's 20% budget, with matched accuracy on the paper's tasks.
Key takeaways
  • The KV cache grows linearly with length: 0.5 MB per token for a 32-layer 4096-wide fp16 model, 64 GB at 128k tokens.
  • Attention sinks (the first tokens) must always be kept. Every policy builds on this.
  • Sliding window is blind to content; StreamingLLM adds sinks to the window; H2O keeps heavy hitters by accumulated attention and holds quality at 20% budget.
  • Eviction is lossy. For tasks that need exact long-range recall, eviction is the wrong tool: buy memory or retrieve differently.
