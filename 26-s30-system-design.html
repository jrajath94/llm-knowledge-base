Why this category exists

An ML system design interview is the round where the coding stops and the engineering judgment starts. The interviewer names a product (a fraud detector, a feed recommender, an ads auction, an LLM serving stack) and asks you to design the whole machine that learns and serves. A strong model is necessary but nowhere near sufficient: what fails in production is almost never the math. It is train-serve skew (features computed one way in training and another way in serving), stale features, an inference bill that grows faster than revenue, a launch with no rollback plan, and evals that cannot tell you whether the new model is actually better.

Concept 1: the reusable system design template

What it is. A design template is a fixed, ordered list of decisions you walk through for any ML system: requirements, metrics, data and labels, modeling plan, serving architecture, monitoring and iteration, and explicit tradeoffs. The week 13 day 1 lab builds it on a fraud-detection case study; the week 13 day 4 lab reuses the same skeleton for recs, search, and ads.

Why it exists. Under time pressure, unstructured answers ramble and drop whole stages. Interviewers grade completeness and tradeoff reasoning, not the cleverness of the architecture sketch. A template guarantees you touch every stage and always land on tradeoffs, which is where the points are.

How it works, step by step. Take card-not-present fraud detection, the lab case study. (1) Requirements: who uses it, at what scale, under what latency and cost constraints. State the SLO, a service level objective: a numeric promise like p99 approval under 200 ms. (2) Metrics: one business metric (chargeback rate), model metrics (precision, recall), and guardrails (false-decline rate must not regress, because declining good customers costs more than most fraud). (3) Data and labeling: which events to log, where labels come from. The trap here is label delay: fraud is confirmed weeks after the transaction, so your training labels are always stale and your features must be point-in-time correct. (4) Modeling plan: ship a rules baseline first, then iterate toward gradient boosting on measured error, not on vibes. (5) Serving: online scoring for the approve path plus batch rescoring for the review queue. (6) Monitoring: watch the business metric and feature drift, with a concrete rollback plan. (7) Tradeoffs: name at least two you chose on purpose, for example real-time scoring cost against caught fraud, and precision against false declines.

Concrete numbers. A mid-size card processor sees on the order of 10,000 authorization requests per second at peak, each needing a decision inside about 200 ms at p99. A single percentage point of false declines on legitimate traffic can cost more revenue than the fraud it prevented, which is why the guardrail metric, not the model metric, often decides whether a model ships.

The misunderstanding. "System design" sounds like "pick the model". The model is one step out of seven, and it is rarely the step that decides the interview. Interviewers probe data (where do labels come from, what is delayed), serving (what happens at 10x traffic), and failure modes (what breaks first, how do you roll back). Candidates who lead with the model architecture run out of time before the graded parts.

Concept 2: feature stores and train-serve consistency

What it is. A feature store is a system that serves the same feature definitions to training and to serving. It has two faces: an offline store that answers "what did we know at time T" for training, and an online store that answers "what do we know right now" for serving with millisecond reads. The week 13 day 2 lab and the week 14 day 4 deep dive build both, plus the parity check between them.

Why it exists. Two silent killers live here. Train-serve skew is a mismatch between the features the model trained on and the features it sees in production: same name, different computation, slow silent degradation. Label leakage is worse: offline features that accidentally peek at the future (for example, aggregating events that happened after the label timestamp) inflate offline metrics and the model looks brilliant until it meets production.

How it works, step by step. Start from one timestamped event log: page views, add-to-carts, purchases. (1) For training, compute features that are point-in-time correct: for each historical training row, aggregate only events that happened before the label timestamp. That is what the offline store stores. (2) For serving, materialize the latest values of the same features into a low-latency online table. (3) Run the parity check: for the same entity at the same time, the offline and online values must match. The week 14 day 4 lab computes this directly and shows the skew when definitions drift. (4) Attach data quality and lineage metadata: who owns each feature, how fresh it is, what breaks if it goes stale.

Concrete numbers. In the week 13 day 2 lab, deliberately leaky offline features inflate the evaluation metric far above what the honest features achieve; the gap is the price of leakage, measured, not argued. In production systems, feature freshness tiers are explicit: real-time features refreshed in under a minute, hourly features, daily features, each with a freshness SLO and an alert when it is missed.

The misunderstanding. "A feature store is a cache." Speed is the easy part. The hard part is consistency: one definition of each feature, time travel for training, and a parity check that proves training and serving agree. Candidates who talk only about latency miss the actual interview question, which is always about skew and leakage.

How to read this diagram

  1. The event log (top left, amber) is the single source of truth: every user action with a timestamp. Both stores derive from it; nothing is hand-built twice.
  2. The offline store (blue) answers "what did we know at time T": <dfn>point-in-time correct</dfn> features. Training reads only from here, so no future information can leak into the labels.
  3. The online store (green) answers "what do we know now": the latest values, materialized for millisecond reads. Serving reads only from here.
  4. The dashed red box is the parity check: the same feature definitions must produce the same values for the same entity at the same time. When they differ, that is train-serve skew, and the week 14 day 4 lab measures it directly.

Interview relevance. Skew and leakage are classic follow-ups in recs and ads design rounds: "your offline AUC is 0.95 but online revenue is flat, walk me through the diagnosis." The answer starts at the parity check.

Concept 3: inference and training at scale

What it is. Scaling ML is two different problems wearing one name. Training scale is throughput: splitting a model and its data across many accelerators to finish in reasonable time. Inference scale is latency and cost: serving predictions to many users without the GPU bill eating the business. The week 14 day 5 lab simulates the training side; the week 14 day 3 lab simulates the inference side; the week 15 day 1 lab attacks serving cost with quantization and distillation.

Why it exists. A frontier model does not fit on one accelerator, and naive scaling wastes money twice: training that goes sublinear because devices spend their time talking instead of computing, and serving that idles GPUs on finished requests. The economics decide the architecture.

How it works, step by step. On the training side: data parallelism replicates the model and splits the batch (gradients are all-reduced); tensor parallelism splits individual matrix multiplies across devices with fast interconnect; pipeline parallelism splits layers into stages with microbatching to hide the bubble. ZeRO/FSDP sharding splits optimizer state, gradients, and parameters across the data-parallel group so no device holds the whole model. Activation checkpointing trades about 30 percent extra recompute for large activation-memory savings. On the inference side: continuous batching admits new requests at every decode step instead of waiting for the batch to drain; the paged KV cache allocates cache in blocks to kill fragmentation; quantization (the week 15 day 1 lab does fake int8) shrinks weights; distillation trains a smaller student to match a larger teacher. Volume 8 built the KV cache and batching primitives; Volume 6 built the parallelism primitives; here you learn when each one pays for itself.

Concrete numbers. Adam in mixed precision costs about 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the weights, 2 for the gradients, 12 for the optimizer state (master weights, momentum, variance in fp32). A 70B model therefore needs about 1.1 TB of state before activations, which is why sharding is mandatory, not optional. The week 14 day 5 timing model shows the 8-worker speedup landing well below 8x once communication overhead is priced in. On the serving side, the week 14 day 3 simulator compares no batching, fixed batching, and dynamic batching on the same workload and reports p95 latency and throughput: dynamic batching wins because the GPU never idles.

The misunderstanding. "More GPUs means proportionally faster." Communication is the tax: every all-reduce and every pipeline stage transfer eats into the speedup, and past a point adding devices slows you down. On the serving side the mirror mistake is "bigger batches are always better": they raise throughput until latency SLOs break, and the right batch size is the one that fits the p99 budget, not the biggest one that fits in memory.

How to read this diagram

  1. Top row (red/orange): naive static batching. Requests B and C finish early, but their GPU slots idle (dashed outlines) until the longest request A drains. Padding and waiting waste both memory and compute.
  2. Middle row (green/blue): continuous batching. The moment B and C finish, new requests D and E join the running batch at the next decode step (arrows). The accelerator stays saturated, which is the biggest utilization win in LLM serving.
  3. Bottom box (dashed amber): the training side of scale. Interview relevance. Interviewers expect the memory math on demand: "how much memory to train 70B" is a one-line calculation, and "design the serving stack" is graded on batching, KV-cache, and quantization choices with the latency budget stated up front. This is also the ground the distributed-training and inference rounds of Volume 28 stand on.

Key takeaways

  • The seven-step template (requirements, metrics, data, modeling, serving, monitoring, tradeoffs) is the default answer skeleton.
  • Feature stores exist for consistency, not speed: point-in-time correctness kills leakage, parity checks kill skew.
  • Training scale is a throughput problem solved with parallelism and sharding; inference scale is a latency and cost problem solved with batching, caching, quantization, and distillation.
  • State the memory math and the tradeoff at every step; that is what gets graded.

def simulate_risk(n_users: int, hours: int, seed: int = 0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    base = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.25, size=(n_users,))
    risk = np.zeros((n_users, hours), dtype=np.float64)
    risk[:, 0] = base
    for t in range(1, hours):
        drift = 0.01 * rng.standard_normal((n_users,))
        risk[:, t] = np.clip(risk[:, t-1] + drift, 0.0, 1.0)
        spike_mask = rng.random((n_users,)) < 0.01
        if np.any(spike_mask):
            add = rng.uniform(0.3, 0.7, size=(int(spike_mask.sum()),))
            risk[spike_mask, t] = np.clip(risk[spike_mask, t] + add, 0.0, 1.0)
    return risk

def find_spike_times_vectorized(risk, thresh):
    above = risk >= thresh

    # Already above threshold at hour 0
    users0 = np.where(above[:, 0])[0]
    spikes0 = [(u, 0) for u in users0]

    # False -> True transitions from hour 1 onward
    starts = above[:, 1:] & ~above[:, :-1]
    users, times = np.where(starts)

    spikes = spikes0 + list(zip(users, times + 1))
    return spikes

    W_q = np.clip(np.round(W / scale), -127, 127).astype(np.int8)
    return W_q, float(scale)


def dequantize_int8(W_q: np.ndarray, scale: float):
    return W_q.astype(np.float64) * float(scale)


def model_bytes_float64(W: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray) -> int:
    return int(W.size * 8 + b.size * 8)


def model_bytes_int8(W_q: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray) -> int:
    return int(W_q.size * 1 + b.size * 8)

Wq, scale = quantize_int8(Wt)
Wt_hat = dequantize_int8(Wq, scale)
logits_q = X @ Wt_hat + bt
acc_teacher = accuracy_from_logits(teacher_logits, y)
acc_q = accuracy_from_logits(logits_q, y)

print('acc_teacher', acc_teacher)
print('acc_q', acc_q)
print('bytes_fp64', model_bytes_float64(Wt, bt))
print('bytes_int8', model_bytes_int8(Wq, bt))
check('size_reduced', model_bytes_int8(Wq, bt) < model_bytes_float64(Wt, bt))
check('quant_acc_close', acc_teacher - acc_q < 0.02)

Instructor explanation

Quantization keeps the model architecture the same but stores weights in a cheaper format. In production, the appeal is usually lower memory footprint and better throughput with only a small quality drop.

Q1. A feed recommender must return about 1,000 candidates in under 50 ms before ranking. Which retrieval choice fits?

Answer: B. Two-tower embeddings with an ANN index give sub-50 ms retrieval over hundreds of millions of items; the heavy ranker only sees the 1,000 survivors.

  • A, wrong: Running the full ranking model over the whole catalog: the latency budget dies here. Ranking is expensive per item; that is why retrieval exists.
  • B, right: Two-tower retrieval plus ANN search is the standard first stage: cheap per-item scoring, millisecond lookup, and the ranker only touches the top slice.
  • C, wrong: Batch precomputation alone: fine for static catalogs, but it cannot react to fresh user behavior inside the 50 ms window.
  • D, wrong: A larger ranking model: improves quality per item but makes the latency problem worse, not better.

Q2. Your team trained on features from the offline store and serves from the online store. Offline metrics look great; production degrades over two weeks. Walk through the diagnosis.

Worked answer. First, run the parity check: recompute the offline features for recent entities at their label timestamps and compare against what the online store served. A mismatch is train-serve skew: same feature names, different computation. Second, audit for leakage: check that no offline feature aggregated events after the label timestamp. Third, check feature freshness SLOs: a stale online feature (for example, a daily aggregate that stopped updating) degrades the model silently. Fix the definitions or the pipeline, backfill, and add the parity check as a recurring monitor.

Trap. Blaming the model or retraining on more data. Retraining on skewed or leaky features reproduces the problem with fresh timestamps.

Q3. Your LLM serving cost is too high and quality must not regress. Which lever do you pull first?

Answer: C. Continuous batching plus a paged KV cache raises GPU utilization without touching model quality at all, so it is pure win before any quality tradeoff.

  • A, wrong: Jumping straight to aggressive quantization: it cuts cost but risks quality regression, and you have not measured whether utilization is the real problem.
  • B, wrong: Buying more GPUs: scales cost linearly and leaves the utilization problem unsolved.
  • C, right: Continuous batching and paged KV cache are quality-neutral: they fix idle-GPU waste first. Only then do you price quantization and distillation against measured quality.
  • D, wrong: Distilling to a smaller model first: a good lever, but it is a quality tradeoff that needs evals, so it comes after the free wins.

Q4. Should this risk model score in real time or in batch? The world drifts hourly and each real-time score costs 10x a batch score.

Worked answer. Decide from the value of freshness, not from habit. Measure how fast the risk signal decays: if the cost of acting on stale scores (missed fraud in the drift window) exceeds the 10x scoring cost, go real time; otherwise batch. The strong answer proposes the hybrid the labs teach: a cheap real-time heuristic for spike events plus a full batch rescore on a schedule, and names the freshness SLO each path must meet. State the tradeoff explicitly: dollars per decision against expected loss from staleness.

Trap. Picking one side without pricing freshness. "Real-time is better" without the cost math is the most common failure in this question.

Key takeaways

  • Lead every design answer with requirements, metrics, and the latency budget; the model comes later.
  • Skew, leakage, and freshness are the three production diagnoses to reach for first.
  • Fix quality-neutral serving waste (batching, KV cache) before quality-trading optimizations (quantization, distillation).
  • Close every answer with two explicit tradeoffs; that is the graded deliverable.
