Inference and Serving Engineering - Crash Pack

This is the compressed volume for Track 03: everything that decides whether a serving stack survives production, reduced to decision rules, memorized numbers, traps, and drills. It assumes the base material (the generate loop, continuous batching, the KV cache, quantization basics) is familiar. What it adds is the judgment layer: which choice to make, what number to check, and what breaks when you choose wrong.

Three questions organize the whole track, and this pack. How fast is each answer? How many answers per GPU per second? What does each good answer cost? Every rule below moves at least one of those three.

Worked-number policy. Every number here is an illustrative example built from public spec-sheet and listing values (September 2026), not a measured benchmark. The method is the point: redo the math with your own hardware prices and measured throughput before spending money. Reference hardware: H100 SXM at 989 TFLOPS BF16 dense (1979 TFLOPS FP8), 3.35 TB/s HBM; on-demand H100 around $3/hr; a 70B-class model with 80 layers and 8 KV heads.

1. Decision rules

1.1 Batching strategy selection

The rule is simple. Use continuous (in-flight) batching for all online serving. Admit a new request the moment any slot frees, at single-decode-step granularity. No request waits for another to finish.

Static batching (only for uniform offline work):

  step 1:  [ req A ............ ]   short request B waits
  step 1:  [ req B ............ ]   for the longest request
  step 2:  [ req C ..][ req D ..]   in the batch. Straggler tax.

Continuous batching (default for online traffic):

  step 1:  [A][B][C][D]
  step 2:  [A][_][C][D]  <- B finished, E admitted instantly
  step 3:  [A][E][C][D]

Pick by traffic shape:

  • Online chat, agents, mixed lengths: continuous batching. This is the default in every serious engine.
  • Long prompts mixed with short chat: add chunked prefill. Split each prompt into fixed-size chunks (a few hundred tokens) and interleave one chunk per decode step. TTFT rises a little; TPOT stays smooth because no single step gets stalled by a giant prefill.
  • Offline bulk jobs with uniform lengths: static batching is acceptable. The straggler tax is small when every request is the same length.
  • Chunk-size knob: bigger chunks lower TTFT but make decode steps lumpier. Smaller chunks smooth TPOT but stretch TTFT. Tune it against your TTFT and TPOT budgets, not by feel.

The trap to avoid: static batching on mixed online traffic. One 128k-output request holds the whole batch hostage.

1.2 Quantization method selection

Pick by hardware first, then by what is scarce (memory vs throughput vs capacity):

Hopper/Ada fleet (compute 8.9+), decode is the bottleneck FP8 W8A8 Native hardware support, near-zero accuracy cost on most models, halves bytes per decode step. Dynamic scales to try it, static scales (calibrated offline) to ship it.

Three rules that sit above the table:

  1. Never quantize without the eval harness first. Perplexity barely moves while math and code accuracy fall off a cliff. Evaluate on the tasks your users run.
  2. Re-calibrate after every fine-tune, merge, or distillation. Scales are not portable across checkpoints.
  3. Test both traffic shapes. Activation quantization bites hardest in prefill. A W8A8 setup that looks lossless on decode-heavy chat can regress on prefill-heavy long-document work.

1.3 Disaggregated prefill/decode: the decision rule

Prefill is compute-bound and wants FLOPS. Decode is memory-bound and wants bandwidth and capacity. Disaggregation puts them on separate GPU pools and ships the KV cache between them over RDMA. It wins only when the transfer tax is smaller than the interference it removes.

Disaggregate when at least two of these hold:

  1. TTFT SLO is tight (under ~1 s) and prefill/decode interference is the measured cause of violations, not queueing.
  2. Prompts are long (8k+): prefill dominates the request's GPU time, so isolating it moves the needle.
  3. Decode batches are small because prefill hogs KV memory: the shared pool forces an unhappy compromise.
  4. The interconnect between pools is fast: same-node NVLink, or RDMA with measured transfer well under the TTFT budget.

Do not disaggregate when prompts are short (transfer overhead dominates), when one pool would idle most of the day (two half-empty pools cost more than one full one), or when the team cannot operate the transfer layer reliably. The default is a shared pool with chunked prefill. Disaggregation is the optimization you graduate to with measurements in hand.

The quantitative gate (worked in code in section 2.7): the transfer must stay under a quarter of the TTFT budget, and the transfer must be cheaper than the interference it removes. A 2k-token prefill chunk stalls running decodes ~0.3 s on a shared GPU; if the transfer costs 6 ms, disaggregation wins big. If the transfer costs 200 ms and the interference was 50 ms, it loses.

How to read this diagram

  1. The router sends each prompt to the prefill pool only. That pool runs the full forward pass over the prompt and builds the KV cache.
  2. The middle arrow is the price of admission: the KV cache ships to the decode pool over RDMA (Mooncake/NIXL style). Its cost in milliseconds is the transfer math in section 2.7.
  3. The decode pool never recomputes the prompt. It streams tokens, freed from prefill stalls, which is where the ~30% lower time-between-tokens comes from in published setups.
  4. Teaching point: if the middle arrow is slow (cross-rack at long context), the whole diagram loses to a shared pool. Measure the arrow before you build the pools.

1.4 Spot vs reserved fleet mix

Cloud GPUs come in two prices. Reserved capacity costs the sticker price and stays yours. Spot capacity costs 50-70% less and can be taken back with minutes of notice. The decision is a mix, set by preemption tolerance:

  • Baseline traffic on reserved. The traffic floor that is always there belongs on capacity that never disappears. Size reserved for the 10th-percentile load, not the mean.
  • Peaks on spot. Traffic above baseline scales onto spot with an autoscaler. When spot vanishes, degrade gracefully: queue, shed low-priority traffic, or fall back to a smaller model through a cascade.
  • Prefill workers are more spot-friendly than decode workers. A prefill is short and cheap to redo. Decode workers hold KV state for in-flight requests, so a preemption kills those requests outright. Keep spot to a fraction where the retry cost stays small.
  • The math favors spot strongly. At 65% cheaper with ~5% of spot-served requests lost to retries, the effective discount is still ~63%. The breakeven is far in spot's favor for anything but the strictest latency SLOs.

What actually kills spot savings is not preemption. It is slow autoscaling that leaves spot GPUs idle while reserved GPUs queue.

1.5 Routing and cascade rules

A cascade serves every request with a small cheap model first, checks the answer with a quality gate, and escalates only the hard ones to the big model. A router is the same idea with more tiers or per-request model choice.

How to read this diagram

  1. Every request enters the small 8B model first ($0.22 per 1M tokens). There is no way to skip this cost.
  2. The quality gate inspects the answer: a confidence threshold (cheap, but confidence is not correctness), a judge model (accurate, costs one more inference call), or a rule like "route math to the big model" (zero cost, blind to difficulty within a type).
  3. About 80% pass and return immediately at ~150 ms TTFT. These users feel the small model's speed.
  4. About 20% escalate and pay the escalation tax: small + gate + big, ~600 ms TTFT. Escalated requests always pay twice.
  5. Teaching point: the gate is the load-bearing piece. A sloppy gate either escalates too much (kills the savings) or accepts bad answers (kills quality while the token-cost metric looks great).

Rules:

  1. Cascade when most traffic is easy. 80/20 splits routinely cut cost 2-3x. Below ~60% easy, the margin thins out.
  2. Budget the escalation rate like money. Track it on a dashboard. If it drifts from 20% to 50%, the cascade costs $0.67 per 1M instead of $0.44 and the p50 latency story breaks (past 50% escalation, even the median request hits the big model).
  3. Keep the big model warm. A cold big model turns the escalation path into a latency disaster.
  4. Measure quality on accepted answers, not just the escalation rate. A gate that silently accepts bad answers makes cost-per-token fall while cost-per-successful-outcome rises.

1.6 Engine selection

vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang, and TGI all do continuous batching now. Choose by traffic shape and fleet, not by brand:

Pick When
vLLM Broadest ecosystem and fastest iteration. Python, pip install, broad model coverage. The default starting point.
TensorRT-LLM NVIDIA-only fleet and maximum perf-per-dollar, and you accept a per-model/GPU engine build step (slower iteration, faster runtime). Earliest FP8/FP4 kernel support.
SGLang Agentic or multi-turn traffic with shared prefixes: RadixAttention shares prefixes across requests as a radix tree, and cache-aware scheduling routes to the worker already holding the prefix. Also the early mover on PD disaggregation.
TGI Standard Hugging Face models with minimal fuss. Production-hardened defaults, less research velocity.

Three scheduler details decide real performance in any engine: chunked prefill (protects TPOT), preemption policy (evict the newest sequence, recompute later; a rising preemption rate is the distress signal), and prefix-cache-aware routing (skips redundant prefills for shared system prompts).

1.7 Multi-tenant LoRA decision

When many fine-tuned variants share one base model, do not deploy one copy per tenant. Load the base once and serve adapters on top: a rank-16 adapter on a 7B model is ~16 MB against 14 GB for the base. Requests name their adapter and batch heterogeneously in one forward pass.

Rules: size --max-loras from the tenant traffic distribution (hot adapters stay on GPU, the rest spill to CPU RAM under LRU); warm adapters before production traffic (a cold adapter pays a CPU fetch as a latency spike); keep the runtime-load endpoint (VLLM_ALLOW_RUNTIME_LORA_UPDATING) trusted-admin-only, since it loads weight files from a request-supplied path.

2. Must-memorize numbers and formulas

2.1 Roofline arithmetic

The roofline says attainable performance is the minimum of two ceilings: what memory bandwidth allows and what compute allows. Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs / bytes moved. Below the ridge point, you are memory-bound. Above it, compute-bound.

  • Ridge point = peak FLOPS / peak bandwidth.
  • H100 BF16 ridge: 989 TFLOPS / 3.35 TB/s = 295 FLOP/byte.
  • H100 FP8 ridge: 1979 / 3.35 = 591 FLOP/byte.
  • Attainable = min(bandwidth x intensity, peak FLOPS).

# roofline.py - place a serving phase on the H100 roofline.
# WHAT: from FLOPs and bytes, compute intensity, the binding roof,
# and the attainable ceiling. This is the whole roofline in one function.
# WHY: it tells you whether to buy bandwidth (batch, quantize) or
# FLOPS (nothing helps decode; prefill is already compute-bound).
# WHAT BREAKS: passing bytes as element counts (forget the x2 for BF16).
# Intensity inflates and a memory-bound phase looks compute-bound.
# Units are the diagnosis: FLOPs per BYTE.

PEAK_BW = 3.35e12    # H100 HBM bandwidth, bytes/second
PEAK_BF16 = 989e12   # H100 dense BF16 tensor-core FLOPS

def diagnose(flops, nbytes, label):
    # Intensity: the x-axis position on the roofline chart.
    intensity = flops / nbytes
    # Ridge point: where the memory roof meets the compute roof.
    ridge = PEAK_BF16 / PEAK_BW
    # Attainable = the lower of the two ceilings.
    ceiling = min(PEAK_BW * intensity, PEAK_BF16)
    side = "memory-bound" if intensity < ridge else "compute-bound"
    print(f"{label:28s} I={intensity:7.1f} {side:12s} "
          f"ceil={ceiling/1e12:6.1f} TFLOPS ({ceiling/PEAK_BF16:.2%} of peak)")
    return ceiling

print(f"decode b=64 step: {64*140e9/d64*1000:.1f} ms -> {d64/140e9:.0f} tok/s")
print(f"prefill 2k time:  {2.87e14/p*1000:.0f} ms")

2.2 KV-cache bytes per token

The KV cache stores, per layer, the keys and values of every processed token. Its size caps concurrent sequences, which caps batch size, which caps throughput. This is the capacity equation of serving.

KV bytes per token = 2 x layers x KV-heads x head-dim x bytes-per-value.

For a 70B-class model (80 layers, 8 KV heads via grouped-query attention, head dim 128, BF16): 2 x 80 x 8 x 128 x 2 = 327,680 bytes = 320 KiB per token.

Context KV per sequence (BF16) What it means
1k tokens 320 MB trivial
8k tokens 2.56 GB noticeable
32k tokens 10.2 GB a real budget line
128k tokens 41 GB more than half an 80 GB GPU

Two consequences that drive real decisions:

  • Long context is a batch-size tax. At 32k context each sequence costs ~10 GB. A GPU with 10 GB of KV headroom fits exactly one such sequence. Throughput collapses, not because the GPU is slow, but because there is no room for a batch.
  • GQA is a serving feature. Cutting KV heads from 64 to 8 cut the cache 8x. A 70B model with GQA can serve cheaper than a 40B model with full multi-head attention at long context. When comparing models for serving, KV bytes per token matters as much as parameter count.

The most common capacity mistake: sizing for weights and forgetting the cache. A 70B model in BF16 needs 140 GB for weights (two 80 GB GPUs with tensor parallelism), but with 10 GB of KV headroom per GPU you serve roughly three concurrent 8k sequences per GPU (verified below). If traffic needs thirty, you need more GPUs or quantization. No kernel tuning changes that.

# kv_capacity.py - the capacity equation as a calculator.
# WHAT: per-token KV bytes from the architecture, then per-sequence
# cost and max concurrent sequences from a headroom budget.
# WHY: this is the arithmetic behind every OOM and every batch-size
# limit. Run it before choosing max-sequences, not after the page.
# WHAT BREAKS: forgetting the factor of 2 (keys AND values) halves
# the estimate and schedules an OOM at config time.

def kv_bytes_per_token(layers, kv_heads, head_dim, bytes_per=2):
    # 2 = one key vector and one value vector per layer per token.
    return 2 * layers * kv_heads * head_dim * bytes_per

BPT = kv_bytes_per_token(80, 8, 128, 2)  # 70B-class GQA, BF16
print(f"per-token KV bytes: {BPT:,} = {BPT/1024:.0f} KiB/token")

for ctx in (1024, 8192, 32768, 131072):

for ctx, headroom_gib in [(8192, 10), (32768, 10), (8192, 40)]:

2.3 TTFT and TPOT targets

Split latency into two parts, because they have different causes and different fixes:

  • TTFT (time to first token) = queue wait + prefill time. Dominated by prefill for long prompts, by queueing under load. This is responsiveness.
  • TPOT (time per output token) = average decode step time. This is the streaming reading speed.

Reference points (illustrative, from the track's worked setups):

  • Chat TTFT budgets live in the hundreds of milliseconds. A tight TTFT SLO is under ~1 s.
  • Cascade fast path: small model TTFT ~150 ms. Escalated path: ~150 ms (small) + ~50 ms (gate + routing) + ~400 ms (big) = ~600 ms.
  • TPOT stays flat as batch grows, until memory bandwidth saturates. A TPOT that climbs with batch is the signal you crossed saturation.
  • Report p99 and p999, not the mean. The mean hides the 1% of users having a terrible experience, and p99 can look fine while p999 is 10x worse. One stuck request in a batch can poison the whole batch's latency.

2.4 Dollars per million tokens

The unit-cost formula:

$/1M tokens = GPU price x GPUs / (tokens/sec x 3600 x utilization) x 1,000,000

Worked on 2 H100s at $3/hr, ~1,500 tok/s sustained:

Utilization $/1M tokens
100% $1.11
80% $1.39
60% $1.85
50% $2.22

Utilization is the silent multiplier. A 30-point utilization gain (50% to 80%) cuts unit cost 37% with zero kernel changes. That is why capacity planning sits beside batching and quantization as a first-class lever.

The batching ladder (same fleet): batch 1 at ~120 tok/s costs $13.89/1M; batch 8 at ~700 tok/s costs $2.38/1M; batch 64 at ~1,500 tok/s costs $1.11/1M. Same GPUs, 12.5x cost difference.

Then the two honesty multipliers. Cost per successful outcome = raw cost per request / (utilization x success rate). For a 2,000-token request: raw $0.0022; at 15% utilization $0.015; at 70% success rate $0.021 per good answer. The raw number said two-tenths of a cent. The honest number says two cents.

How to read this diagram

  1. The first bar is the raw GPU cost for one 2,000-token request on a fully loaded 70B fleet: about $0.002.
  2. The second bar adds the utilization layer. Idle GPUs still bill, so at 15% utilization the same request costs ~$0.015.
  3. The third bar adds the success layer. Retries and fallbacks mean each good answer carries 1/0.70 = 1.43 attempts: ~$0.021.
  4. Teaching point: the honest metric is the third bar, and utilization is usually the biggest lever, bigger than the GPU price.

# serving_cost.py - unit cost, honest cost, cascade cost, spot math.
# WHAT: four small functions that price a serving design end to end.
# WHY: every fleet argument is really an argument about one of these
# numbers. Writing them down ends the argument.
# WHAT BREAKS: dropping the utilization term (assumes 100% util,
# understates real cost 3-10x) or the success-rate term (a "cheaper"
# model that hallucinates more can cost more per good answer).

def cost_per_million_tokens(gpu_price_per_hr, num_gpus,
                            tokens_per_sec, utilization=1.0):
    # Fleet bill per hour divided by tokens actually produced that hour.
    fleet_cost_per_hr = gpu_price_per_hr * num_gpus
    tokens_per_hr = tokens_per_sec * 3600 * utilization
    return fleet_cost_per_hr / tokens_per_hr * 1_000_000

def cost_per_successful_outcome(cost_per_request, utilization, success_rate):
    # Idle time and failed attempts are real money. Divide them in.
    return cost_per_request / (utilization * success_rate)

def cascade_cost(small_per_1m, big_per_1m, escalation_rate, gate_per_1m=0.0):
    # Every request pays small + gate. Escalated ones pay big too.
    # Forgetting the small-model cost on escalated requests flatters
    # the math: the escalation tax is small + gate + big.
    return small_per_1m + gate_per_1m + escalation_rate * big_per_1m

2.5 Utilization and MFU

Two efficiency numbers, and the discipline to read them together:

  • Batch-1 decode uses ~0.34% of an H100's BF16 peak. That is not a bug in your code. It is the physics of reading 140 GB to produce one token. The fix is batching and quantization, not kernels.
  • MFU (model FLOPs utilization) = achieved FLOPs / peak FLOPS. The roofline version: fraction of the attainable ceiling. Kernel A (FP32 vector add) hit ~8% of its ceiling: a memory-access-pattern suspect. Kernel B (FP8 GEMM) hit ~60%: compute-bound and healthy, look at precision and instruction mix if you want more.
  • Utilization without intensity is vanity. SM utilization counts warps with work to do, not useful work done. A kernel doing wasted strided reads can show 90% SM utilization while moving a tenth of the bytes it should. Intensity tells you what the work is worth; utilization tells you the SM was awake. You need both.

2.6 Quantization accuracy deltas

What each method costs in quality, honestly:

  • FP8 W8A8 on Hopper: near-zero accuracy loss on most models. The default serious quantization, not an exotic option. vLLM reports up to 1.6x throughput.
  • The perplexity trap: perplexity barely moves while math and code accuracy fall off a cliff. A 4-bit model can show unchanged perplexity with a 6-point drop on math tasks. The cause is almost always calibration (wrong data, too few samples, per-tensor scales on outlier layers), not the bit-width.
  • int8 KV cache: nearly lossless in production engines. int4 KV: increasingly available with minor trade-offs. 2-bit (KIVI-style): the research frontier.
  • FP8 formats: E4M3 = 1 sign + 4 exponent + 3 mantissa bits, range +/-448, used for weights and activations (precision). E5M2 = 1 + 5 + 2, range +/-57,344, used where range matters. E4M3 has no infinities: overflow saturates, which silently biases sums. E5M2 has infinities: overflow becomes inf and poisons everything downstream.
  • Block-wise microscaling (the decision that matters more than the format): one scale per block of 128 values quarantines outliers. Per-tensor scaling with one loud outlier erases ~100% of normal values to zero; block-128 erases ~3%; block-32 ~0.8% (verified below). DeepSeek-V3 training used 128 and 128x128 blocks with FP32 scales, all E4M3, loss error under 0.25% vs BF16. Inference formats continue the pattern: MXFP8 (32 values, one E8M0 power-of-two scale, 8.25 bits/element), NVFP4 on Blackwell (4-bit, blocks of 16).

# fp8_blocks.py - why block-wise scaling beats per-tensor scaling.
# WHAT: quantize a tensor with one global scale vs one scale per block
# of 128 (then 32), and count values erased to zero.
# WHY: one outlier's oversized scale zeroes every normal value that
# shares it. Block-wise scaling confines the damage to the outlier's
# own block. The scale placement matters more than the format.
# WHAT BREAKS: block_size=1. Error drops to ~0, but you store a 4-byte
# scale per 1-byte value: 40 bits/element, worse than fp32. Block size
# trades accuracy for scale overhead; 128 and 32 are the compromises.

import numpy as np

E4M3_MAX = 448.0  # largest finite E4M3 value; codes saturate here

def quantize(x, block_size):
    # One scale per block: the block's max abs value maps to 448.
    n_blocks = int(np.ceil(len(x) / block_size))
    # Pad the tail so every block is full; padding is discarded after.
    padded = np.pad(x, (0, n_blocks * block_size - len(x)))
    blocks = padded.reshape(n_blocks, block_size)
    amax = np.abs(blocks).max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    # All-zero blocks get scale 1.0: avoids division by zero, maps 0->0.
    scale = np.where(amax > 0, amax / E4M3_MAX, 1.0)
    codes = np.clip(np.round(blocks / scale), -E4M3_MAX, E4M3_MAX)
    return codes.reshape(-1)[:len(x)], scale.reshape(-1)

def erased_fraction(x, x_hat):
    # A value that rounds to zero is not noisy, it is gone. This is
    # the visible damage of a too-large scale.
    big = np.abs(x) > 0.05
    return float((x_hat[big] == 0).mean())

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = (rng.standard_normal(4096) * 0.5).astype(np.float32)
x[1234] = 10000.0  # one loud outlier, like real activation spikes
for name, bs in [("per-tensor", len(x)), ("block-128", 128), ("block-32", 32)]:
    codes, scales = quantize(x, bs)
    nb = int(np.ceil(len(x) / bs))
    x_hat = (codes.reshape(nb, bs) * scales.reshape(nb, 1)).reshape(-1)[:len(x)]
    print(f"{name:10s}: {nb:4d} scales, erased {erased_fraction(x, x_hat):.1%}")

The KIVI asymmetry for KV-cache quantization (keys per-channel, values per-token), verified:

# kivi.py - keys and values want different quantization layouts.
# WHAT: quantize a synthetic key matrix two ways (per-channel vs
# per-token) and measure the damage on the 63 quiet channels.
# WHY: keys carry persistent per-channel outliers (attention sinks).
# Per-token scales stretch to cover the outlier channel, crushing
# every normal value in that token. Per-channel confines each
# outlier to its own scale.
# WHAT BREAKS: judging by max abs error. The outlier dominates that
# metric either way. The real damage is on the quiet channels, which
# carry most of the signal: per-token zeroes 41% of their values.

import numpy as np

def quantize_per_channel(x, bits=4):
    # One (min, max) pair per channel: reduce down the token axis.
    lo = x.min(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    hi = x.max(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    scale = np.maximum(hi - lo, 1e-12) / (2 ** bits - 1)
    return np.round((x - lo) / scale).astype(np.int32) * scale + lo

def quantize_per_token(x, bits=4):
    # One (min, max) pair per token: reduce across the channel axis.
    lo = x.min(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    hi = x.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    scale = np.maximum(hi - lo, 1e-12) / (2 ** bits - 1)
    return np.round((x - lo) / scale).astype(np.int32) * scale + lo

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
keys = rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=(256, 64)).astype(np.float32)
keys[:, 5] = rng.normal(0, 3.0, size=256)  # persistent outlier channel
qc = quantize_per_channel(keys)
qt = quantize_per_token(keys)
quiet = np.ones(64, dtype=bool)
quiet[5] = False
ec = np.abs(keys[:, quiet] - qc[:, quiet]).mean()
et = np.abs(keys[:, quiet] - qt[:, quiet]).mean()
big = np.abs(keys[:, quiet]) > 0.05
zc = float(((qc[:, quiet] == 0) & big).mean())
zt = float(((qt[:, quiet] == 0) & big).mean())
print(f"quiet-channel mean abs err, per-channel: {ec:.4f}")
print(f"quiet-channel mean abs err, per-token:   {et:.4f} ({et/ec:.1f}x worse)")
print(f"quiet values erased to 0, per-channel: {zc:.1%}")
print(f"quiet values erased to 0, per-token:   {zt:.1%}")

Two more KIVI details that make it work: the residual window (the most recent ~128 tokens stay in fp16 and quantize only as they age out; recent tokens dominate attention and keeping them exact is cheap) and streaming-friendly grouping (per-channel key scales need a full group before a scale exists, so the cache flushes in whole groups).

2.8 MoE serving numbers

DeepSeek-V3, the canonical example: 671B total parameters, 256 routed experts plus one shared expert, only ~37B active per token (8 routed + 1 shared). 94.5% of the feed-forward parameters sit idle for any given token.

  • Expert parallelism shards experts across GPUs; tokens travel to their experts over all-to-all dispatch. The network is part of the model now. In vLLM: --enable-expert-parallel.
  • Hot experts are the failure mode. Routing is never uniform. One published measurement showed a single rank receiving the bulk of routed tokens because two hot experts lived on it, while other ranks idled. The hottest rank sets the layer's latency.
  • Monitor per-expert token counts. Healthy is roughly uniform within +/-20%. One expert above 40% of tokens is the alert threshold.
  • Fixes: EPLB (expert parallel load balancer, --eplb-config in vLLM: redundant copies of hot experts on extra ranks), Waterfill in SGLang (+1.5% to +4.7% throughput on DeepSeek-V3/R1-style workloads), LPLB in SGLang (+0.8% to +7.3%).
  • Cost truth: active parameters per token are fixed by top-k routing (37B regardless of the 671B total). The cost of MoE is not compute. It is memory (all experts must live on GPUs), network (dispatch/combine), and the load-balance tax.

# moe_imbalance.py - how skewed is MoE routing?
# WHAT: divide the hottest expert's load by the average load.
# WHY: under expert parallelism the slowest rank sets the layer's
# latency. The average hides the straggler; the max IS the problem.
# WHAT BREAKS: averaging instead of taking the max. The whole point
# is the skew, and the mean erases it.

import random

def expert_imbalance(token_counts):
    # 1.0 = perfectly balanced. 3.5 = the hot expert does 3.5x the
    # average work, so the layer waits on it 3.5x longer.
    avg = sum(token_counts) / len(token_counts)
    return max(token_counts) / avg

rng = random.Random(7)

for _ in range(800):

    expert = 3 if rng.random() < 0.40 else rng.choice([0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7])
    counts[expert] += 1
print("tokens per expert:", counts)
print(f"skewed imbalance factor: {expert_imbalance(counts):.2f}")
print(f"uniform imbalance factor: {expert_imbalance([100]*8):.2f}")

3. Classic traps

Twelve ways serving stacks fail that look like mysteries until you have seen them once.

1. Coordinated omission in benchmarks. A load generator that waits for a slow response before sending the next request hides queueing. Real users do not wait politely, so the measured p99 looks fine while real p99 is terrible. Detect: compare generator-send timestamps against a fixed schedule. Fix: use a generator that sends on a fixed schedule regardless of response times.

2. The KV-cache OOM cliff. Max sequences x max context x bytes/token exceeds the KV budget, and the OOM was scheduled at config time, not caused by traffic. Detect: do the section 2.2 arithmetic before choosing max-sequences. Fix: lower admission, shorten max context, or quantize the KV cache. After any OOM, capture block-manager stats and queue state before restarting. Restarting destroys the evidence.

3. Prefix-cache poisoning. One extra whitespace in the system prompt template zeroes out prefix-cache hits overnight. TTFT jumps, nothing else changes. Detect: watch cache hits per thousand requests, segmented by template version. Fix: pin template versions; treat prompt templates as code.

4. Confusing throughput with goodput. Throughput counts tokens computed. Goodput counts tokens that reach users. Recompute after preemption burns FLOPs for zero new tokens, so throughput can look healthy while goodput collapses. Detect: track preemptions per minute next to tokens/sec/GPU. Fix: admission control, applied early. Better to queue requests than to admit and evict them.

5. Perplexity-only quantization eval. Perplexity barely moves while math and code accuracy fall off a cliff. Detect: the eval harness must include task metrics (reasoning, code, long-context retrieval) on traffic-like data. Fix: fix calibration (distribution match, more samples, per-channel scales on outlier layers), not the bit-width.

6. Stale calibration scales after fine-tuning. Fine-tunes shift weight distributions. Scales are not portable across checkpoints. Detect: re-run the eval harness after every fine-tune, merge, or distillation. Fix: re-calibrate. Every time.

7. Symmetric K/V quantization. Applying one scheme to both keys and values gets the worst of both worlds: wasted scales on values, crushed channels on keys. Keys per-channel, values per-token, always. Section 2.6 shows the 8.4x damage difference.

8. The static-batching straggler tax. One long request holds the whole batch hostage; short requests pay the longest request's time. Detect: p99 latency far above p50 on mixed traffic. Fix: continuous batching. This should never happen in 2026.

9. Slow autoscaling killing spot savings. Spot GPUs sit idle while reserved GPUs queue, and the team blames preemptions. Preemptions cost ~5% of spot-served requests as retries. Slow scale-up costs far more. Detect: compare spot-fleet utilization against reserved-fleet queue depth during ramps. Fix: tune the autoscaler first, argue about preemption tolerance second.

10. Shadow traffic without a comparator, or without a budget. Logs nobody reads are an expensive way to feel careful, and a 100% mirror doubles the GPU bill for the shadow period. Detect: ask who reads the comparator output and what the shadow week costs. Fix: sample 10% at first, write promotion gates before starting, shut the shadow down the day the decision is made.

11. The over-admission preemption storm. Someone raises max-concurrency "to use the GPUs better." Throughput per GPU falls 40%, preemptions jump 20x. Evicted sequences recompute, which consumes the FLOPs that would have served new tokens, which keeps the queue long, which keeps pressure high. The storm feeds itself. Detect: preemptions per minute spiking after a config change. Fix: revert the concurrency increase; set admission from the KV-budget math in section 2.2.

12. Disaggregating without measuring both sides. The team splits prefill and decode because "interference is bad" but never measures the transfer tax. A 32k-prompt transfer over IB costs ~215 ms; if the interference it removed was 50 ms, the split loses. Detect: run the transfer gate in section 2.7 with your link speeds. Fix: keep pools IB-local, or stay shared with chunked prefill.

Two incident rules that prevent most of the traps above from becoming outages. Roll back before root-causing: if a deploy correlates with the spike, reverting restores users in minutes; the post-mortem can take days. Change one thing at a time: two changes during an incident produce arguments, not knowledge. The third trap is treating the symptom with more GPUs when the cause is a config (admission, chunk size, cache key). GPUs are expensive; configs are free.

How to read this diagram

  1. Start at the top with traffic. Most spikes are demand, not defects. Compare RPS and tokens/sec against the same hour last week before touching anything.
  2. A deploy in the window means roll back first. Pride has no place in incident response.
  3. Cache hit rate is the quiet killer: a template change can zero prefix hits overnight with no other metric moving.
  4. Rising preemptions mean the scheduler is evicting sequences: cut admission from the KV-budget math.
  5. If all of those are clean, check GPU health: a thermally throttling card (hot, clocks down) poisons every batch it touches. DCGM catches it in seconds.

4. Rapid-fire Q&A

Q1. Decode at batch 1 on an H100-class GPU uses roughly what fraction of peak BF16 compute, and what is the binding constraint? A. ~50%, compute-bound by attention FLOPs B. ~0.3%, memory-bandwidth-bound by weight reads C. ~10%, latency-bound by kernel launch overhead D. ~90%, compute-bound by the large batch

Answer: B. Each decode step reads the full 140 GB weight matrix to produce one token: 140 GFLOP against 140 GB moved gives ~1 FLOP/byte, far below the ~295 FLOP/byte ridge point. Attainable performance is ~3.35 TFLOPS out of 989, about 0.34% of peak. This is why batching, not faster math, is the first throughput lever. A is wrong: attention FLOPs are small next to the weight reads. C is wrong: launch overhead is dwarfed by the 41.8 ms weight-read time. D is wrong: batch 1 is the opposite of a large batch; utilization is the problem.

Q2. A 70B-class model with GQA (8 KV heads) serves 32k-context requests. Roughly how much KV cache does each sequence need in BF16, and what does that imply for batch size on a GPU with 10 GB of KV headroom? A. ~320 MB; about 30 sequences fit B. ~2.5 GB; about 4 sequences fit C. ~10 GB; about 1 sequence fits D. ~40 GB; it does not fit at all

Answer: C. 32,768 tokens x 320 KiB/token = ~10 GB per sequence. With 10 GB of headroom, exactly one such sequence fits: batch size 1, and throughput collapses. This is the long-context batch-size tax, and it is why KV-cache quantization and disaggregated decode pools exist. A is the 1k-context figure. B is the 8k figure. D is the 128k figure.

Q3. The H100's BF16 ridge point is 295 FLOP/byte. A kernel has arithmetic intensity 55.6 FLOP/byte. Which roof binds, and what is the attainable performance? A. Compute roof; 989 TFLOPS B. Memory roof; 3.35 x 55.6 = ~186 TFLOPS C. Neither; the kernel is latency-bound D. Memory roof; 3.35 TFLOPS

Answer: B. Intensity 55.6 is below the ridge 295, so the memory roof binds: attainable = bandwidth x intensity = 3.35 TB/s x 55.6 = ~186 TFLOPS. This is exactly the batch-64 decode case. A would need intensity above 295. D forgets to multiply by intensity.

Q4. Batch 64 decode has ~55x the throughput of batch 1, but the step time barely changes (48 ms vs 42 ms). Why? A. The GPU runs 55x faster at larger batches B. Batching amortizes the weight reads: one 140 GB read serves 64 tokens instead of 1 C. KV cache reads shrink with batch size D. Prefill gets faster with batching

Answer: B. Every decode step reads the full weight matrix once regardless of batch size. At batch 64, that one read produces 64 tokens, so the bytes-per-token fall 64x while the step time stays roughly flat. Batching does not speed up any single request; it amortizes the weight reads across requests. C is backwards: KV reads grow with batch. D is irrelevant to decode step time.

Q5. Prefill of 2k tokens takes 0.29 s; one decode step takes 0.042 s. Per token, prefill is roughly 7x cheaper than decode. Why? A. Prefill uses fewer FLOPs per token B. Prefill reuses each weight read across all 2048 prompt tokens, so it sits on the compute roof instead of the memory slope C. Decode does more layers per token D. Prefill skips the KV cache

Answer: B. Prefill processes all prompt tokens in parallel: one pass over the weights serves 2048 tokens, pushing intensity to ~2033 FLOP/byte, past the ridge into compute-bound territory. Decode reads the same weights for a single token (intensity 1). Same weights, wildly different reuse. C is wrong: both run all layers. D is wrong: prefill writes the KV cache.

Q6. Two models have the same 70B parameter count. One uses full multi-head attention (64 KV heads), the other GQA (8 KV heads). At 32k context, which serves cheaper and roughly by how much on KV memory? A. Same cost; KV heads do not affect serving B. The GQA model; ~8x less KV cache per token (320 KiB vs 2.5 MiB) C. The MHA model; more heads means better batching D. The GQA model; ~2x less

Answer: B. KV bytes per token = 2 x layers x KV-heads x head-dim x bytes. Cutting heads 64 -> 8 cuts the cache 8x: 320 KiB/token vs ~2.5 MiB/token. At 32k context that is 10 GB vs 80 GB per sequence. KV bytes per token matters as much as parameter count when comparing models for serving.

Q7. Your fleet is Hopper-class GPUs and decode throughput is the bottleneck. Which quantization choice is the best default, and why? A. GPTQ 4-bit weights, for maximum memory saving B. FP8 W8A8, near-zero accuracy cost with native hardware support C. GGUF 2-bit, for the smallest checkpoint D. No quantization; Hopper is fast enough already

Answer: B. On Hopper, FP8 is natively supported: W8A8 halves memory traffic with near-zero accuracy loss on most models, and calibration is light (static) or unnecessary (dynamic per-token). It attacks exactly the decode bottleneck, which is bytes per step. A saves more memory but needs fused kernels for full speed and a careful calibration story; on Hopper, FP8 gets most of the win with less risk. C targets CPU/edge with real accuracy cost. D ignores the physics: decode is memory-bandwidth-bound on any GPU.

Q8. A 4-bit quantized model shows unchanged perplexity but a 6-point drop on math tasks. What is the most likely cause, and the fix? A. The GPU is too slow; use a faster GPU B. Perplexity-only evaluation hid the damage; investigate starting with calibration-data match and per-channel scales on outlier layers C. The batch size is wrong; increase it D. 4-bit is unusable; abandon quantization

Answer: B. Perplexity barely moves while reasoning degrades: this is the classic calibration failure. Start with the calibration set (does it match deployment distribution?), check sample count (reseed sensitivity means too few), then per-channel scales on outlier layers, then re-evaluate on task metrics. A is wrong: hardware speed does not change accuracy. C is wrong: batch size affects throughput, not task accuracy. D is wrong: 4-bit works well with correct calibration; the method is fine, the process failed.

Q9. In KV-cache quantization, keys use per-channel scales and values use per-token scales. Why this asymmetry? A. It is arbitrary; either layout works the same B. Keys carry persistent per-channel outliers (attention sinks), so per-token scales would stretch to cover the outlier and crush every normal value in that token; values vary more token-to-token, so per-token is the natural and cheaper grouping C. Values are larger than keys D. Per-channel is always better for everything

Answer: B. Key outliers sit in fixed channels across all tokens. One scale per channel confines each outlier to its own range. Values have no such structure; they differ most from token to token, and a new token's scale is final the moment it arrives, which suits a streaming cache. Verified in section 2.6: per-token keys give 8.4x worse quiet-channel error and erase 41% of meaningful values to zero.

Q10. FP8 has two flavors: E4M3 (range +/-448) and E5M2 (range +/-57,344). Which do weights and activations use, and what goes wrong on overflow in each? A. E5M2 for weights; overflow saturates safely in both B. E4M3 for weights and activations (precision); E4M3 overflow saturates to 448 and silently biases sums, while E5M2 overflow becomes inf and poisons downstream math C. E4M3 for gradients only D. They are interchangeable

Answer: B. E4M3's 3 mantissa bits give the best precision of any 8-bit float, which is what weights and activations need. But E4M3 has no infinities: overflow saturates, silently biasing sums. E5M2 has infinities: overflow becomes inf, which poisons everything downstream. The format choice matters less than block-wise scaling, but the overflow behaviors must be known.

Q11. For FP8 serving on Hopper, when do you use dynamic per-token scales vs static calibrated scales? A. Always dynamic; calibration is obsolete B. Always static; dynamic never works C. Dynamic to try FP8 quickly with no prep; static (calibrated offline) for production, since dynamic per-step scale computation costs speed D. It makes no measurable difference

Answer: C. Dynamic FP8 (--quantization fp8) needs no calibration and is the fast path to trying FP8. But the per-step min/max computation costs speed, so production uses static scales calibrated offline (llm-compressor), with no runtime overhead. Benchmarking dynamic FP8 against static and concluding "FP8 is slow" measures the wrong mode.

Q12. A tensor has one huge outlier. Per-tensor FP8 scaling erases 100% of normal values to zero; block-128 scaling erases 3.2%. What is the mechanism? A. Smaller blocks use more bits per value B. Each block gets its own scale from its own max, so the outlier's oversized scale is confined to its own block and the other 31 blocks keep tight scales C. Block scaling skips the outlier D. Per-tensor scaling is a bug

Answer: B. Scale = block_amax / 448. The outlier sets a huge scale only for its own block; the other blocks' scales come from their own small maxima, preserving precision. Per-tensor scaling lets one value's scale zero out the whole tensor. The cost of block-128 is 32 scales for 4096 values: negligible overhead for the accuracy.

Q13. Under heavy load, an engine's preemption rate climbs sharply. What does this signal, and what is the first knob to turn? A. The GPUs are too slow; buy faster GPUs B. KV cache pressure: sequences are being evicted and recomputed, wasting work. First check max concurrent sequences and per-sequence context; reduce admission or add KV-cache quantization C. The network is saturated; add bandwidth D. Prefill chunk size is too small; increase it

Answer: B. Preemption means the KV pool filled and the scheduler started evicting sequences, whose recompute burns FLOPs for zero new tokens. It is a capacity signal, not a speed signal. The fixes are admission control (fewer concurrent sequences), shorter contexts, or KV-cache quantization to fit more. A is wrong: faster GPUs do not add KV capacity. C is wrong: preemption is GPU-local memory pressure. D is wrong: larger prefill chunks make steps longer and pressure worse.

Q14. Agentic traffic shares a 4k-token system prompt across thousands of requests. Which engine feature attacks this best, and how? A. TensorRT-LLM's engine build, which compiles the prompt away B. Answer: B. RadixAttention stores the shared prefix once and shares it across requests; cache-aware routing sends each request to the worker already holding it. The 4k-token prefill happens once per worker, not once per request: a large TTFT and cost win. A is wrong: the engine build compiles the model, not per-request prompts. C is wrong: batching amortizes weight reads, not prompt compute. D is wrong: FP8 halves bytes but the prefill compute still happens per request.

Q15. What does chunked prefill protect, and what is the tuning knob? A. It protects TTFT; the knob is batch size B. It protects TPOT smoothness: a whole long prefill would stall running decodes, so the prompt is split into fixed-size chunks interleaved one per step; the chunk size is the knob (bigger chunks: lower TTFT, lumpier TPOT) C. It protects KV memory; the knob is context length D. It is only for benchmarks

Answer: B. Without chunking, one big prefill is a long compute chunk that stalls every running decode, spiking TPOT. Interleaving fixed-size chunks keeps each step short. The chunk size trades TTFT against TPOT smoothness directly, so tune it against both budgets.

Q16. Your fleet is NVIDIA-only and you want maximum perf-per-dollar, accepting slower iteration. Your traffic is agentic with long shared prefixes. Which engine, and why might you still start elsewhere? A. TGI, for HF-native simplicity B. TensorRT-LLM for max NVIDIA perf-per-dollar (earliest FP8/FP4 kernels, hand-tuned plugins), but start with vLLM or SGLang for iteration speed and prefix sharing, then move hot paths to TRT-LLM once the design is stable C. vLLM, always, no exceptions D. Whichever has the nicest docs

Answer: B. TensorRT-LLM wins on raw NVIDIA perf-per-dollar but charges an engine build step per model/GPU, which slows iteration. SGLang's RadixAttention is the best answer for shared-prefix agentic traffic. The pragmatic path: iterate fast on vLLM/SGLang, promote the stable hot path to TRT-LLM. Engine choice follows traffic shape and fleet, not brand loyalty.

Q17. A 70B-class model serves 32k-token prompts. The team considers disaggregated serving with prefill and decode in different racks connected by InfiniBand (~50 GB/s effective). What does the transfer math say? A. Transfer is ~10 GB / 50 GB/s = ~215 ms per request, likely blowing a tight TTFT budget. Keep the pools interconnect-local or stay shared B. Transfer is free because RDMA has zero overhead C. Transfer is ~10 MB, negligible at any scale D. Disaggregation always wins regardless of transfer cost

Answer: A. 32,768 tokens x 320 KiB = ~10.7 GB of KV cache; at 50 GB/s that is ~215 ms added to every request's TTFT, before prefill compute. Unless the TTFT budget tolerates it, this deployment loses to a shared pool. RDMA avoids CPU copies but the bytes still take 215 ms on the wire (B is wrong). C is off by 1000x. D ignores the measured tax.

Q18. After disaggregating, the prefill pool idles at 30% utilization while the decode pool queues. What is wrong, and what is the fix? A. The model is too small; use a bigger model B. The pool ratio does not match the traffic mix: too many prefill GPUs for the actual prompt/output ratio. Re-run the sizer with measured traffic and rebalance; put each pool on its own autoscaler C. The transfer link is too fast; slow it down D. Nothing; idle prefill GPUs are expected

Answer: B. The two pools must be sized from the traffic mix independently. An idle prefill pool with a queueing decode pool means the ratio assumed longer prompts (or fewer outputs) than reality. Measure the real prompt/output distribution, resize, and autoscale each pool separately since the two phases scale differently. 30% idle on expensive GPUs is exactly the waste disaggregation is supposed to avoid.

Q19. State the disaggregation decision rule: when do you split, and what two numbers must you compare? A. Always split; compare nothing B. Split when at least two of: tight TTFT SLO with interference as the measured cause, prompts 8k+, decode starved by prefill's KV usage, fast interconnect. Then verify: transfer under 25% of the TTFT budget AND transfer cheaper than the interference it removes C. Split when prompts are short D. Split when one pool would idle

Answer: B. The default is a shared pool with chunked prefill; disaggregation is the optimization you graduate to with measurements. Short prompts (C) make the transfer tax dominate. An idling pool (D) is a reason not to split. The two comparisons are the whole decision: budget fit and interference-vs-transfer.

Q20. A fleet's unit cost is $2.00 per 1M tokens at 50% utilization. Throughput per GPU is unchanged. What is the unit cost at 80% utilization, and where should you invest? A. $3.20; invest in faster GPUs B. $1.25; utilization work (autoscaling, traffic shaping, pool rightsizing) pays more than most kernel work C. $2.00; utilization does not affect unit cost D. $1.00; unit cost halves whenever utilization rises

Answer: B. Unit cost scales inversely with utilization: $2.00 x 0.50/0.80 = $1.25. A 30-point utilization gain cut cost 37% with zero kernel changes. This is why capacity planning sits beside batching and quantization as a first-class lever. A inverts the direction. D misstates the proportion.

Q21. Spot GPUs are 65% cheaper but can be preempted. Which workload split is sound, and why? A. Everything on spot; preemptions never matter B. Baseline load on reserved (sized for 10th-percentile load), peaks on spot, with graceful degradation (queue, shed, or cascade to a smaller model) when spot vanishes C. Everything on reserved; spot is never worth the complexity D. Decode on spot, prefill on reserved, because decode is stateless

Answer: B. The baseline is always there, so it belongs on capacity that never disappears. Peaks are variable, so they belong on cheap variable capacity. Graceful degradation bounds the damage when spot goes away, and the effective discount (~63%) survives realistic retry costs. D is inverted: decode workers hold in-flight KV state, so they are the less preemptible-friendly phase; prefill is short and cheap to redo.

Q22. A cascade serves 80% of traffic on a $0.22/1M small model and escalates 20% to a $1.11/1M big model, with a $0.05/1M judge gate. What is the average cost per 1M tokens, and what is the escalation tax? A. $0.40; escalated requests pay only the big model B. $0.49; every request pays small + gate, and escalated requests pay the big model on top (small + gate + big) C. $1.11; cascades never save money D. $0.22; the big model is free

Answer: B. 0.22 + 0.05 + 0.20 x 1.11 = $0.49/1M, still 2.3x cheaper than big-model-only. The escalation tax: escalated requests always pay twice (they ran the small model too). A ($0.40) is the shortcut that forgets the small-model cost on escalated requests. Track the escalation rate like a budget: at 50% escalation the cost math nearly collapses and even p50 latency hits the big model.

Q23. Raw GPU cost for a 2,000-token request is $0.0022. The fleet runs at 15% utilization and the task has a 70% success rate. What is the cost per successful outcome, and what does each layer mean? A. $0.0022; raw cost is all that matters B. $0.021; utilization divides by 0.15 (idle GPUs still bill) and success rate divides by 0.70 (each good answer carries 1.43 attempts) C. $0.015; success rate does not matter D. $0.21; utilization multiplies cost by 10

Answer: B. $0.0022 / (0.15 x 0.70) = $0.021. The raw number said two-tenths of a cent; the honest number says two cents, 10x higher. Utilization is usually the biggest lever (a scheduling problem, not a hardware problem), and the success layer is why a "cheaper" model that hallucinates more can cost more per good answer.

Q24. You shadow a cheaper quantized candidate for a week: 350k mirrored requests at $0.014/request vs a $0.020 baseline. Weekly savings after promotion would be $2,100. What is the breakeven, and what must be true before you promote? A. 2.3 weeks; promotion gates (quality within 0.5 pts, p99 within 110%, cost at/below baseline, error bump under 0.5 pts) written before the shadow started, all passing, then a gradual ramp with rollback triggers B. Immediate; promote on day one C. Never; shadow traffic is free so there is nothing to break even on D. 2.3 weeks; promote if the mean latency looks good

Answer: A. $4,900 shadow spend / $2,100 per week = ~2.3 weeks, after which the shadow week was free. The gates must be written before the shadow starts, or you will always find a story where the candidate wins. Compare tails (p99), not means: a candidate can match mean latency and still double your p99. C is wrong: at 100% mirroring the shadow fleet costs as much as production.

Q25. p99 latency doubles within an hour. RPS is flat, no deploy went out, cache hit rate is steady, preemptions are near zero. DCGM shows one GPU 65 C above its peers with clocks 30% below spec. Diagnosis and action? A. Scheduler bug; restart the engine B. Thermal throttling on a failing card: the hot GPU slows its steps and poisons batch latency. Drain the node, check cooling, RMA the card if the pattern repeats C. Traffic surge; add GPUs D. KV pressure; enable KV quantization

Answer: B. The tree rules out traffic, deploy, cache, and scheduler. A lone hot GPU with reduced clocks is thermal throttling, often a failing fan or blocked airflow. Because decode steps run in lockstep across the batch, one slow GPU slows every request it touches. A is wrong: nothing points at the scheduler. C is wrong: RPS is flat. D is wrong: preemptions near zero means KV pressure is not the cause.

Q26. After enabling a higher max-concurrency setting, throughput per GPU falls 40% and preemptions per minute jump 20x. What happened, and what is the correct response? A. The GPUs got slower; replace them B. A preemption storm: admission exceeds KV capacity, sequences get evicted and recomputed, and recompute burns the FLOPs that would have served tokens. Revert the concurrency increase; set admission from the KV-budget math C. The network is congested; add bandwidth D. This is expected; higher concurrency always costs throughput

Answer: B. This is the textbook preemption storm: more admitted sequences than KV blocks, evictions, recompute waste, falling goodput. The storm feeds itself: recompute consumes the FLOPs that would serve new tokens, keeping the queue long and pressure high. The fix is admission control grounded in the capacity math, not more hardware. D is wrong: higher concurrency helps until KV capacity binds; past that point it destroys throughput, and the cliff is the signal you crossed the line.

Q27. A 70B BF16 model OOMs on a single 80 GB GPU at load time. The team wants to tune the scheduler. What do you check first, second, third? A. Scheduler flags, then batch size, then the model B. Weights first (140 GB cannot fit on 80 GB; this is not a tuning problem), then the KV budget (max sequences x max context x 320 KiB/token must fit with headroom), then fragmentation vs leak (memory growth over 24h: a climbing sawtooth is a leak, flat-then-death is fragmentation or a burst) C. The network, then the disk, then the scheduler D. Add more GPUs immediately

Answer: B. OOMs are arithmetic, done in order. Weights: 70B x 2 bytes = 140 GB, which needs two 80 GB GPUs with tensor parallelism before anything else is discussed. Also verify quantization actually applied (a config typo can silently load FP16). Then the KV budget from section 2.2. Then the time dimension: growth over hours means leak (unreleased blocks, cached graphs), sudden death on a flat line means fragmentation or a burst. A tunes the wrong layer. D spends money on an arithmetic problem.

Q28. One 128k-output request halves a pool's throughput. Why, and what are the two defenses? A. It uses more FLOPs; buy faster GPUs B. Long outputs hold KV blocks for the whole generation, starving the batch of KV memory. Defenses: cap output length, or route long generations to a separate pool C. It is a network problem; add bandwidth D. Nothing can be done; this is normal

Answer: B. KV blocks are held for the entire generation, so one very long output occupies memory that could have served many short requests. It is a capacity problem, not a speed problem. Caps on output length protect the main pool; a separate pool for long generations isolates the damage. This is the same batch-size tax as section 2.2, wearing an output-length hat.

Q29. An MoE layer shows one expert receiving 40% of tokens while seven others idle. The imbalance factor is ~3.5. What does this cost, and what are the fixes? A. Nothing; experts are independent B. The hottest rank sets the layer's latency: the layer waits ~3.5x longer on the hot expert. Fixes: EPLB (redundant copies of hot experts on extra ranks), SGLang Waterfill (+1.5-4.7%), LPLB (+0.8-7.3%). Alert threshold: one expert above 40% of tokens; healthy is uniform within +/-20% C. Add more experts D. Reduce top-k to 1

Answer: B. Under expert parallelism, tokens dispatch over the network to expert shards; the overloaded rank is the straggler for the whole layer. The fix is not faster experts, it is spreading the hot expert's load. Monitor per-expert token counts in production (vLLM exposes them via Prometheus) and alert when the imbalance factor crosses ~2.

Q30. You must serve 100 fine-tuned variants of a 7B model. Compare one deployment per tenant vs multi-tenant LoRA, and name the operational risks of the LoRA path. A. Same cost either way B. Naive: 100 x 14 GB = 1,400 GB (eighteen 80 GB GPUs before KV cache). Multi-tenant LoRA: one 14 GB base + 100 x 16 MB adapters = ~15.6 GB, ~90x less, with heterogeneous batching sharing the base matmul. Risks: cold-adapter fetch latency (warm adapters first), adapter versioning, and the runtime-load endpoint, which must stay trusted-admin-only since it loads weight files from a request-supplied path C. LoRA is always worse D. Just buy 100 GPUs

Answer: B. The win is architectural: per-tenant cost drops from gigabytes to megabytes, and heterogeneous batching keeps utilization high. The remaining hard problems are operational, not mathematical: size --max-loras from the tenant traffic distribution (hot adapters on GPU, rest spilled to CPU under LRU), warm before production, and lock down dynamic loading.

Q31. Your benchmark reports p99 latency of 400 ms, but users complain. The load generator sends the next request only after the previous response arrives. What is wrong, and how do you fix the harness? A. Nothing; users are wrong B. Coordinated omission: the generator waits politely for slow responses, so it never queues and never measures queueing. Real users arrive on their own schedule. Fix: send on a fixed schedule (Poisson arrivals) regardless of response times, and warm up 50+ requests before measuring C. The model is too slow D. p99 is the wrong metric; use the mean

Answer: B. A closed-loop generator hides the very queueing you are trying to measure: when the system slows, the generator slows its sends to match, and the latency histogram looks rosy. An open-loop fixed-schedule generator keeps arriving during slowdowns, which is what real traffic does. Also warm up before measuring (cold kernels and caches poison early samples), split by traffic class, and watch p999 too.

Q32. Tokens/sec/GPU looks healthy, but users see retries and slow streams. Preemptions per minute are high. What metric confusion is happening? A. None; the stack is fine B. Throughput vs goodput: the engine computes many tokens (throughput) but preemption-recompute burns FLOPs for zero new tokens, so few reach users (goodput). Watch goodput = tokens reaching users / tokens computed, and treat rising preemptions as the scheduler's distress signal C. The GPUs are too slow D. Goodput is not a real metric

Answer: B. Throughput counts work done; goodput counts work delivered. Evicted sequences get recomputed, which looks like activity on the throughput graph while users wait. The pair to watch together is preemptions per minute and tokens/sec/GPU: when the first rises and the second falls, you are past the admission cliff. Fix with admission control, not hardware.

Q33. Overnight, TTFT p99 jumps 3x. Throughput, error rate, and preemptions are all flat. What is the most likely cause, and which metric confirms it? A. Traffic surge; check RPS B. Prefix-cache poisoning: a prompt-template change (even one whitespace) zeroed prefix-cache hits. Confirm with cache hits per thousand requests, segmented by template version C. GPU failure; check DCGM D. Quantization regression; check evals

Answer: B. TTFT is dominated by prefill, and prefill is what prefix caching skips. When hits drop to zero, every request pays full prefill again: TTFT jumps while nothing else moves. Throughput is flat because the GPUs are doing the same total work, just more prefill and less useful decode. The confirming metric is prefix-cache hit rate segmented by template version, and the fix is treating prompt templates as versioned code.

5. Cheat sheet: the 30 numbers and rules that matter most

  1. H100 SXM: 989 TFLOPS BF16 dense, 1979 TFLOPS FP8, 3.35 TB/s HBM.
  2. Ridge points: 295 FLOP/byte (BF16), 591 FLOP/byte (FP8). Below the ridge you are memory-bound; the fix is fewer bytes, not more FLOPS.
  3. Decode batch 1 (70B BF16): intensity 1, ~3.35 TFLOPS attainable = 0.34% of peak, 41.8 ms/step, ~24 tok/s.
  4. Decode batch 64: intensity 55.6, ~186 TFLOPS, 48 ms for 64 tokens = ~1,333 tok/s. Batching amortizes weight reads; it never speeds up one request.
  5. Prefill 2k tokens: compute-bound, 0.29 s, ~7x cheaper per token than decode.
  6. KV bytes/token = 2 x layers x KV-heads x head-dim x bytes. 70B GQA BF16: 320 KiB/token.
  7. Per-sequence KV: 1k -> 320 MB; 8k -> 2.56 GB; 32k -> 10.2 GB; 128k -> 41 GB.
  8. GQA 8 heads vs MHA 64 heads: 8x less KV cache. Architecture is a serving decision.
  9. TTFT = queue wait + prefill. TPOT = average decode step. Tight TTFT SLO: under ~1 s.
  10. Always report p99 and p999, never the mean alone.
  11. $/1M tokens = price x GPUs / (tok/s x 3600 x utilization) x 1e6.
  12. 70B on 2x H100 at $3/hr: $1.11/1M at 100% util, $1.85 at 60%, $2.22 at 50%.
  13. Batch ladder: b=1 -> $13.89; b=8 -> $2.38; b=64 -> $1.11 per 1M. Same GPUs.
  14. Cost per successful outcome = raw / (utilization x success rate). $0.0022 raw -> $0.021 honest.
  15. Utilization: 50% -> 80% cuts unit cost 37% ($2.00 -> $1.25). Capacity planning is a first-class lever.
  16. FP8: E4M3 (+/-448, weights/activations) vs E5M2 (+/-57,344, range). W8A8 needs compute 8.9+. 2x memory, up to 1.6x throughput.
  17. Static FP8 (calibrated) ships; dynamic FP8 (per-token scales) is for trying. Never judge FP8's speed on the dynamic mode.
  18. Block-128 FP8 scaling: one outlier erases 100% of values per-tensor, 3.2% block-128, 0.8% block-32.
  19. KIVI: keys per-channel, values per-token, ~128-token fp16 residual window. int8 KV ~lossless; int4 for long context.
  20. GPTQ/AWQ 4-bit: pre-Hopper max memory saving; pick by engine kernel support and benchmark both.
  21. Chunked prefill protects TPOT; chunk size trades TTFT vs smoothness.
  22. Rising preemptions = scheduler distress. Recompute burns FLOPs for zero tokens. Cut admission.
  23. Prefix-cache-aware routing (SGLang RadixAttention): shared prompts computed once per worker.
  24. Disaggregate when 2 of 4 hold: tight TTFT with measured interference, prompts 8k+, decode starved of KV, fast interconnect. Default: shared + chunked prefill.
  25. Transfer sizes (70B): 8k -> ~6 ms NVLink / ~54 ms IB; 32k -> ~24 ms NVLink / ~215 ms IB. Transfer must beat the interference it removes.
  26. Spot 50-70% cheaper (~63% effective). Baseline on reserved (10th percentile); peaks on spot. Prefill is more spot-friendly than decode.
  27. Cascade: every request pays small + gate; escalated pay big too. 80/20 -> $0.44-0.49/1M vs $1.11 (2.3-2.5x). Budget the escalation rate.
  28. Shadow: gates written before (quality +/-0.5, p99 <=110%, cost <=, errors +0.5pp); ramp 1/10/50/100; breakeven ~2.3 weeks in the worked example.
  29. MoE: 671B total / 37B active. Hot-expert alert: one expert >40% of tokens. Fixes: EPLB, Waterfill, LPLB.
  30. LoRA: rank-16 7B adapter ~16 MB. 100 tenants: 1,400 GB naive vs 15.6 GB shared (~90x).
