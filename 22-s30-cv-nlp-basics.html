CV and NLP Basics: Seeing and Reading

The language half of the basics: turn raw text into tokens and vectors with normalization, BPE, TF-IDF, and Naive Bayes, the foundation every transformer volume assumes.

2 labs14 tasksDifficulty: Hard, both labsWeek 8, days 4 and 5

Why this category exists

An honest note on the title: this category is called "CV and NLP Basics," but its vision half already happened. The convolutional networks, transfer learning, and augmentation of Volume 21 are the CV basics; the two labs here are the language half. They answer the question every NLP system faces before any model runs: how does raw text become numbers a model can read?

Without these skills, three things fail. First, you feed models text they cannot parse consistently: the same word split two ways in training and serving. Second, you reach for a transformer when a classical baseline would have been faster, cheaper, and nearly as good. Third, you cannot debug retrieval or classification because you never learned what the features mean. Both labs are Hard, and they sit at Week 8, days 4 and 5, right after the vision labs, which is exactly the right order: pixels first, then words.

Day 8.4 builds tokenization from normalization to subword merges. Day 8.5 builds the classical NLP toolkit: n-grams, TF-IDF, cosine retrieval, Naive Bayes, and a head-to-head of unigram vs bigram features with Naive Bayes and logistic regression. Volume 23 takes tokenization much deeper for transformers; what you learn here is the foundation it assumes.

How to read this diagram

  1. Raw text enters as documents: messy, variable length, full of case and punctuation.
  2. Normalization makes equivalent strings identical: lowercase, unicode forms, punctuation rules.
  3. Tokenization splits text into units: words, characters, or learned subwords.
  4. Vectorization turns token sequences into fixed-size numeric vectors: counts or TF-IDF.
  5. Those vectors feed two consumers: classifiers (Naive Bayes, logistic regression) and retrieval (cosine similarity).
  6. Every transformer in Volume 23 still starts with the first three boxes; only the vectorizer changes.

Core ideas, worked end to end

Concept 1: tokenization turns text into units a model can count

A token is the atomic unit a model reads: it can be a word, a character, or a subword piece. The vocabulary is the fixed set of tokens the model knows, each mapped to an integer id. Normalization is the cleanup before splitting: lowercasing, unicode normalization, and punctuation handling, so that "Hello!" and "hello" become the same input. BPE (byte-pair encoding) is the subword algorithm: start from characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, and encode new text greedily with the learned merges.

Word tokens exist because they match intuition, but they fail on rare words, typos, and morphology: every unseen word becomes an unknown token and its meaning is lost. Character tokens never hit unknowns but make sequences very long. BPE exists as the compromise the whole field converged on: frequent words become single tokens, rare words split into meaningful pieces, and there are no unknowns as long as the character set is covered. As built in the w8-d4 lab, you learn the merges from a corpus and then encode with them.

The mechanics on the diagram: corpus "low" twice and "lower" once, with an end-of-word marker. Count adjacent pairs: (l,o) appears 3 times, (o,w) 3 times, (w,end) 2 times. Merge 1 takes (l,o) into "lo". Merge 2 takes (lo,w) into "low". Merge 3 takes (low,end) into "low" as a complete word token. Encoding "lower" greedily with these merges gives ["low", "e", "r"]: the known stem stays whole and the unseen ending splits into characters.

Concrete numbers: a word vocabulary for English needs hundreds of thousands of entries to cover rare words and still misses typos. A BPE vocabulary of 30,000 to 50,000 tokens covers effectively everything, with the average English word splitting into about 1.3 tokens. The w8-d4 lab has you implement normalize_text, punctuation-aware word tokenization, character round-tripping, and the merge-learning plus encoding helpers.

The misunderstanding: "tokens are words." In every modern model, tokens are subword pieces, and that changes how you count: context limits, costs, and sequence lengths are all measured in tokens, not words. The second misunderstanding: "normalization does not matter." Skip it and "Apple" the company and "apple" the fruit become different tokens in one pipeline and the same in another.

How to read this diagram

  1. Start from characters; the pair (l,o) occurs 3 times, the most frequent, so it merges first.
  2. After merging, (lo,w) occurs 3 times and merges into "low".
  3. The third merge joins "low" with the end-of-word marker: the full word is now one token.
  4. Encoding "lower" greedily: "low" matches, then "e" and "r" fall back to characters.
  5. No token is unknown: any string decomposes into characters at worst.
  6. This is the exact algorithm the w8-d4 lab implements, scaled up to a real corpus.

Interview relevance. Google lists tokenization and text preprocessing as high-confidence topics for these labs. "Why subwords instead of words?" and "what breaks if train and serving tokenize differently?" are the two questions to be ready for.

Key takeaways

  • Normalize before you tokenize: case, unicode, and punctuation decisions change the vocabulary.
  • Word tokens hit unknowns; character tokens are long; BPE splits the difference and the field standardized on it.
  • BPE learns merges from pair frequencies and encodes greedily; any string decomposes to characters at worst.
  • Measure context, cost, and length in tokens, not words.

Concept 2: the classical toolkit, counts plus Bayes

An n-gram is a contiguous sequence of n tokens: unigrams are single tokens, bigrams are pairs. TF (term frequency) counts how often a term appears in a document. IDF (inverse document frequency) downweights terms that appear in many documents: idf(t) = log(N / df(t)), where N is the document count and df(t) the number of documents containing t. TF-IDF multiplies them: rare, document-specific words get weight, ubiquitous words like "the" get near zero. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors: the dot product divided by both lengths, 1 for identical direction, 0 for no overlap. Naive Bayes classifies with Bayes rule under a conditional independence assumption (features independent given the class), with Laplace smoothing adding a small count to every feature so no probability is ever zero, computed in log-space to avoid underflow.

TF-IDF exists because raw counts let stopwords dominate: "the" appears everywhere and means nothing. IDF is the correction that rewards discriminative words. Cosine similarity exists for retrieval: it compares direction, not length, so a long document and a short query can still match. Naive Bayes exists as the fastest honest text classifier: as built in Volume 19 for general probabilistic thinking, it returns here as a text workhorse that trains in one pass over the counts.

Retrieval mechanics on the diagram: three documents, N = 3. "the" appears in all three, so idf = log(3/3) = 0: it contributes nothing. "cat" appears in two, idf = log(3/2) = 0.18. "dog" and "ran" appear once each, idf = log(3) = 0.48. The query "cat" has cosine 0.50 with d1, 0.35 with d3 (which shares "cat" but is diluted by "ran"), and 0.00 with d2. Ranking by cosine returns d1 first.

Classification numbers: spam vs ham, priors 0.5 each. P("free"|spam) = 0.4, P("money"|spam) = 0.3; P("free"|ham) = 0.01, P("money"|ham) = 0.02. Document "free money". Log-space spam score: log 0.5 + log 0.4 + log 0.3 = -0.693 - 0.916 - 1.204 = -2.81. Ham score: -0.693 - 4.605 - 3.912 = -9.21. Spam wins by a wide margin, and the independence assumption, false but useful, is what makes the computation a sum of logs.

The misunderstanding: "TF-IDF understands meaning." It counts. "cat" and "feline" are completely unrelated to TF-IDF; that gap is exactly what embeddings (Volume 23) were invented to close. The second misunderstanding: "Naive Bayes is too naive to be useful." On text with Laplace smoothing it is a strong baseline that the w8-d5 lab compares against logistic regression on TF-IDF, and it often wins on small data.

How to read this diagram

  1. "the" appears in all 3 documents: idf = log(3/3) = 0, so it contributes nothing.
  2. "cat" appears in 2 documents: idf 0.18; "dog" and "ran" appear once: idf 0.48.
  3. TF-IDF vectors weight each term by tf times idf; direction is what matters.
  4. Query "cat" scores cosine 0.50 with d1, which shares "cat" with little extra mass.
  5. d3 also shares "cat" but "ran" adds orthogonal mass, diluting the cosine to 0.35.
  6. d2 has no overlap: cosine exactly 0. This is retrieval with no learning at all.

Interview relevance. "Derive TF-IDF and explain why IDF is logarithmic" and "when would you pick Naive Bayes over logistic regression for text" both come straight from the w8-d5 lab.

Key takeaways

  • IDF downweights ubiquitous terms; TF-IDF vectors reward words that are frequent in the document but rare in the corpus.
  • Cosine similarity compares vector direction, which makes it length-invariant for retrieval.
  • Naive Bayes: Bayes rule, conditional independence, Laplace smoothing, all computed in log-space.
  • Counts do not understand meaning; that limitation motivates embeddings in Volume 23.

_WS_RE = re.compile(r'\s+')

def normalize_text(s: str, *, lowercase: bool = True) -> str:
    s2 = unicodedata.normalize('NFKC', s)
    if lowercase:
        s2 = s2.lower()
    s2 = _WS_RE.sub(' ', s2).strip()
    return s2

check('ws_collapse', normalize_text('  A\nB\tC  ') == 'a b c')
for i, s in enumerate(corpus):
    print(i, repr(s))

    print(normalize_text(corpus[i]))

_WORD_RE = re.compile(r'[a-z]+')

def tokenize(text: str, ngram=(1, 1)) -> list[str]:
    text = text.lower()
    words = _WORD_RE.findall(text)
    n_min, n_max = ngram
    out = []
    for n in range(n_min, n_max + 1):
        if n == 1:
            out.extend(words)
        else:
            for i in range(len(words) - n + 1):
                out.append('_'.join(words[i:i+n]))
    return out

def build_vocab(texts: list[str], *, min_df: int = 1, ngram=(1,1)) -> dict[str, int]:
    df = Counter()
    for s in texts:
        df.update(set(tokenize(s, ngram=ngram)))
    kept = sorted([t for t, c in df.items() if c >= min_df])
    return {t: i for i, t in enumerate(kept)}

def vectorize_counts(texts: list[str], vocab: dict[str, int], *, ngram=(1,1)) -> np.ndarray:
    X = np.zeros((len(texts), len(vocab)), dtype=np.float64)
    for i, s in enumerate(texts):
        for tok in tokenize(s, ngram=ngram):
            j = vocab.get(tok)
            if j is not None:
                X[i, j] += 1.0
    return X

def fit_nb(X_counts: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray, alpha: float = 1.0):
    n, V = X_counts.shape
    log_prior = np.zeros((2,), dtype=np.float64)
    log_likelihood = np.zeros((2, V), dtype=np.float64)
    for c in (0, 1):
        mask = (y == c)
        Xc = X_counts[mask]
        log_prior[c] = math.log((mask.sum() + 1.0) / (n + 2.0))
        wc = Xc.sum(axis=0)
        log_likelihood[c] = np.log((wc + alpha) / (wc.sum() + alpha * V))
    return log_prior, log_likelihood

def predict_nb(X_counts: np.ndarray, log_prior: np.ndarray, log_likelihood: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    s0 = log_prior[0] + X_counts @ log_likelihood[0]
    s1 = log_prior[1] + X_counts @ log_likelihood[1]
    return (s1 > s0).astype(np.int64)

lp, ll = fit_nb(Xtr, y_train, alpha=1.0)
yhat_nb = predict_nb(Xva, lp, ll)
print('NB val_acc', accuracy(y_val, yhat_nb))

def sigmoid(z: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))

def fit_logreg(X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray, lr: float = 0.5, steps: int = 600, l2: float = 1e-3):
    n, d = X.shape
    w = np.zeros((d,), dtype=np.float64)
    b = 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w + b)
        w -= lr * ((X.T @ (p - y)) / n + l2 * w)
        b -= lr * float((p - y).mean())
    return w, b

def predict_logreg(X: np.ndarray, w: np.ndarray, b: float) -> np.ndarray:
    return (sigmoid(X @ w + b) >= 0.5).astype(np.int64)

w, b = fit_logreg(Xtr_tfidf, y_train)
yhat_lr = predict_logreg(Xva_tfidf, w, b)

print(confusion_2x2(y_val, yhat_lr))

Q1. Corpus: "low" x2, "lower" x1. What are the first two BPE merges?

Answer: merge 1: (l,o) into "lo"; merge 2: (lo,w) into "low".

  • (l,o) then (lo,w), right: (l,o) and (o,w) tie at count 3; the algorithm merges one (conventionally the first), after which (lo,w) also has count 3 and merges next.
  • (w,e) first, wrong: (w,e) occurs once; BPE always merges the most frequent pair, not a rare one.
  • Order of the tie, minor: what is graded is that merges follow pair frequencies, highest first, not which tied pair wins.

Q2. In a 1,000-document corpus, "the" appears in 1,000 docs and "quasar" in 4. Compute both idf values (natural log) and explain what the numbers mean.

Answer: idf("the") = log(1000/1000) = 0; idf("quasar") = log(1000/4) = log(250) = 5.52.

  • 0 and 5.52, right: a term in every document carries zero discriminative weight; a rare term gets a large weight.
  • Both positive, wrong: idf is exactly zero for ubiquitous terms, which is the whole point of the correction.
  • Forgetting the log, wrong: raw N/df (1000 vs 250) overweights rare terms; the log compresses the scale.

Q3. Your Naive Bayes spam filter assigns probability exactly 0 to a test email containing a word never seen in training. Name the bug and the one-line fix.

Answer: missing Laplace smoothing: an unseen word has zero count, so its zero probability zeroes the whole product. Fix: add a pseudocount alpha to every feature count (and alpha times vocabulary size to the denominator).

  • No smoothing, add pseudocounts, right: this is the standard failure mode the w8-d5 lab guards against.
  • Underflow, wrong: underflow gives wrong-but-nonzero tiny numbers in probability space; an exact zero is a missing count.
  • Bad prior, wrong: the prior cannot rescue a zero likelihood term; the product is zero regardless.
