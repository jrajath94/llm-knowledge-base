About this track

This track teaches you the work of the forward deployed engineer. A forward deployed engineer works with a customer. The engineer changes a vague business request into a working AI system. The engineer keeps the system running within real enterprise limits. The name comes from Palantir. At Palantir, engineers worked "forward" with clients.

Today, Anthropic, OpenAI, and many other companies hire people for this job. They use this title or similar titles. Examples are: forward deployed AI engineer, applied AI engineer, AI consultant, or solutions engineer for AI.

The base curriculum teaches you to build AI systems. This track teaches you to install them in another company. This is a different skill. The model is the same. The data is less organized. The security team says no.

The legal team asks where the data goes. The demo worked on your laptop. It does not work on their network.

This track connects the lab and the customer.

This track has three parts. Part 1 is the role brief. It tells you what the job is, what you do each day, and how people judge your work. Part 2 is a reading path through the base volumes. It tells you why each volume is important for this job. Part 3 has seven detailed chapters with new material.

These chapters are about: scoping, enterprise architecture, evaluations as contracts, the gap between prototype and production, unit economics, reliability and SLOs, and communication with stakeholders.

Key takeaways

  • Customer results measure a forward deployed engineer. Model quality does not measure a forward deployed engineer. Adoption, reliability in production, and time to value are the measures.
  • The model is not the most difficult part of the job. The most difficult parts are: scoping, evaluations that the customer trusts, enterprise limits, and the gap between prototype and production.
  • Every detailed chapter here ends with a template, a checklist, or working Python code. You can use these items in a real project.

Part 1: The target-role brief

What a forward deployed engineer does

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) works between the AI lab and the customer. The lab builds the model. The customer has a business problem. The FDE connects the two.

A typical project starts with a vague request. For example: "We want AI for our support team". Or: "Can this summarize our contracts". Or: "We need to reduce review time by half". The FDE's job is to make a plan from that request.

The plan includes: what to build, what data it needs, what a good result looks like, what it will cost, and what can go wrong. Then the FDE builds it. The FDE usually builds a prototype first. The prototype shows the idea with the customer's own data.

Then the FDE hardens the system for production. Then the FDE hands over a system that the customer's team can run.

Three things make this job different from a pure research job or a product job:

  1. The problem is not clear. No one gives you a specification. You find the specification by asking questions, watching users, and making prototypes that do not work. Scoping is half of the job.
  2. The customer's limits apply, not yours. Your stack, your cloud, your data policies do not apply. You build within their security model. You build within their data residency rules.
  3. These rules state where their data can legally stay. You build within their old systems. You build within their procurement process.
  4. The customer decides what "works" means. Your evaluation suite must convince a skeptical VP, not a reviewer. An evaluation suite is a set of tests. These tests measure if the system does its job. If the customer does not trust the measurement, nothing ships.

The title is different. "Forward deployed AI engineer" (Anthropic), "forward deployed engineer" (Palantir, OpenAI), "applied AI engineer", "AI consultant", "customer engineer" are examples. The work is the same. You embed, scope, prototype, harden, deploy, measure, and support.

The day-to-day loop

How to read this diagram

  1. Start at the top: Discover. Sit with users and find the real pain.
  2. Move right: Scope. Write what you will build and what you will not build.
  3. Next: Prototype. Build a working demo in days, not months.
  4. Next: Pilot. One team uses it for real work.
  5. Next: Production. Harden it, monitor it, support it.
  6. Last: Expand. Roll it out wider, then discover the next pain. The loop repeats.

The parts

  • Discover. What it is: time with users. Why it exists: the real pain is never in the ticket. If skipped: you build the wrong thing fast.
  • Scope. What it is: the written boundary. Why it exists: it stops scope creep. If skipped: the project grows until it dies.
  • Prototype. What it is: a working demo, fast. Why it exists: it makes the abstract concrete. If skipped: you commit to a design nobody has seen.
  • Pilot. What it is: real use by one team. Why it exists: reality tests the design. If skipped: production is the first real test.
  • Production. What it is: the hardened system. Why it exists: it must survive every user. If skipped: the pilot becomes permanent and fragile.
  • Expand. What it is: wider rollout. Why it exists: one win funds the next. If skipped: the win stays small.

No two weeks are the same. But engagements follow a rhythm. Learn the rhythm. Then you can run any engagement.

How to read this diagram

  1. Start at the left box. Discover means you watch real users do the job. Do not read a requirements document. The best requirements are in people's heads.
  2. Scope turns discovery into a written plan with numbers. The plan shows what success looks like. It also shows the kill criteria. The kill criteria are the conditions when you stop. Kill criteria are the most important line in the plan.
  3. Prototype is intentionally ugly and fast. It runs on the customer's real data. It does not run on sample data. If the prototype fails here, you kill it cheaply. This is a good outcome.
  4. Harden is when the demo becomes a system. This includes security review, evaluations, edge cases, monitoring, and fallbacks. Most of the calendar time is for this step.
  5. Deploy starts with a small pilot. A pilot is a trial with a few real users. It is not a launch. Use real users and real traffic. Monitor closely.
  6. Measure checks the success criteria from the scope step. If you meet them, you expand to more users. Or you hand over to the customer's team. You give them runbooks and training. Runbooks are written playbooks for operating the system and handling incidents.

A week can look like this. On Monday, you have a discovery workshop with the customer's support leads. On Tuesday, you iterate the prototype on their ticket data. On Wednesday, you have a security review with their infosec team. Infosec is the customer's information-security team. On Thursday, you build the acceptance evaluation suite.

On Friday, you give a status update to the executive sponsor. You also do one hour of on-call for the pilot. The job is a mix of engineering, meetings, and writing. Chapter 7 is the writing half.

How success is measured

How to read this diagram

  1. Adoption: weekly active users. Are they coming back.
  2. Task success: tasks completed without human rescue.
  3. Expansion: new teams asking for the same system.
  4. Reference: the customer will vouch for you in public.
  5. Track all four from the pilot. The reference customer is the prize: it sells the next deal.

The parts

  • Adoption. What it is: weekly active users. Why it matters: use is the truth. If flat: the system is shelfware.
  • Task success. What it is: unaided completions. Why it matters: it measures real value. If low: humans carry the system.
  • Expansion. What it is: new teams asking in. Why it matters: it proves the pattern repeats. If absent: the win is a one-off.
  • Reference. What it is: a customer who vouches publicly. Why it matters: it sells the next deal. If missing: every sale starts from zero.

You do not measure FDEs by papers, model benchmarks, or lines of code. You measure them by what happens at the customer. Know the scorecard before you start.

Customer outcomes. Did the system do what the customer wanted? Handle time per ticket. Contract review cycle time. Defects caught before production.

Pick one to three outcome metrics in the scoping phase. Report them every week. Everything else is supporting detail.

Adoption. A system that nobody uses is a failed engagement. This is true even if it is technically excellent. Track weekly active users. Track the share of eligible work that flows through the system. Track retention after the novelty goes away.

Adoption that fades in month two shows that the system does not fit the workflow.

Reliability in production. The customer experiences your error rate. They do not experience your benchmark score. A benchmark is a standard test scored in the lab. It is not scored on real users. Track uptime.

Track p95 latency. This is the latency that 95 percent of requests beat. It is the tail, not the average. Track task success rate on real traffic. Track mean time to recover from incidents. Chapter 6 of this track turns these into SLOs.

SLOs are service level objectives. They are written targets for how reliable the system must be. They have error budgets.

Time to value. How fast did the customer see the first real result? Engagements stop because of long silent builds. A working prototype on real data in the first two to three weeks builds trust. You will need that trust for the hard months of hardening.

Expansion and handover. The best engagements end when the customer buys more. Or they end when the customer runs the system themselves. Expansion means more teams and more use cases. Clean handover means runbooks, trained operators, and documented evaluations. These are the two good endings.

The demo is not the product. Customers remember its best moment. They expect this moment every time. Promise less than the demo shows. Write down what the demo did not cover. Chapter 4 explains why demos fail on real data.

Chapter 7 gives you the expectation curve to manage this.

Part 3: In-depth chapters

Chapter 1: Scoping under ambiguity

The vague ask

Every engagement starts with a sentence that is not a specification. "We want AI for our support team." "Can this read our contracts?" "We need to cut review time." These sentences are not requirements. They are wishes with a budget. Your first job is to make the wish into a plan. Do not pretend the wish was a plan from the start.

Discovery is the core skill. It is structured learning about the customer's work. You do this before you propose any technical solution. Engineers like to skip discovery and start building. Skipping discovery is the most costly mistake on customer work. One week of good discovery saves three months of building the wrong thing.

Discovery has three actions: watch, ask, and map.

Watch. Shadow the people who do the job now. Sit with a support agent for two hours. Watch a paralegal review a contract.

You look for the parts of the work that use pattern-matching. Those are good AI candidates. Then find the parts that need judgment when there is uncertainty. Those are hard for AI.

Then find the compliance theater. The human signature is the main point here. Do not change that part. Nobody can tell you this in a meeting. You must see it.

Ask. Run a discovery session with the question bank below. The questions look simple. The answers are not simple. The customer has usually never answered these questions with this level of detail.

Map. Draw the current workflow. Use boxes and arrows. Show where work enters. Show who touches it. Show where it waits.

Show where errors happen. Show where it exits. Mark each box. Use automate, assist, or leave alone. The map becomes the shared picture. You and the customer will point at it for the rest of the engagement.

How to read this diagram

  1. Start at the top. The vague request enters discovery, not engineering.
  2. Watch, ask, and map are in sequence. Watching first makes your questions specific. Mapping last makes you commit to what you learn.
  3. The diamond is the honest gate. Sometimes the answer is no. The task is all judgment. The data does not exist.
  4. The ROI is negative. Killing it here costs one week. Killing it after three months of building costs the relationship.
  5. Write success criteria and kill criteria before any prototype. The scoping memo is the contract that both sides sign.

The discovery question bank

Use these in the first workshop. Write notes word for word. The exact words are important later. You will write acceptance criteria.

# Question What you ask
1 Tell me about the last time you did this task. Give me each step. Where do you spend the time? Where does the task wait?
2 What does a perfect result look like? Show me one. The golden example. You will need it for evaluations.
3 What does a bad result look like? Show me the worst one you saw. The failure modes. Your evaluations must include these.
4 How do you know today if the work was good? The current quality check. Your system must be better than or equal to it.
5 What happens if it goes wrong? Who sees it? What is the cost? The blast radius. This sets the reliability you need.
6 Which parts are boring and repeated? Which parts need real judgment? The automate-vs-assist split.
7 What data exists about this task? Where is it? Who owns it? Data reality check. "In the CRM" often means "in three CRMs and a spreadsheet."
8 Who must approve this before it goes live? What will they ask? The hidden stakeholders. Security, legal, and compliance always appear. It is better in week one than in week twelve.
9 What caused the last attempt at this to fail? The landmines. Each failed pilot teaches you something if you ask.
10 If this works perfectly, what changes for the business? Give a number. The ROI input. You cannot do Chapter 5 without a number.

Question 9 is the most important question on the list. Customers rarely tell you about past failures. Ask directly. Ask what the vendor did wrong. Do not only ask what the technology did wrong. The answer is usually about trust.

It is not about accuracy.

Success criteria and kill criteria

A scope without numbers is a wish. Write both types of criteria before you build.

Success criteria are the numbers that mean "this worked." You must measure them on the customer's data. They must have a deadline. Good: "By week 8, the system drafts responses for 60 percent of tier-1 tickets. Agents accept the draft without changes at least 70 percent of the time. We measure this on live traffic." Bad: "The system improves support efficiency." The bad version cannot fail. This means it cannot succeed.

Kill criteria are the conditions that mean "stop." They protect both sides. Examples:

  • "If the acceptance evaluation stays below 80 percent after 4 weeks on real data, we stop. We then reassess."
  • "If legal blocks customer data from leaving the VPC, and no on-premise option fits the budget, we stop."
  • "If fewer than 5 pilot users work weekly by week 6, we stop." Write them down. Sign them. Killing a bad engagement early is a success. It is not a failure. The most trusted FDEs are the ones who killed something fast. They said why.

The scoping memo

How to read this diagram

  1. Top: the vague request. Example: make AI do our support tickets.
  2. The funnel: ask sharp questions. Who is it for, what must it do, how will we measure it.
  3. Bottom: the written scope. Goal, non-goals, success metrics, constraints, timeline, owner.
  4. If it is not written, it is not scoped. Verbal agreement drifts.

The parts

  • Vague request. What it is: the customer's first ask. Why it matters: it holds the real need inside fuzzy words. If accepted as-is: you build to a moving target.
  • Sharp questions. What they are: who, what, how measured. Why they exist: they turn fuzz into facts. If skipped: the scope stays a guess.
  • Written scope. What it is: the memo with goal, non-goals, metrics, constraints, timeline, owner. Why it exists: it is the contract you both sign. If skipped: every disagreement has no referee.

The scoping memo is a one-to-two-page document. Both sides sign it. It is the most useful artifact in this chapter. Here is the template.

Worked example: from vague ask to plan

The ask: "We want AI to help our procurement team review vendor contracts faster."

Discovery ran two days of shadowing. It showed that reviewers spend 40 minutes per contract. They mostly check 12 standard clauses against a policy document. The judgment call is only on non-standard clauses. This is about 20 percent of contracts. Data: 800 past contracts with reviewer markup are in SharePoint.

Legal must approve any system that touches contracts. The last attempt failed. This is because the vendor showed clean sample contracts. The real ones are scanned PDFs with coffee stains.

The scoping memo states the following: Automate the 12 standard clause checks. This is assist mode. A reviewer approves the checks. Leave non-standard clauses to humans. The success criteria are as follows: The median review time for standard contracts decreases from 40 minutes to 15 minutes by week 10. Measure this on live reviews.

Clause-check accuracy must be at least 95 percent on the acceptance set. The kill criteria are as follows: If scanned-PDF extraction accuracy stays below 90 percent after week 4, stop the project. This is the failure mode of the last attempt. The constraint is as follows: Contracts cannot leave the customer's Azure tenant. The risk is as follows: SharePoint permissions are per-folder and complex.

Notice what occurred. The vague request became a plan. The plan has numbers. It has a named failure mode from the past. It has a kill criterion. This is scoping.

Key takeaways

  • Discovery is watch, ask, map. Do not propose a technical approach before the map exists.
  • Every scope needs success criteria and kill criteria. Success criteria have numbers and dates. Kill criteria are conditions to stop. Write both before you build.
  • The scoping memo is a signed contract. It is one to two pages long. It is the artifact that the whole engagement refers to.
  • Question 9 is the most important question you can ask. Question 9 is "what did the last attempt fail on?".

Chapter 2: Solution architecture under enterprise constraints

Why this chapter comes next. Chapter 1 made a scoped plan. The plan shows the problem, the success criteria, and the kill criteria. That plan states what to build. It does not state where it can run. It does not state who can see the data.

It does not state which approvals gate it. The customer's enterprise provides those answers, not you. This chapter teaches you to read those constraints like a contract. It teaches you to pick the deployment pattern that survives them.

The customer's world, not yours

How to read this diagram

  1. Center top: your solution. The smallest thing that solves the pain.
  2. Below it, four gates. Your design must pass all four.
  3. Security review: who can see what. Audit every access.
  4. Compliance: SOC 2, HIPAA, GDPR. Prove it, do not claim it.
  5. Data residency: data stays in region. No silent exfiltration.
  6. Procurement: vendor approval, contract, SLA terms.
  7. Ask about the gates in week one, not week ten.

The parts

  • Security review. What it is: the customer's check on access. Why it exists: their data is their business. If it fails: the project stops.
  • Compliance. What it is: the regulatory checklist. Why it exists: the law requires proof. If it fails: the customer signs no contract.
  • Data residency. What it is: the rule on where data lives. Why it exists: regions have laws. If it fails: the architecture is illegal there.
  • Procurement. What it is: the buying process. Why it exists: companies buy through rules. If it fails: there is no purchase order.

You pick the stack in a lab. The stack picks you on an engagement. The customer's constraints decide the architecture. They decide this before you draw one box. Learn to read constraints. Read them like a lawyer reads a contract.

Every line changes what you can build.

The five main constraints appear on almost every enterprise engagement:

  1. Security review. The customer's information security team reviews your design. This happens before anything touches production data. They ask: what data flows where? What does the system log?
  2. Who can access the logs? How do you store secrets? What happens when an employee leaves? The review takes two to six weeks. Start it in week one.
  3. Do not start it in week eight. They do not accept "Trust us" as an answer. The playbook below lists the three artifacts to bring to the first meeting.
  4. Data residency. Some data cannot leave a country. Some data cannot leave a cloud region. Some data cannot leave the customer's own network. Healthcare, finance, and government add their own rules.
  5. Residency decides where the model runs. A vendor API is acceptable for public data. It is not possible for restricted data. Ask "where is this data allowed to live?" during discovery. The answer can stop an entire architecture.
  6. Legacy integration. The customer data is in systems. People built these systems before transformers. Transformers are the neural-network architecture behind modern language models. Examples are SAP, Salesforce with many custom fields, a document store with no API, and email inboxes. An API is a way for software to talk to the system.
  7. Your agent does not replace these systems. It talks to them. It usually uses the most basic interface. Plan enough time for integration. It always takes more time than you think.
  8. Identity and access. Enterprise users log in with single sign-on. Permissions are for each user, each folder, and each record. Your system must use the same permissions that the user already has.
  9. An agent that shows a document the user cannot see is a security problem. It is not a feature. Check permissions before the retrieval step. Never check them after the retrieval step.
  10. Procurement and budget cycles. Buying software takes many months. A pilot project needs a purchase order in week two. This will stop the project.
  11. Know if you use an existing contract. Know if you start a new procurement. Plan the timeline based on this information.

The four deployment patterns

Almost all projects use one of four patterns. The table compares them. The Python sample below makes the comparison into a decision tool.

Pattern Where the model runs Best when Watch out for
Vendor API (SaaS: software as a service) The AI vendor's cloud. You call it over the internet. Data is not restricted. Speed is most important. The customer already approved the vendor. Data leaves the customer's network. There is a cost per token at scale. Tokens are the small text pieces the model reads. Vendors charge per million tokens. Vendor outages are your outages.
Private cloud (VPC) The customer's cloud account, in their virtual private cloud Data must stay in the region. The customer has cloud engineers. There is a steady high volume. You operate inside their network. GPU capacity is their problem and your problem. Setup takes more time.
On-premises The customer's own data center Regulators restrict the data. Policy blocks every internet path. There is an existing GPU or CPU fleet. A GPU is a graphics processing unit. It is the chip that runs AI models. A CPU is a central processing unit. It is the computer's general-purpose chip. Hardware limits are hard limits. Updates are slow. You debug through their IT team.
Hybrid Sensitive steps on-prem or in VPC. General steps use the vendor API. Data sensitivity is mixed. You can split the workflow by classification. You operate two systems. The split itself needs a security review. Data labeling must be correct.

How to read this diagram

  1. The first question is always data classification. All other things follow from it.
  2. "Restricted" means regulated or contractually limited. Examples are health records, financial data, defense work, or customer data under a strict DPA. A DPA is a data processing agreement. It is the contract that controls how people handle customer data.
  3. If data cannot leave the network, and there is no internet path, you are on-premises. Accept this early. Fighting it wastes months.
  4. Volume decides between VPC and hybrid. A steady high volume justifies dedicated GPU capacity in the VPC. Spiky or mixed workloads fit a hybrid split.
  5. Unrestricted data goes to the vendor API. Do not build infrastructure for a problem. The API already solves the problem.

Worked example: a pattern decision in Python

The sample below scores the four patterns. It scores them against a customer's constraints. It is simple on purpose. The point is to have a repeatable, explainable decision. It is not about the algorithm. This is better than an architect's gut feeling.

Customers trust a scored table that they can discuss. They cannot discuss a recommendation that they cannot inspect.

# pattern_picker.py
#
# WHAT: Scores the four enterprise deployment patterns against a customer's
#       stated constraints and prints a ranked table with reasons.
# WHY:  Architecture arguments with customers go better when the reasoning is
#       visible. A scored table turns "I recommend VPC" into "VPC wins on
#       your three hard constraints, here is the math." The customer can
#       disagree with a weight, which is a productive argument. They cannot
#       argue with a gut feeling.
# WHAT BREAKS IF CHANGED:
#   - If you add a pattern, add it to PATTERNS and to the scorer. A pattern
#     missing from the scorer silently never wins, which is worse than an
#     error because nobody notices.
#   - Weights are opinions, not facts. If the customer says residency matters
#     more than cost, change the weights in front of them, re-run, and show
#     the new ranking. Never hard-code weights you are not willing to defend.

# Each pattern gets a 0-2 score on each dimension. 2 = strong fit, 0 = poor.
# Scores are relative judgments, documented in the comments, not measurements.
PATTERNS = {
    # Vendor API: fastest to build, but data leaves the customer's network.
    "vendor_api": {
        "data_stays_in_network": 0,  # data crosses the internet by design
        "speed_to_value": 2,         # no infra to build; call an endpoint
        "cost_at_scale": 1,          # per-token pricing hurts at high volume
        "ops_burden": 2,             # the vendor operates the model
        "works_offline": 0,          # no internet path means no service
    },
    # Private cloud VPC: the model runs in the customer's cloud account.
    "vpc": {
        "data_stays_in_network": 2,  # traffic stays inside their cloud
        "speed_to_value": 1,         # weeks of setup: networking, GPUs, images
        "cost_at_scale": 2,          # reserved GPUs beat per-token at volume
        "ops_burden": 0,             # someone must run the fleet; often you
        "works_offline": 1,          # works without public internet, needs cloud
    },
    # On-premises: the customer's data center, their hardware.
    "onprem": {
        "data_stays_in_network": 2,  # data never leaves the building
        "speed_to_value": 0,         # procurement plus install is months
        "cost_at_scale": 1,          # sunk hardware is cheap; new GPUs are not
        "ops_burden": 0,             # their IT team, your debugging-by-proxy
        "works_offline": 2,          # air-gapped (no network connection in or out) is the whole point
    },
    # Hybrid: split by data classification; sensitive steps stay inside.
    "hybrid": {
        "data_stays_in_network": 1,  # only if the split is correct; review it
        "speed_to_value": 1,         # two systems to wire up
        "cost_at_scale": 1,          # API for spikes, owned GPUs for base load
        "ops_burden": 1,             # the split is a second system to operate
        "works_offline": 1,          # the inside part works; the API part does not
    },
}

# Default weights: one possible opinion about what matters. Change these with
# the customer in the room. The ranking must be re-computable in seconds.
DEFAULT_WEIGHTS = {
    "data_stays_in_network": 3.0,  # usually the hardest constraint; weight it so
    "speed_to_value": 2.0,
    "cost_at_scale": 1.5,
    "ops_burden": 1.0,
    "works_offline": 1.0,
}


def score_patterns(weights=None):
    """Rank patterns by weighted score. Returns (name, score, notes) sorted best first.

    Complexity: O(P * D) for P patterns and D dimensions; trivially small.
    """
    weights = weights or DEFAULT_WEIGHTS
    ranked = []
    for name, dims in PATTERNS.items():
        # Weighted sum. Dimensions are 0-2, weights are customer-tuned.
        # A hard constraint can also be a veto (see veto_check below); the
        # score ranks the survivors, it does not overrule a veto.
        total = sum(dims[d] * weights[d] for d in weights)
        ranked.append((name, round(total, 1)))
    ranked.sort(key=lambda r: r[1], reverse=True)
    return ranked


def veto_check(hard_constraints):
    """Apply non-negotiable constraints. Returns the patterns still allowed.

    hard_constraints is a set like {"data_stays_in_network", "works_offline"}.
    A pattern survives only if it scores 2 (strong fit) on every hard
    constraint. Vetoes run BEFORE scoring: no weight can rescue a vetoed
    pattern. This models how security reviews actually work.
    """
    survivors = []
    for name, dims in PATTERNS.items():
        if all(dims[c] == 2 for c in hard_constraints):
            survivors.append(name)
    return survivors


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Example: a bank. Data cannot leave the network (hard constraint).
    # Speed matters (pilot deadline), cost at scale matters (high volume).
    hard = {"data_stays_in_network"}
    print("Survivors after veto:", veto_check(hard))
    print()
    print(f"{'pattern':<12}{'score':>7}")
    for name, score in score_patterns():
        mark = "  <-- vetoed" if name not in veto_check(hard) else ""
        print(f"{name:<12}{score:>7}{mark}")

  1. PATTERNS holds the judgment. It shows how well each pattern fits each dimension. It uses a 0-2 scale. The comments explain why each number is what it is. Change a number only if you can explain the new one aloud.
  2. DEFAULT_WEIGHTS holds the priorities. Note the comment: weights are opinions. The tool lets you change them live with the customer. Then you re-run the tool.
  3. veto_check runs before scoring. This shows reality. A hard constraint is "data cannot leave the network". This is not a preference that you can trade off. It removes options completely. Scoring only ranks the options that remain.
  4. The main block shows the bank example. vendor_api has a good speed score. But the hard constraint vetoes it. The printed table shows the score and the veto mark. The customer sees why the fast option is not available.
  5. What this does not do: It does not replace the security review. It does not know the customer's real GPU capacity. It structures the conversation. The conversation is the product.

The security review playbook

Start the review in week one. Bring three artifacts to the first meeting with infosec:

  1. A data-flow diagram. Show every system your data touches. Label every arrow with what flows. Show the encryption status of the data in transit and at rest. Draw it before the meeting. You cannot approve an undrawn architecture.
  2. A data inventory. Show what you store, where you store it, for how long, and who can read it. Include logs, prompts, and evaluation traces. Customers forget that prompts contain customer data. Infosec does not forget this.
  3. The permission model. Show how the system checks that a user can see what it shows them. "We check the same ACL (access control list: the record of who can see each document) the source system uses, before retrieval" is a sentence that ends meetings early. This is a good thing.

Common misunderstanding: the security review is not a hurdle to clear one time. It happens again every time the data flow changes. This includes a new tool, a new log sink, or a new vendor. Budget review time for every architecture change. Do not budget only for the first one.

  • Read the customer's constraints before you draw the architecture. These constraints include security review, data residency, legacy integration, identity and access, and procurement.
  • Four patterns cover almost every engagement. These patterns are vendor API, private VPC, on-premises, and hybrid. Data classification picks the pattern.
  • Hard constraints are vetoes. They are not weights. Score only what survives.
  • Start the security review in week one. Use a data-flow diagram, a data inventory, and the permission model.

Chapter 3: Evals as customer contracts

The measurement becomes the contract.

Why the customer must trust the measurement

Your model can be excellent. But your engagement can still fail. The failure looks like this: you show the customer a dashboard. It says 94 percent accuracy. The VP asks, "94 percent of what?" You explain your evaluation set. She says, "Those are not our real cases." The meeting ends.

Nothing ships.

The lesson: the eval is not a research artifact on customer work. It is a contract. Both sides agree in advance what to measure. They agree on what data to use. They agree on the pass threshold. They agree on what happens if the system passes or fails.

"Pass the acceptance eval" becomes the definition of done. This is in the scoping memo from Chapter 1.

The anatomy of an acceptance eval

How to read this diagram

  1. Top row: the three ingredients. A metric, a threshold, and a test set.
  2. The arrows combine them into one acceptance criterion.
  3. The example: 90 percent of 200 real tickets routed to the right queue, measured weekly.
  4. Pass the criterion and the customer signs. Miss it and you both know exactly why.

The parts

  • Metric. What it is: what you measure. Why it exists: without it, quality is an opinion. If it fails: nobody can agree on good.
  • Threshold. What it is: the number that means pass. Why it exists: it turns the metric into a decision. If it fails: every result is arguable.
  • Test set. What it is: the real examples you test on. Why it exists: it keeps the metric honest. If it fails: you optimize for toy data.

An acceptance eval has five parts. If you miss any part, the customer will find the gap at the worst moment.

  1. The golden set. This is a fixed set of real customer cases. It has known-correct answers. You build it with the customer's own experts. It is not sample data. It is not synthetic data at first.
  2. It uses real tickets, real contracts, and real questions. It has the answers that the customer's best people agree on. Supplement 11B shows how to build one without the labelers drifting. Size: start with 100 to 300 cases. If you use fewer than 100 cases, the confidence intervals are too wide to discuss (Supplement 11A). If you use more than 300 cases, the customer's experts will not finish labeling.
  3. The grader. This is code that scores the system on each case. It is deterministic where possible. It uses exact match for structured outputs. It uses rubric-based checks for free text.
  4. Use an LLM judge only if a deterministic check is not possible. Then, calibrate it against human graders first (Supplement 9A, Chapter 3). The customer must be able to read the grader. The customer must agree that it is fair. A grader that nobody understands is a grader that nobody trusts.
  5. The pass threshold. This is a number. You agree on it in advance. "The system passes if it scores at least 90 percent on the golden set.
  6. No critical-category case must fail." The "no critical failure" clause is important. Customers care more about the worst case than the average. A system at 95 percent that leaks one salary number is a failed system.
  7. The failure taxonomy. Every miss gets a category. Examples are: wrong answer, refused to answer, answered the wrong question, too slow, leaked restricted data. The taxonomy changes "it got 12 wrong" into "9 were formatting edge cases we can fix, 3 are real reasoning gaps." Customers accept categorized failures. They do not accept a single red number.
  8. The re-run rule. This rule states when and how the eval re-runs. It re-runs on every model or prompt change. It re-runs on a schedule.
  9. It re-runs before every release to pilot users. The eval is a gate in the deployment pipeline (Supplement 9A, Patch 2). It is not a one-time ceremony.

UAT for AI: the user acceptance test

Traditional software has user acceptance testing (UAT). Users try the system and sign off. AI systems need a UAT that is for non-determinism. This is because "try it and see if you like it" produces vibes, not a decision.

Run the AI UAT in three rounds:

Round 1: blind grading. Give 3 to 5 customer experts a shuffled mix of system outputs and human outputs. These are on golden-set cases. They grade without knowing which is which. This kills two biases at once. Nobody can dismiss an output just because it is AI.

Nobody can praise an output just because the demo was impressive.

Round 2: adversarial session. Invite the skeptics. Give them 30 minutes to break the system on purpose. Use tricky cases, edge inputs, and the unusual ticket from 2019. Log every break. Each break becomes a new golden-set case or a documented limitation.

Skeptics who break the system in the room become advocates when the system survives.

Round 3: the pilot shadow. Run the system with the current process for two weeks. Do not let the system act. The system drafts. A human decides. Measure the agreement rate between the system and the human.

This is the number the executive sponsor will quote. Make sure it is real before anyone quotes it.

Worked example: an acceptance eval runner in Python

The sample below is a small framework. It runs an acceptance evaluation. It loads a golden set. It runs a system function over the golden set. It grades each case. It checks the pass threshold.

It checks the no-critical-failure rule. It prints a failure taxonomy. This is the type of code you leave at a customer site.

# acceptance_eval.py
#
# WHAT: A minimal acceptance-eval runner. Loads a golden set (JSON), runs the
#       system's answer function on each case, grades with per-case grader
#       functions, checks the pass threshold plus the no-critical-failure
#       rule, and prints a failure taxonomy.
# WHY:  Customers trust evals they can read and re-run. This file is short
#       enough to walk through in a meeting and complete enough to gate a
#       pilot. Deterministic graders come first; an LLM judge is a plug-in,
#       not the default, because a judge the customer cannot inspect is a
#       judge the customer will not trust.
# WHAT BREAKS IF CHANGED:
#   - If the golden set file changes, version it (golden_v3.json) and record
#     which version gated which release. Re-running an old release against a
#     new golden set and comparing scores across versions is meaningless.
#   - The no-critical-failure rule is load-bearing. Removing it lets a 95%
#     average hide one catastrophic case. Customers remember the catastrophe.

import json


def load_golden_set(path):
    """Load golden cases. Each case: id, input, expected, critical (bool),
    and category (str) for the failure taxonomy.

    The golden set is data, not code. It lives in version control next to
    this runner, and the customer's experts own its contents.
    """
    with open(path, encoding="utf-8") as f:
        cases = json.load(f)
    # Validate shape early: a malformed case failing at 2am during a release
    # gate is how evals lose trust. Fail fast, fail loudly, fail now.
    for c in cases:
        assert {"id", "input", "expected", "critical", "category"} <= set(c), c["id"]
    return cases


def exact_grader(case, actual):
    """Deterministic grader for structured outputs. Returns (passed, detail).

    Exact match is the strictest fair grader for fields like clause names,
    amounts, or categories. For free text, write a rubric grader instead;
    do not fall back to vibes.
    """
    passed = actual.strip() == case["expected"].strip()
    return passed, "exact match" if passed else f"expected {case['expected']!r}"


def run_acceptance(system_fn, grader_fn, golden_path, pass_threshold=0.90):
    """Run the full acceptance eval. Returns a report dict.

    system_fn:  takes case["input"], returns the system's answer (str).
    grader_fn:  takes (case, actual), returns (passed: bool, detail: str).
    pass_threshold: minimum overall pass rate, agreed in the scoping memo.

    Complexity: O(N * (system + grader)) for N cases. The system call
    dominates; graders should stay cheap and deterministic.
    """
    cases = load_golden_set(golden_path)
    results = []
    for case in cases:
        # One case at a time, no shared state between cases. If the system
        # under test keeps conversation memory, reset it here; otherwise
        # case 12's answer leaks into case 13 and the eval is fiction.
        actual = system_fn(case["input"])
        passed, detail = grader_fn(case, actual)
        results.append({
            "id": case["id"], "passed": passed, "detail": detail,
            "critical": case["critical"], "category": case["category"],
        })

    total = len(results)
    passed_n = sum(1 for r in results if r["passed"])
    rate = passed_n / total if total else 0.0

    # The no-critical-failure rule: any critical miss fails the whole eval,
    # regardless of the average. This is the clause the customer's risk team
    # cares about. It is checked separately so it cannot be averaged away.
    critical_misses = [r for r in results if r["critical"] and not r["passed"]]
    accepted = (rate >= pass_threshold) and not critical_misses

    # Failure taxonomy: group misses by category so the fix list is concrete.
    taxonomy = {}
    for r in results:
        if not r["passed"]:
            taxonomy.setdefault(r["category"], []).append(r["id"])

    return {
        "total": total, "passed": passed_n, "rate": round(rate, 3),
        "threshold": pass_threshold, "accepted": accepted,
        "critical_misses": [r["id"] for r in critical_misses],
        "taxonomy": taxonomy,
    }


def print_report(report):
    """Print the report the way you would show it in a customer meeting."""
    print(f"Cases: {report['total']}  Passed: {report['passed']}  "
          f"Rate: {report['rate']:.1%}  Threshold: {report['threshold']:.0%}")
    print("ACCEPTED" if report["accepted"] else "NOT ACCEPTED")
    if report["critical_misses"]:
        # Critical misses are listed first and by name. No hiding.
        print("Critical misses:", ", ".join(report["critical_misses"]))
    if report["taxonomy"]:
        print("Failure taxonomy:")
        for category, ids in sorted(report["taxonomy"].items()):
            print(f"  {category}: {len(ids)} misses ({', '.join(ids[:5])}"
                  f"{'...' if len(ids) > 5 else ''})")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Demo wiring: a stub system and three toy cases. On a real engagement
    # the system_fn calls the deployed agent and the golden set has 100+
    # customer-labeled cases.
    toy_cases = [
        {"id": "c1", "input": "clause?", "expected": "termination",
         "critical": False, "category": "wrong_label"},
        {"id": "c2", "input": "amount?", "expected": "$50,000",
         "critical": True, "category": "wrong_amount"},
        {"id": "c3", "input": "date?", "expected": "2026-03-01",
         "critical": False, "category": "wrong_date"},
    ]
    with open("/tmp/toy_golden.json", "w", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        json.dump(toy_cases, f)

    def stub_system(prompt):
        # A deliberately imperfect stub so the report shows a taxonomy.
        return {"clause?": "termination", "amount?": "$5,000",
                "date?": "2026-03-01"}[prompt]

    report = run_acceptance(stub_system, exact_grader, "/tmp/toy_golden.json",
                            pass_threshold=0.90)
    print_report(report)

How to read this diagram

  1. load_golden_set validates every case at the start. A golden set that is not correct fails during the run. This destroys trust in the process. The assertion is the cheapest insurance.
  2. exact_grader is strict on purpose. For structured fields, an exact match is fair and clear. Structured fields include names, amounts, and dates. The comment shows where rubric graders belong. They belong in free text, written as code, not as opinions.
  3. run_acceptance loops. There is no shared state between cases. The comment about resetting conversation memory is a detail. This detail decides if the number is real.
  4. The no-critical-failure rule is a separate boolean. It is not part of the average. This is the structural guarantee. The risk team asked for this guarantee in the scoping memo.
  5. The taxonomy groups misses by category. "3 wrong_amount, 2 wrong_date" is a fix list. "87 percent" is not useful.
  6. The demo block wires a stub. You can run the file today. For an engagement, stub_system becomes the deployed agent. The toy file becomes the versioned golden set.

Running it prints:

Cases: 3  Passed: 2  Rate: 66.7%  Threshold: 90%
NOT ACCEPTED
Critical misses: c2
Failure taxonomy:
  wrong_amount: 1 misses (c2)

The stub fails on the critical amount case. The whole evaluation fails. This happens even if two of three cases pass. This is the rule. It works as designed.

Common misunderstanding: the demo versus the contract

The misunderstanding is: "The demo scored well. We are almost done." The demo ran on cases that someone chose to look good. The contract runs on cases that the customer's experts chose. These cases are representative. They include the difficult cases. The gap between demo accuracy and acceptance accuracy is usually 10 to 30 points.

This gap decides if engagements succeed or fail. Never quote the demo number after the acceptance evaluation exists. The acceptance number is the only number.

Key takeaways

  • The evaluation is a contract. It has a golden set, a grader, a pass threshold, a failure taxonomy, and a re-run rule. Agree on all five items before you start.
  • Build the golden set from real customer cases. Work with the customer's experts. Start with 100 to 300 cases.
  • Use deterministic graders first. Use an LLM judge only if deterministic checks are not possible. Calibrate the LLM judge first.
  • The no-critical-failure rule is not negotiable. One catastrophic miss fails the evaluation. This is true regardless of the average score.
  • Run UAT in three rounds. These rounds are blind grading, adversarial session, and pilot shadow. Skeptics who break the system in the room become advocates when the system survives.

Chapter 4: The prototype-to-production chasm

Why this chapter comes next. Chapter 3 defines good performance and how to measure it. You build the prototype. You show it to the customer. The customer claps. Then the prototype meets real data.

This chapter describes the most common failure in this work. The prototype fails on real data. This chapter shows how to harden the prototype to make it work again.

Why demos die on real data

The prototype works. The customer claps. Then you use it with real data. The prototype fails. This is the most common event in forward deployed work. It is also the least planned event.

The prototype works in a good environment. It uses clean sample documents. It has one user. It has no permissions. It has no traffic spikes. No one watches the logs.

Production is not good in specific, repeatable ways. Learn these ways. You can harden the prototype against them before the pilot. You do not harden it after an incident.

Real data is dirty. Scanned PDFs have coffee stains. Spreadsheets have merged cells and three header rows. Names have four different spellings. Dates have six different formats.

The demo's parser processes the sample. The real data set breaks the parser on document 40. Plan a full data-cleaning pass between the prototype and the pilot. This task is not glamorous. It decides if the engagement is successful.

Real users cause problems by accident. They paste 50 pages into a box that is for 5 pages. They ask questions in slang. They ask questions in fragments. They ask questions in the wrong language. They click the button two times.

They submit the form three times. The prototype assumes a cooperative user. Production must assume a tired human at 6pm on a Friday.

Real systems have permissions. The demo runs as an administrator. Production runs as a user. This user has partial access to a messy folder tree. The first permission-denied error comes in the pilot's first hour. Design the permission check in Chapter 2.

This helps you now. If you add it later, you must rewrite the retrieval path.

Real traffic has shape. The demo handles one request at a time. Production gets spikes on Monday mornings. It gets floods at quarter-end. One user pastes the entire archive. Load-test with the customer's actual traffic pattern.

Do not use a flat rate. Ask for last quarter's usage logs during discovery.

Real failures need owners. The demo's errors go to your laptop screen. Production's errors go to someone's phone at 2am. Before the pilot, write the runbook. The runbook shows what each alert means. It shows who responds.

It shows how to roll back. It shows how to pause the system without losing work that is in progress.

The hardening checklist

How to read this diagram

  1. Left: the prototype. It works on your laptop, for one user, on the happy path.
  2. Right: production. Many users, every path, auth, logs, alerts, support, docs, rollback.
  3. Middle: the chasm. Five workstreams sit inside it.
  4. Each item is a workstream: auth and SSO, scale and latency, monitoring and alerts, support and docs, rollback plan.
  5. Name them in the scope, or they surprise you later.

The parts

  • Prototype. What it is: the demo that works for you. Why it exists: it proves the idea. If confused with production: the pilot becomes the product.
  • Production. What it is: the system that works for everyone. Why it exists: customers need reliability. If skipped: the demo dies on real data.
  • The five workstreams. What they are: auth, scale, monitoring, support, rollback. Why they exist: each is real engineering work. If ignored: each becomes an incident.

Worked example: fuzzing the edges before the pilot

The sample below is an edge-case fuzzer. It tests an agent's input handling. It sends bad inputs to the system before real users do. These inputs include oversized inputs, strange encodings, double submissions, and permission edge cases. Each probe makes sure the system fails cleanly. A clean failure is a clear error, a timeout, or a refusal.

The system must not hang or leak data.

# edge_fuzzer.py
#
# WHAT: Throws unkind inputs at a system's entry point and checks that every
#       failure is clean: a clear error, a timeout, or a refusal. Never a
#       hang, never a leak, never a stack trace to the user.
# WHY:  Real users are adversarial by accident. Finding the ugly failures in
#       a fuzzer costs an afternoon. Finding them in the pilot costs trust,
#       and trust is the currency of the whole engagement.
# WHAT BREAKS IF CHANGED:
#   - The timeout values encode the SLO from Chapter 6. If the SLO changes,
#     update the timeouts here too, or the fuzzer certifies a system that
#     violates its own contract.
#   - These probes assume the system under test is the pilot build. Running
#     them against production without the customer's written permission is
#     a load test on someone else's system. Get permission first.

import time

# A probe is (name, input_builder, expectation). The expectation is a
# function of (result, elapsed_s) returning (ok: bool, note: str).
# Keeping probes as data means adding a new edge case is one dict entry,
# which is how the list grows to hundreds on a real engagement.


def _huge_text(n_chars=200_000):
    # 200k chars: the "user pastes the entire archive" case. Real support
    # tickets with full email threads hit this size. The system must reject
    # or truncate with a clear message, not hang or OOM.
    return "lorem ipsum dolor sit amet. " * (n_chars // 28)


def _weird_encoding():
    # Mixed encodings, zero-width chars, right-to-left marks: the copy-paste
    # from a PDF case. Parsers that assume clean UTF-8 die here.
    return "R\u00e9sum\u00e9\u200b\u202e of the caf\u00e9 \ud83d\ude00 plan"


PROBES = [
    {
        "name": "oversized_input",
        "input": _huge_text(),
        # Must fail fast with a clear message. Hanging is the failure mode
        # that pages someone at 2am; a fast clear error is a good outcome.
        "expect": lambda r, e: (e < 5.0 and "too large" in r.lower(),
                                f"{e:.1f}s"),
    },
    {
        "name": "empty_input",
        "input": "",
        # Empty input must produce a helpful prompt, not a model call that
        # burns tokens on nothing and returns a confident hallucination (an invented answer stated as fact).
        "expect": lambda r, e: ("please provide" in r.lower() or
                                "empty" in r.lower(), r[:60]),
    },
    {
        "name": "weird_encoding",
        "input": _weird_encoding(),
        # Must not crash and must not echo raw control characters back.
        # Echoing them back can break the customer's UI or, worse, execute
        # in a downstream system that interprets them.
        "expect": lambda r, e: ("\u202e" not in r and "\u200b" not in r, r[:60]),
    },
    {
        "name": "double_submit",
        "input": "DOUBLE_SUBMIT_MARKER",
        # Sent twice in a row: the system must be idempotent (safe to repeat: doing it twice has the same effect as once) or detect the
        # duplicate. Double-billing a customer because they double-clicked
        # is the kind of bug that ends engagements.
        "expect": lambda r, e: ("duplicate" in r.lower() or
                                "already" in r.lower(), r[:60]),
    },
]


def fuzz(system_fn, timeout_s=30.0):
    """Run all probes against system_fn(input) -> str. Returns a report.

    system_fn is the system's entry point. It must raise or return; a probe
    that neither returns nor raises within timeout_s is a hang, which is
    the worst possible outcome and is reported as such.
    """
    report = []
    for probe in PROBES:
        start = time.time()
        try:
            # NOTE: a production-grade fuzzer would run each probe in a
            # subprocess with a hard kill. This version relies on system_fn
            # cooperating with timeouts. For pilot hardening it is enough;
            # for adversarial testing, upgrade to subprocess isolation.
            if probe["name"] == "double_submit":
                first = system_fn(probe["input"])
                result = system_fn(probe["input"])  # send it twice
            else:
                result = system_fn(probe["input"])
            elapsed = time.time() - start
            ok, note = probe["expect"](result, elapsed)
            report.append({"probe": probe["name"], "ok": ok, "note": note})
        except Exception as exc:  # noqa: BLE001 - the fuzzer must not die
            # An exception from the system is itself a finding: it means an
            # edge input escaped input validation. Log it, do not crash.
            report.append({"probe": probe["name"], "ok": False,
                           "note": f"raised {type(exc).__name__}: {exc}"[:80]})
    return report


def _safe_note(note):
    # Notes can contain unprintable characters: the weird-encoding probe
    # feeds lone surrogates, and the stub echoes them back. Printing the
    # raw note would crash the report with a UnicodeEncodeError, which is
    # exactly the kind of "the measurement tool broke" failure that loses
    # trust. backslashreplace keeps the finding visible and the report alive.
    return note.encode("utf-8", "backslashreplace").decode("utf-8")


def print_fuzz_report(report):
    failures = [r for r in report if not r["ok"]]
    print(f"Probes: {len(report)}  Clean failures: "
          f"{len(report) - len(failures)}  Dirty: {len(failures)}")
    for r in report:
        mark = "ok  " if r["ok"] else "FAIL"
        print(f"  [{mark}] {r['probe']}: {_safe_note(r['note'])}")
    if failures:
        print("Fix every FAIL before the pilot. A dirty failure in the "
              "pilot is an incident; in the fuzzer it is a Tuesday.")

    def toy_system(prompt):
        # A stub with realistic flaws: no input limits, no dedup, no

        if prompt == "DOUBLE_SUBMIT_MARKER":

Common misunderstanding: hardening as a phase

The misunderstanding is this: hardening is an action you do one time. You do it after the prototype and before the pilot.

In practice, each new feature makes the gap a little wider. A new tool needs a permission review. A new input type needs fuzzing. A new model version re-runs the acceptance evaluation. Treat the checklist as a gate. Each release must pass through this gate.

Do not treat it as a phase you complete. The teams that treat it as a phase are the teams with a "hardening sprint" that never ends.

Key takeaways

  • The demo fails on real data in expected ways. These ways include dirty data, accidental adversarial users, permissions, traffic shape, and unowned failures. Plan for each of these.
  • Do the 12-item hardening checklist between the prototype and the pilot. Run it again as a gate on each later release.
  • Fuzz the edges before the pilot. Use oversized inputs, empty inputs, strange encodings, and double submits. Each production surprise becomes a new probe.
  • A clean failure is a good outcome. A clean failure is fast and gives a clear error. A hang, a leak, or a stack trace to the user is an incident.

The question every customer asks

How to read this diagram

  1. Top left: cost per task. Tokens plus infra plus support. Example: $0.42.
  2. Top right: price per seat. What the customer pays. Example: $49 per month.
  3. The bar: one seat at 100 tasks per month. Red is the $42 cost, green is the $7 margin. Widths are to scale.

The parts

  • Cost per task. What it is: tokens plus infra plus support for one task. Why it exists: it is the true unit cost. If unknown: you price blind.
  • Price per seat. What it is: what the customer pays monthly. Why it exists: it is the revenue unit. If wrong: the deal loses money.
  • Margin. What it is: price minus cost. Why it exists: it funds everything else. If thin: one usage spike erases it.

"What will this cost us?" This question comes in the first meeting. The honest answer is usually "it depends." Customers hear "it depends" as "we have not thought about it." This chapter gives you the model. So the answer is a number with assumptions. It is not a shrug.

The unit that matters is cost per successful outcome. This is the total cost of one task that is correct. It is not cost per token. It is not cost per API call. It is cost per successful outcome.

A cheap model has a 40 percent success rate. It needs human rework. This usually costs more than a pricier model with a 90 percent success rate. Customers see this in their budget. They cannot always name it. You must name it for them.

The full cost has five parts. Token cost is only the first part.

  1. Model cost. This is the input and output tokens per task. Multiply this by the model's price. Cached and repeated prompts change this much. Measure this on real traffic. Do not measure this on the demo.
  2. Infrastructure cost. This includes GPUs in the VPC. It includes the vector database. A vector database is a store. It searches documents by meaning.
  3. It includes hosting. It includes the eval rig. The eval rig re-runs on every change. Divide the fixed monthly costs by the monthly tasks.
  4. Human cost. This includes review time per task in assist mode. It includes exception handling. It includes on-call.
  5. This is usually the largest cost. Teams often forget this cost. One hour of reviewer time costs more than ten thousand model calls.
  6. Failure cost. This includes rework on wrong answers. It includes escalations. It includes the occasional incident. Model this as (1 minus success rate). Multiply this by the cost of handling a failure.
  7. Build and maintenance cost. This is the engineering time to build it. Amortize this over the expected lifetime. It also includes ongoing maintenance.
  8. This includes prompt updates. It includes eval maintenance. It includes model version migrations.

How to read this diagram

  1. Every task pays the model cost. It also pays its share of infrastructure. This is true whether the task succeeds or not.
  2. The diamond splits the human cost. Successful tasks need review time in assist mode. Failed tasks need rework. Both are labor costs. They have different rates.
  3. Build and maintenance costs amortize over the lifetime. A system that lives 6 months has 6 times the monthly build cost per task. Compare this to a system that lives 3 years. The lifetime assumptions must be clear.
  4. The output is one number. This number is what a correct completion costs. Compare options with this number. Do not compare options with token price.

Worked example: the cost model in Python

# unit_economics.py
#
# WHAT: Compares AI solution options on cost per successful outcome, the
#       number the customer actually feels. Includes model, infra, human
#       review, failure rework, and amortized build cost.
# WHY:  Token-price comparisons mislead. A cheap model with a low success
#       rate and heavy human review usually loses to a pricier model that
#       just works. This model makes the trade-off a number, and every
#       assumption is a named parameter the customer can challenge.
# WHAT BREAKS IF CHANGED:
#   - Success rates must come from the acceptance eval (Chapter 3), not from
#     vendor benchmarks or the demo. A success rate from the wrong
#     distribution makes every downstream number fiction.
#   - Human review minutes are the most uncertain input. Measure them in
#     the pilot shadow (Chapter 3, UAT round 3), do not estimate them in
#     a meeting. People are optimistic about review speed by 2-3x.

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Option:
    """One way of solving the task: a model choice plus an operating mode."""
    name: str
    # Model cost per task in dollars: measured tokens in/out x price.
    model_cost_per_task: float
    # Fraction of tasks completed correctly, from the acceptance eval.
    success_rate: float
    # Human minutes to review one successful task (assist mode).
    review_minutes: float
    # Human minutes to rework one failed task (escalation, correction).
    rework_minutes: float
    # Reviewer labor cost per hour in dollars, fully loaded.
    labor_per_hour: float = 75.0


@dataclass
class Program:
    """The fixed costs shared across tasks."""
    tasks_per_month: int        # real volume, from discovery (ask for logs)
    infra_per_month: float      # GPUs, vector DB, hosting, eval re-runs
    build_cost: float           # one-time engineering cost to build
    lifetime_months: int        # how long before a rebuild; be honest
    monthly_maintenance: float  # prompt updates, eval upkeep, migrations


def cost_per_successful_outcome(option, program):
    """The number that matters. Dollars per task completed correctly.

    Formula, per task:
      model + infra_share + build_share + maintenance_share
      + success_rate * review_cost + (1 - success_rate) * rework_cost
    then divided by success_rate to get cost per SUCCESSFUL task.

    Dividing by the success rate is the step people skip. It converts
    "cost per attempt" into "cost per outcome," which is what the
    customer's budget experiences.
    """
    infra_share = program.infra_per_month / program.tasks_per_month
    build_share = (program.build_cost / program.lifetime_months
                   / program.tasks_per_month)
    maint_share = program.monthly_maintenance / program.tasks_per_month

    review_cost = option.success_rate * (option.review_minutes / 60
                                         * option.labor_per_hour)
    rework_cost = ((1 - option.success_rate) * (option.rework_minutes / 60
                                                * option.labor_per_hour))

    per_attempt = (option.model_cost_per_task + infra_share + build_share
                   + maint_share + review_cost + rework_cost)
    # Guard: a zero success rate means infinite cost per outcome, which is
    # the math telling you the option is not viable. Do not smooth it away.
    assert option.success_rate > 0, f"{option.name} never succeeds"
    return per_attempt / option.success_rate


def compare(options, program):
    """Print a ranked comparison table."""
    rows = [(o.name, cost_per_successful_outcome(o, program)) for o in options]
    rows.sort(key=lambda r: r[1])
    print(f"{'option':<28}{'$/successful task':>20}")
    for name, cost in rows:
        print(f"{name:<28}{cost:>20.2f}")
    return rows


def breakeven_vs_human(option, program, human_cost_per_task):
    """How many tasks per month make the AI option cheaper than the human?

    Solves for volume where AI cost/task == human cost/task. Below that
    volume the fixed costs (infra, build) dominate and the human wins.
    This is the honest answer to 'is it worth it for our small team?'
    """
    # Per-task variable cost of the AI option (excludes volume-independent
    # fixed costs, which we add back as a monthly lump).
    var = (option.model_cost_per_task
           + option.success_rate * option.review_minutes / 60
           * option.labor_per_hour
           + (1 - option.success_rate) * option.rework_minutes / 60
           * option.labor_per_hour)
    fixed_monthly = (program.infra_per_month + program.monthly_maintenance
                     + program.build_cost / program.lifetime_months)
    # AI cheaper when var + fixed/n < human/success... solve for n:
    # n > fixed / (human_per_success - var_per_success)
    human_per_success = human_cost_per_task  # assume humans succeed
    denom = human_per_success - var / option.success_rate
    if denom <= 0:
        return None  # AI never wins: variable cost alone exceeds the human
    return fixed_monthly / denom


if __name__ == "__main__":
    program = Program(tasks_per_month=20_000, infra_per_month=4_000,
                      build_cost=120_000, lifetime_months=24,
                      monthly_maintenance=3_000)

    cheap = Option("small model + heavy review", model_cost_per_task=0.02,
                   success_rate=0.70, review_minutes=4.0, rework_minutes=15.0)
    pricey = Option("large model + light review", model_cost_per_task=0.12,
                    success_rate=0.92, review_minutes=1.5, rework_minutes=15.0)
    human = 12.50  # fully loaded cost of a human doing the task once

    print("Cost per successful outcome:")
    compare([cheap, pricey], program)
    print()
    for opt in (cheap, pricey):
        n = breakeven_vs_human(opt, program, human)
        print(f"{opt.name}: breakeven vs human at "
              f"{int(n):,} tasks/month" if n else f"{opt.name}: never cheaper")

Chapter 6: Reliability and SLOs for customer-facing AI

Why this chapter comes next. Chapter 5 priced the system. Now consider a harder question. What does the customer experience when the system fails? Traditional uptime metrics do not show the third state of AI systems. This state is an answer that looks correct but is wrong.

This chapter sets service level objectives for that reality. It includes error budgets and fallback cascades.

Why deterministic SLOs do not fit

Traditional SLOs assume deterministic systems. The service either returns the correct answer or it gives an error. AI systems add a third state. It returns an answer that looks correct but is wrong. The customer experiences all three states. So the SLO must cover all three.

An AI SLO has three dimensions. You need all of them:

Availability. Is the system up and responding? This is the classic dimension. Target: 99.9 percent of requests get a response within the timeout. The load balancer measures this. The model does not measure this.

Latency. How fast is the response? AI latency has a long tail. The p50 value looks good. The p95 value is bad. This is because long inputs make long outputs.

Make a Service Level Objective (SLO) on p95 and p99. Do not make an SLO on the average. The average hides the exact requests that the customer's executives tried in the demo.

Quality. Is the answer good? This is the new dimension. Measure quality with the acceptance evaluation. Re-run the evaluation on sampled production traffic (Chapter 3). Grade it in the same way.

An example target is: at least 90 percent of sampled production tasks pass the grader. There must be zero critical-category failures per week. Quality SLOs need the evaluation rig from Chapter 3. The rig must run all the time. It must not run only at release time.

Error budgets for non-deterministic systems

How to read this diagram

  1. Rung 1: availability, 99.9 percent uptime. About 43 minutes down per month.
  2. Rung 2: latency p95 under 3 seconds for the common task.
  3. Rung 3: task success. 95 percent of tasks complete without human rescue.
  4. Rung 4: time to recovery under 30 minutes, from alert to fixed.
  5. An SLO without a number is a wish. Write the number, then build the alert.

The parts

  • Availability. What it is: the uptime promise. Why it exists: customers plan on you. If it fails: trust breaks first.
  • Latency p95. What it is: the speed promise for most users. Why it exists: slow AI feels broken. If it fails: users stop waiting.
  • Task success. What it is: the completion promise. Why it exists: it measures the job done. If it fails: the system is a demo.
  • Time to recovery. What it is: the fix-speed promise. Why it exists: incidents happen. If it fails: small incidents become outages.

An error budget is the amount of failure you can have. You must stop shipping features and fix reliability if you use all of the budget. For deterministic services, the error budget is "1 minus the SLO". A 99.9 percent availability SLO gives a 0.1 percent budget. For AI systems, divide the budget into three parts. This is because the failure modes are different.

The fixes are also different.

Dimension Example SLO (30 days) Budget What burns it What the burn means
Availability 99.9% of requests answered 0.1% unanswered (~43 min) Outages, deploy failures, quota exhaustion Stop shipping; fix infra
Latency 95% of tasks under 30s 5% slow tasks Long inputs, model slowness, retry storms Cap input size, add caching, shed load
Quality 90% of sampled tasks pass grader 10% bad answers Prompt drift, data drift, model version change Freeze changes; re-run eval; roll back

The key rule is: feature work stops when a budget burns faster than planned. This is the agreement you make with the customer in the scoping memo. Reliability has a number. The number has power. Without this rule, every reliability conversation becomes a negotiation. With this rule, the dashboard makes the decision.

The burn rate is more important than the total burn. A quality budget burns at 2x the sustainable rate. With three weeks left in the month, you will go over budget before the month ends. Make an alert on the burn rate. Do not make an alert only on the budget breach.

Fallback design: the cascade

Every AI call can fail. The fallback cascade is the ordered list of what happens next. Design it before the pilot. The 2am version of you will not design it well.

How to read this diagram

  1. Order the cascade by cost and quality. Each step is cheaper and more reliable than the last step. Each step is usually lower quality. You must make this trade-off clear. You must agree to it in advance.
  2. Retry once. Do not retry in a loop. Retry storms can make one slow model stop the whole system. Retry one time. Then move down the cascade.
  3. The smaller model step exists because a fast, less accurate answer is better than no answer for most tasks. Know which tasks can use it (drafting). Know which tasks cannot use it (sending to a customer).
  4. The human handoff is the bottom of the cascade. It is not a failure. It carries full context. It shows what you tried.
  5. It shows what failed. It shows the partial work so far. A handoff that says "it broke, you deal with it" makes the human lose trust.
  6. Critical tasks skip the cascade. They go directly to mandatory human review. Sending a contract, a medical note, or a customer-facing message is never fully automatic. Decide the critical list in scoping. Do not decide it at 2am.
  7. Everything logs. The post-mortem needs the trail.

# slo_tracker.py
#
# WHAT: Tracks availability, latency, and quality SLOs for an AI system,
#       computes error-budget burn rates, and flags when feature work
#       should stop. Reads request records; prints a status table.
# WHY:  Reliability arguments with customers go badly when they are about
#       feelings. A burn-rate table turns "the system feels flaky" into
#       "quality is burning at 2.4x; per our agreement, features pause
#       until it is under 1x." The agreement part matters: set the rule
#       in the scoping memo (Chapter 1), enforce it with this script.
# WHAT BREAKS IF CHANGED:
#   - The quality sample must be representative production traffic graded
#     by the acceptance grader (Chapter 3). Grading only the easy traffic
#     makes the quality SLO a lie the dashboard tells.
#   - Thresholds below encode the example SLOs from the table above. If the
#     customer negotiates different SLOs, change them here and in the memo
#     together. A tracker and a memo that disagree is worse than neither.

from dataclasses import dataclass

# Request record: one task through the system. In production this comes
# from the audit log (Chapter 4, checklist item 5). Here it is a dict.
# Fields: ok (answered?), latency_s, quality_pass (grader verdict or None
# if the request was not sampled for quality grading).


@dataclass
class SLOBudget:
    """One error budget: target, window, and the burn-rate alert line."""
    name: str
    slo: float            # e.g. 0.999 availability
    window_days: float = 30.0
    # Pause features when burn rate exceeds this multiple of the
    # sustainable rate. 2x is the conventional fast-burn alert line.
    pause_multiple: float = 2.0

    def budget_fraction(self):
        # Allowed failure fraction over the window: 1 - SLO.
        return 1.0 - self.slo

    def burn_rate(self, failures, total):
        """How fast the budget is burning vs. the sustainable rate.

        Burn rate 1.0 = exactly on pace to exhaust the budget at the end
        of the window. Above 1.0 = breaching early. This is the number to
        alert on, not the raw failure count.
        """
        if total == 0:
            return 0.0
        observed = failures / total
        allowed = self.budget_fraction()
        # Guard: a 100% SLO (allowed == 0) means any failure is a breach.
        # Return infinity so the alert fires; do not divide by zero.
        if allowed == 0:
            return float("inf") if failures else 0.0
        return observed / allowed


def assess(records, latency_slo_s=30.0, latency_pct=0.95, quality_slo=0.90):
    """Assess all three budgets from request records. Returns a report."""
    total = len(records)
    avail = SLOBudget("availability", 0.999)
    lat = SLOBudget("latency", latency_pct)
    qual = SLOBudget("quality", quality_slo)

    avail_fail = sum(1 for r in records if not r["ok"])
    lat_fail = sum(1 for r in records if r["ok"] and r["latency_s"] > latency_slo_s)
    # Quality is measured on the sampled subset only. The sample must be
    # random production traffic, not hand-picked cases. If the sample is
    # small, say so: the report carries n so nobody over-reads it.
    sampled = [r for r in records if r["quality_pass"] is not None]
    qual_fail = sum(1 for r in sampled if not r["quality_pass"])

    out = {"total": total, "quality_sample_n": len(sampled), "budgets": []}
    for budget, failures, denom in (
        (avail, avail_fail, total),
        (lat, lat_fail, total),
        (qual, qual_fail, len(sampled)),
    ):
        rate = budget.burn_rate(failures, denom)
        out["budgets"].append({
            "name": budget.name,
            "burn_rate": round(rate, 2),
            "pause_features": rate > budget.pause_multiple,
        })
    return out


def print_assessment(report):
    print(f"Requests: {report['total']}  "
          f"Quality sample n: {report['quality_sample_n']}")
    for b in report["budgets"]:
        flag = "  <-- PAUSE FEATURES" if b["pause_features"] else ""
        print(f"  {b['name']:<12} burn rate {b['burn_rate']:>6.2f}x{flag}")
    if any(b["pause_features"] for b in report["budgets"]):
        print("Rule: a burning budget pauses feature work until burn < 1x.")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Toy traffic: 1000 requests, mostly fine, quality degrading.
    import random
    rng = random.Random(7)
    records = []
    for i in range(1000):
        ok = rng.random() > 0.0005
        latency = rng.gammavariate(4, 4)  # mean ~16s, long tail
        # Sample 10% for quality grading; recent drift makes 25% fail.
        qp = (rng.random() > 0.25) if i % 10 == 0 else None
        records.append({"ok": ok, "latency_s": latency, "quality_pass": qp})
    print_assessment(assess(records))

How to read this diagram

  1. SLOBudget holds one budget. This budget is the SLO target and the pause line. The burn_rate method is the core method. This method is the observed failure fraction divided by the allowed failure fraction.
  2. A rate of 1.0 means that the system is exactly on pace. The method has three lines because the idea is simple. The discipline is in the use of the method.
  3. assess splits failures into three dimensions. These dimensions come from independent fields of the same request records. Availability comes from ok. Latency comes from the tail. Quality comes from the sampled grader verdicts. One log stream feeds all three dimensions.
  4. The quality sample is the subtle part. You cannot grade every production task. This is because of cost and latency. So, you grade a random sample.
  5. The report prints the sample size. This ensures that no one treats n=30 like n=3000. Supplement 11A tells you the necessary sample size for your claim.
  6. pause_features is the teeth. If burn exceeds 2x, the report says PAUSE FEATURES. The team agreed the rule in the scoping memo. The script only reads the dashboard.
  7. The toy traffic shows quality burning. Availability and latency look fine. This is the classic AI failure mode. A latency-only dashboard would call this system healthy. The three-budget table does not call this system healthy.

The incident ritual

If something breaks in production, do the same ritual every time:

  1. Mitigate first. Roll back, pause the system, or route to the human handoff. The customer cares that the problem stops. The customer does not care that you understand the problem yet.
  2. Write the timeline. Write what happened. Write when it happened. Write what you did. Write only facts.
  3. Find the cause. Use five whys. Do not blame. "The eval did not cover scanned PDFs" is a cause. "The intern messed up" is not a cause.
  4. Add the probe. Every incident becomes a fuzzer probe (Chapter 4). It usually becomes a new golden-set case (Chapter 3). The system gets stronger in the exact place where it broke.
  5. Share the post-mortem with the customer. Share it within 48 hours. Be honest. Be brief.
  6. Include the fix and the prevention. Customers forgive incidents. They do not forgive silence.

Key takeaways

  • AI SLOs need three dimensions. These dimensions are availability, latency (p95/p99, never the average), and quality (grader on sampled production traffic).
  • Split the error budget into three parts. Alert on burn rate. Do not alert on raw counts. If a budget burns past the agreed line, feature work stops.
  • Design the fallback cascade before the pilot. Retry once. Use a smaller model. Use a cache. Use a clean human handoff. Critical tasks always need human review.
  • Every incident becomes a fuzzer probe and a golden-set case. Share the post-mortem within 48 hours.

Chapter 7: Stakeholder communication

Why this chapter comes next. Chapters 1 through 6 built the engineering half. This includes scope, architecture, evals, hardening, economics, and reliability. Experienced FDEs will tell you that this is the smaller half. The customer never sees your code. They see your memos, your updates, and your answers if something breaks.

This chapter teaches the writing that carries all the rest.

The job is half writing

Ask experienced FDEs what the job is. Many will say: "20 percent building, 80 percent communicating." That ratio surprises engineers. It should not surprise them. The customer cannot see your code. They see your updates, your memos, and how you handle bad news. Communication is the interface of the engagement.

There are three audiences. There are three different needs:

The executive sponsor (VP or above) wants outcomes and risks. Give this information in five minutes, one time each week. They do not want architecture information. They want to know: Are we on track? What did we learn? What do you need from me?

If you need a decision, state it in the first three sentences.

The working team (the users and their managers) wants specific information and honesty. What changed this week? What broke? What do you need from them? (For example, data access, labelers, pilot users). They can sense dishonesty.

Do not be dishonest.

The blockers (security, legal, procurement, IT) want precision and lead time. What exactly are you asking to approve, what data it involves, when do you need the answer. Give them the data-flow diagram from Chapter 2 and six weeks, not a vague request and six days.

How to read this diagram

  1. Start with you in the center. The same facts go out. But the message changes for each audience.
  2. The sponsor gets outcomes and risks in five minutes, one time each week. If you need a decision, state it in the first three sentences.
  3. The working team gets specific information and honesty. What changed? What broke? What do you need from them? Do not be dishonest.
  4. The blockers get precise information and lead time. Give them exactly what to approve. Tell them what data is necessary. Tell them when you need the answer.

The weekly status update

How to read this diagram

  1. Exec sponsor: monthly. Outcomes, risks, asks.
  2. End users: weekly. What changed and how it helps them.
  3. IT and security: per change. What data moves and what the system logs.
  4. Your team: daily. Blockers, decisions, next step.
  5. Silence reads as bad news. A short update on time beats a long update late.

The parts

  • Exec sponsor. Who: the buyer. What they need: outcomes and risks. If neglected: funding dries up.
  • End users. Who: the people doing the work. What they need: what changed for them. If neglected: adoption dies.
  • IT and security. Who: the gatekeepers. What they need: data movement and logs per change. If neglected: the gates close.
  • Your team. Who: the builders. What they need: blockers and decisions daily. If neglected: work stalls silently.

Send it every week. Send it on the same day. Use the same format. Regularity builds trust faster than good news. This is the template:

WEEKLY UPDATE - [engagement name] - [date]

Status: GREEN / YELLOW / RED (one word, first line)

Done this week (3 bullets max, outcomes not activity):
- ...
Shipped or learned, not "worked on."

Plan for next week (3 bullets max):
- ...

Needs from you (named owner + date for each):
- [name]: [decision or action] by [date]

Risks (each with owner and mitigation):
- [risk]: [what we are doing], owner [name]

Numbers (the same metrics every week):
- [outcome metric 1]: [value] (target [x] by [date])
- [outcome metric 2]: [value] (target [x] by [date])
- Acceptance eval: [pass/fail, rate]  |  Pilot users: [n]  |  Cost/task: [$]

These are the rules for the update: The status word goes first. It must be honest. YELLOW is not a failure. YELLOW with a mitigation plan shows competence. RED buried in paragraph four causes trust to end. Never let the customer find a problem before you tell them.

"You will hear this from me first" is a promise. You make it one time. You keep it always.

Setting expectations: the expectation curve

Every engagement follows the same curve. The demo sets expectations high. Reality pulls them down. (For example, dirty data, security review, edge cases). Then steady progress rebuilds them. This rebuild uses real results as a foundation.

Your communication job is to make the curve flat. Under-promise early. Show real progress often. Never let the gap between promise and reality grow silently.

How to read this diagram

  1. The demo sets expectations high. Reality pulls them down. For example, dirty data, security review, edge cases.
  2. The trough is normal. It reaches its lowest point around the pilot. This is when the system meets real data.
  3. Steady progress rebuilds expectations. This rebuild uses real results. The rebuilt line stays honest. It stays below the demo peak.
  4. Your communication job is to make the curve flat. Under-promise early. Show real progress often. Name the trough before it arrives.

The trough is normal. Name it in advance. Say: "After the demo, expect a dip when we use your real data. Every engagement has one. The acceptance evaluation is how we climb out." A predicted trough is a plan. An unpredicted trough is a crisis.

Saying no to infeasible asks

Customers ask for things that you cannot build. One asks for 100 percent accuracy. One asks to go live next week. One asks the system to read the CEO's mind from three bullet points.

Saying yes to protect the relationship destroys the relationship later. Saying no protects it. You must say it correctly.

The structure of a good no has four parts. They are the direct answer, the reason in their terms, what you can do instead, and the cost of the alternative.

How to read this diagram

  1. Read the four boxes in order. A good "no" is a sequence. It is not a single sentence.
  2. The direct answer comes first. It is plain. Then give the reason. Translate it into their costs and risks.
  3. Every "no" ends with an alternative and a price tag. A "no" without an alternative is only a rejection.
  4. The bottom box lists the asks that you refuse with no alternative. These are 100 percent accuracy, skipped security reviews, go-live without a passing evaluation, and unlicensed data.

Subject: Re: going live next week

Direct answer: We cannot go live next week responsibly.

Reason (their terms, not ours): The acceptance eval is at 78% and the
no-critical-failure rule from our scoping memo is not met. Going live now
means roughly 1 in 5 contract reviews would need full rework, which puts
more load on your team, not less.

What we can do instead: Run the pilot shadow for two more weeks while we
fix the two failing categories (scanned-PDF extraction, date formats).
That gets us to the 90% bar we agreed on.

Cost of the alternative: Two weeks of timeline. No additional cost.

Your call: I recommend the two weeks. If the timeline is fixed, tell me
and we will descope to typed contracts only, which already pass.

Note what the "no" does not do. It does not blame. It does not hide behind jargon. It does not leave the customer with nothing. Every "no" comes with an alternative and a price tag. "No, but here is what yes looks like" is the whole skill.

You must always refuse these requests. Refuse the request for 100 percent accuracy. Refuse the request to skip the security review. Refuse the request to go live without a passing acceptance evaluation. Refuse the request to use data the customer does not have rights to. For the first three requests, offer the alternative in parentheses.

For 100 percent accuracy, offer the measured rate plus the failure taxonomy. For skipping the security review, offer starting the review today in parallel. For going live without a passing acceptance evaluation, offer the pilot shadow. The fourth request is always no.

The difficult conversations playbook

The evaluation failed. State the number first. Then state the taxonomy. Then state the plan. For example: "The acceptance evaluation came in at 82 percent. Our target is 90 percent.

The misses are in two categories: scanned-PDF extraction (11 cases) and date formats (7 cases). We will fix extraction this week. We will re-run the evaluation on Friday. The timeline moves two weeks. This is the cost."

If you send these messages early, they stay updates. If you send them late, they become crises.

Bad news with a plan is a status update. Bad news without a plan is a crisis.

The timeline slipped. Say this the week you know it. Do not wait until it happens. State the cause in one sentence. State the new date. State what changed to protect the new date.

Never state a slip without a new date. "We are late" is a feeling. "We are two weeks late. We will ship on the 14th. The SharePoint permission audit took three weeks instead of one." This is information.

The customer wants to kill it. The kill criteria from Chapter 1 can trigger. Or the sponsor can lose budget. Do not fight this. Kill the project well. Document what you learned.

Hand over the evaluation rig and the golden set.

The customer will restart in six months. The customer will remember who left things clean. Write the honest retro. Engagements that end cleanly restart. Engagements that end in a fight do not restart.

Scope creep. "Can it also handle invoices?" The answer is the scoping memo. "That is a different workflow. It has different success criteria. Let us finish the contract review scope we agreed on. Then we will scope invoices as phase two with its own memo." The memo is your shield.

Write it well in Chapter 1. It protects you here.

Common misunderstanding: communication as overhead

The misunderstanding is this: status updates, memos, and expectation-setting are overhead. They take time away from building. For customer work, the communication is the building. The customer cannot evaluate your code. So the customer evaluates your clarity. A brilliant system with confusing updates gets killed.

A good system with clear updates gets expanded. Budget the writing time like engineering time. It is engineering time.

Key takeaways

  • There are three audiences. The sponsor wants outcomes and risks in five minutes. The working team wants specifics and honesty. The blockers want precision and lead time.
  • Send the weekly update every week. Use the same format. State the status word first. Always be honest. The customer hears bad news from you first, every time.
  • Name the expectation trough in advance. A predicted dip is a plan. An unpredicted dip is a crisis.
  • Every "no" has four parts. It has the direct answer. It has the reason in their terms. It has what you can do instead. It has its cost. Always refuse 100 percent accuracy, skipped security reviews, going live without a passing evaluation, and unlicensed data.
