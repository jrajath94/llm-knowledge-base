Post-training and RL

Pre-training builds a model that predicts the internet. This volume turns the model into one that does what you mean. It covers supervised fine-tuning, preference data, and the full RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) pipeline. It explains PPO (proximal policy optimization) without mystique.

It derives DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) line by line. It covers GRPO (group-relative policy optimization) and verifiable rewards. It covers Constitutional AI, reward hacking, and the eval-driven loop that ties it all together. It ends with SFT plus DPO on the tiny GPT you built in Volume 5.

This volume assumes that you know the prerequisites well. The prerequisites are KL divergence and cross-entropy (Vol 1), the pre-training objective and loss curves (Vol 5), and distributed setups (Vol 6). It names these topics and never re-teaches them. The forward links are: Vol 8 serves the aligned model, Vol 11 evaluates alignment claims, and Vol 12 covers the InstructGPT, DPO, and R1 papers.

1. The alignment problem: what pre-training gives you, and why it is not enough

Design prompt

  1. "What does pre-training actually optimize? Why is that different from being helpful?"
  2. "Give me three concrete ways a raw base model fails when you use it as an assistant."
  3. "What do researchers mean by helpful, harmless, and honest?"

1.1 What pre-training gives you

Pre-training, as built in Volume 5, trains one objective: predict the next token. The model reads trillions of tokens of internet text and learns the probability distribution of what comes next. That is all it learns to do. The result is a simulator of text, not an assistant.

Think about what next-token prediction means. Use real internet text as the example. The training text holds a question and an answer. It also holds a question followed by more questions. It holds an argument, a joke, or a rant. It holds code with bugs and a polite lie.

The model learns all these patterns. When you hand the model a prompt, it continues the pattern that fits the prompt. The model does not know that you wanted an answer. It only knows what text like this usually looks like.

1.2 Why that is not enough

There is a gap. On one side is "what text usually follows this prompt on the internet". On the other side is "what the user actually wants". That gap is the alignment problem.

A base model optimizes for plausibility. Plausibility means a likely-looking continuation. An assistant optimizes for intent. It finds what the human meant. It says it in a helpful way. It causes no harm.

Scale alone does not close the gap. The InstructGPT team asked human labelers to compare model outputs. The labelers chose the outputs of the small instruction-tuned model. That model had 1.3 billion parameters. They did not choose the outputs of the raw base model. That model had 175 billion parameters.

The small aligned model beat the giant unaligned model at the job that users actually wanted. For assistant behavior, alignment mattered more than raw scale.

[KEY]

The sentence to carry: pre-training teaches the model what text looks like. Post-training teaches the model which text to produce. The first is about the world. The second is about behavior.

1.3 Concrete failures of a raw base model

These are not hypothetical. Feed a base model these prompts and watch what happens:

PromptWhat you wantedWhat a base model often does
"Translate 'hello' to French:"The word "bonjour".It continues the pattern. It writes "Translate 'goodbye' to French:" and "Translate 'cat' to French:". It learned that translation prompts on the internet come in lists.
"Q: What is 2+2? A:""4"."Q: What is 3+3? A:". The model answers with another question. The most common continuation of a Q/A prompt is another Q/A pair, not an answer.
"Write a polite rejection email."A polite email.The pattern can suggest anything: a rude email, a funny email, or ten variants. The objective never requested politeness. The model only predicts politeness from context.

Two more failure patterns matter. Persona drift: the model takes the voice that the prompt hints at. It can sound like a liar, a sycophant, or a conspiracy theorist. It simulates text. It does not assist.

No refusal calibration: a base model has no notion of "I should not answer this". Sometimes it completes a request that is harmful. That is bad. Sometimes, after naive filtering, it refuses a request that is harmless. That is also bad.

Calibration is a trained behavior. Calibration means that the model knows where the line is.

1.4 What "aligned" means: helpful, harmless, honest

The research community converged on three words, often shortened to HHH:

  • Helpful. Do the task the user actually meant. Ask a clarifying question when the request is ambiguous. Prefer the useful answer over the plausible one.
  • Harmless. Do not help with wrongdoing. Do not make hateful or dangerous content. Avoid subtle harms like manipulation. Refuse cleanly. Explain briefly.
  • Honest. Do not make things up. Say "I do not know" when appropriate. Say how uncertain the model is. Do not act more confident than the model is.

These three goals conflict in practice. The conflicts are the interesting part. Helpfulness says "answer the question". Harmlessness says "not that one".

Honesty says "admit uncertainty". Helpfulness says "still try". Post-training resolves these conflicts. It follows human judgment. There is no formula for that. That is why human feedback is in the loop.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-training optimizes next-token prediction. The model simulates text patterns. It does not assist.
  • A raw base model continues the text. It does not answer. It drifts across personas. It has no refusal calibration.
  • Alignment shapes behavior. It has three goals: helpful, harmless, honest. It resolves conflicts between the goals. It follows what humans want.
  • Evidence it matters more than scale: 1.3B InstructGPT preferred over 175B GPT-3, as reported.

2. SFT: supervised fine-tuning, mechanically

Design prompt

  1. "What is SFT, mechanically? Which tokens get loss and why?"
  2. "What can SFT teach a model? What is fundamentally beyond its reach?"
  3. "Why is quality more important than quantity in SFT data?"

Chapter 1 named the goal: a model that behaves like an assistant, not a text simulator. The problem it left unsolved is where to start. SFT is the cheapest first answer: show the model the shape of the job by example, before any talk of preferences or rewards.

2.1 What it is, mechanically

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the simplest post-training step. You collect pairs of (instruction, ideal response). Humans write them. Then you keep training the base model with the exact same next-token loss it saw in pre-training (cross-entropy, from Volume 1), but now on these pairs. One mechanical difference matters.

The loss covers only the response tokens. The model masks out the prompt tokens.

Why mask the prompt? The model does not need to learn to predict your instructions. It needs to learn to predict good responses to instructions. If you put loss on the prompt, the model learns to imitate user prompts. That wastes capacity. It also dilutes the gradient on the part that matters.

# SFT loss: standard cross-entropy, response tokens only.
# prompt_ids: tokenized instruction. response_ids: tokenized ideal answer.
input_ids = prompt_ids + response_ids

# -100 is PyTorch's "ignore this position" label. The prompt
# contributes zero loss and zero gradient. Only the response teaches.
labels = [-100] * len(prompt_ids) + response_ids

loss = model(input_ids=input_ids, labels=labels).loss
loss.backward()  # the model learns P(response | prompt), nothing else

Here is a common training recipe. The values are illustrative. Use a small learning rate. Make it roughly 10x smaller than the pre-training rate. Run a few epochs over the SFT set. Often 1 to 3 is enough.

Use a chat template. It marks the roles, like <user> ... <assistant> .... The template matters. It teaches the model a rule. The rule is: "when I see the assistant marker, I produce assistant behavior". Later chapters build on that marker.

How to read this diagram
  1. The token row, left to right. Start with the token row. Read it from left to right. This is one training example as the model sees it. The instruction tokens come first. Then come the ideal response tokens. The model reads the prompt first, then it predicts the response.
  2. Gray chips labeled PROMPT, bracket "NO LOSS" crossed out. The pipeline masks these positions (the -100 labels in the code). No gradient flows from them. With loss here, the model learns to generate user prompts. That skill is useless.
  3. Teal chips labeled RESPONSE, bracket "LOSS HERE". Every one of these positions contributes cross-entropy loss. The gradient pushes the model to assign high probability to the shown answer, token by token.
  4. The network box below. It is the same transformer from pre-training. It still predicts the next tokens. SFT changes the data, not the architecture. The objective sees new data. It is still the next-token objective.
  5. What breaks if this step fails: mask the wrong tokens. You train a prompt-imitator. Too high a learning rate erases pre-trained knowledge (catastrophic forgetting, from Volume 3). Too many epochs on a tiny set, and the model memorizes the demos. It never learns the general pattern.

2.2 Data curation: the actual work

SFT is 10 percent algorithm and 90 percent data curation. The questions curators answer:

  • Who writes the demonstrations? The classic recipe: trained human contractors write the demonstrations. They follow detailed guidelines. Strong models increasingly draft the responses. Humans verify and edit the drafts. This is distillation with a human filter.
  • What makes a demonstration good? Correctness comes first. Then comes the target style. The style has direct answers and clear structure. It is honest about uncertainty. It says "I am not sure" where appropriate, and it handles refusal correctly.
  • How diverse must the set be? Cover the shape of deployment. That means Q and A, summarization, and coding. It also means writing help, multi-turn follow-ups, and edge cases. A model trained only on single-turn Q and A fumbles follow-up questions. It never saw that pattern.
  • How much data? The InstructGPT recipe used about 13,000 demonstration prompts, as reported. The LIMA paper used only 1,000 examples. The 1,000 carefully curated examples aligned a 65B model, as reported. Both point the same way. A smaller set of excellent demonstrations beats a large set of mediocre ones.
How to read this diagram
  1. Top: the intake. Prompts and draft responses enter wide: 100K+ candidates, with strong models drafting and humans filtering.
  2. The narrowing. Each stage removes material: correctness first, then target style, then diversity across Q and A, coding, writing, multi-turn, and edge cases.

2.3 What SFT can do

SFT teaches the model the shape of the job:

  • The assistant format. Respond to the user, in the assistant role, in the target language and style. This is the biggest single win. It converts the simulator into something that at least plays the assistant role.
  • Output schemas. JSON when asked for JSON. Bullet lists, code blocks, step-by-step working. Format compliance is highly learnable from demos.
  • Task patterns. How a good summary looks, how to phrase a refusal, how to ask a clarifying question. Each is a pattern the model can copy.
  • A starting policy for RL. Every later stage in this volume (reward model training, PPO, DPO) initializes from the SFT model and treats it as the reference. A good SFT checkpoint makes everything downstream easier.

2.4 What SFT cannot do

This is the part reviewers probe. SFT is behavioral cloning: it copies behavior it has seen. That sets hard limits:

  • It cannot teach what is not shown. If no demo shows the model reasoning through a hard math problem, SFT will not create that ability. It surfaces capabilities from pre-training. It rarely creates new ones. The LIMA authors called this the superficial alignment hypothesis: alignment is mostly about eliciting existing capability, not installing new knowledge.
  • It shows good, never contrasts bad. SFT says "do this". It never says "this looked plausible but was worse, and here is why". Ranking quality among decent answers is beyond it. That gap is exactly what preference training (Chapters 3 to 5) fills.
  • It clones mistakes too. Noisy or mediocre demonstrations bake in mediocre behavior. A demo with a confident wrong answer teaches confident wrongness. Garbage in, garbage out, with no mechanism to correct it.
  • It is brittle off-distribution. Prompts shaped unlike the demos (new domains, adversarial phrasing) degrade fast, because cloning only works where the examples reach.
[MISUNDERSTANDING]

"SFT teaches the model new knowledge." Mostly no. If the base model never saw the fact in pre-training, a few SFT demos will not install it reliably. At best they teach the model to parrot the demo's phrasing. Knowledge comes from pre-training (Vol 5). SFT teaches behavior given knowledge.

The classic trap: "we SFT'd on our internal docs, why does it still hallucinate our API?" Do not use SFT for knowledge injection. That job belongs to retrieval (Vol 9). It also belongs to continued pre-training.

[FIELD NOTE]

How a strong candidate says it: "SFT is behavioral cloning. The loss covers only response tokens. It teaches format, style, and task patterns. It gives the reference policy for everything else."

"But it only copies shown behavior. It never puts a good answer above a mediocre one. It cannot install knowledge the base model lacks. When SFT plateaus, add preference signal. Do not add more demos."

Key takeaways

  • SFT = next-token cross-entropy on (instruction, ideal response) pairs, loss on response tokens only (prompt masked with ignore labels).
  • It teaches the assistant format, output schemas, and task patterns, and produces the reference policy for all later stages.
  • Limits: behavioral cloning. Cannot create un-demonstrated abilities, cannot rank good vs better, clones demo mistakes, brittle off-distribution.
  • Data quality dominates quantity: ~13k demos (InstructGPT) and even 1k excellent demos (LIMA) suffice, as reported.

Lab pointer (15 min): write 5 instruction/response pairs in a narrow domain (e.g. summarizing error messages). For each, mark exactly which tokens would carry loss. Then deliberately write one bad demo (confident wrong answer) and note what the model would learn from it.

3. Preference data: the fuel everything runs on

Design prompt

  1. "Why do we collect rankings instead of absolute scores?"
  2. "What makes a preference pair high quality? What are the classic pitfalls?"
  3. "Why do practitioners say data beats algorithm in RLHF?"

SFT can only imitate the demonstrations you write. It cannot learn which of two good answers is better, because no demo ever says that. This chapter collects the other kind of human signal, comparisons, which is the fuel for everything in Chapters 4 through 6.

How to read this diagram
  1. Left circle: the training demos. Everything the model was shown to copy.
  2. Right circle: the deployment prompts. Everything users will actually ask.
  3. The overlap: where cloning works. New domains and adversarial phrasing outside it degrade fast, because cloning only works where the examples reach.
  4. The reading rule. This is the superficial alignment hypothesis in one picture: SFT surfaces capabilities from pre-training; it rarely creates new ones.

3.1 What it is

Preference data is a dataset of comparisons. Each item is a tuple: a prompt, two (or more) model responses, and a human judgment of which is better. Often there is also a "tie" option. That is the whole data structure. Everything in Chapters 4 through 6, reward models, PPO, DPO, GRPO, is machinery for squeezing signal out of these comparisons.

Why comparisons instead of scores like "rate this answer 1 to 5"? Humans are bad at absolute scales and good at relative ones. Your "4 out of 5" and my "4 out of 5" mean different things, and both of us drift over a long labeling shift. But "A is better than B" is a judgment people make quickly and consistently. Rankings also dodge the calibration problem: the model never needs to know what a "4" means, only which of two options wins.

How to read this diagram
  1. Top card, amber, "PROMPT". One fixed prompt. Both candidate responses answer this same prompt. Fixing the prompt is what makes the comparison fair: the judge compares answers, not questions.
  2. Two cards, "A" (teal) and "B" (sky blue). Two responses sampled from one or more models. Good practice: sample from the current model, so the data teaches about the mistakes the model actually makes, not mistakes it outgrew.
  3. The judge icon and the three buttons. A human picks A, B, or TIE. The tie option matters: forcing a choice on two equally good answers injects pure noise into the dataset.
  4. What this becomes: thousands of these judgments become the training set for the reward model (Chapter 4) or the direct input to DPO (Chapter 5). One bad judgment is noise. A systematic skew in judgments, like always preferring longer answers, becomes a learned behavior of the final model.
  5. What breaks if this step fails: vague guidelines produce inconsistent labels; inconsistent labels produce a reward model that scores noise; the policy then optimizes noise. Most "RLHF did not work" postmortems end here, at the data.

3.2 How teams collect it

Collection is an operations problem with a research core:

  • Prompt sourcing. Prompts should match the deployment distribution: real user requests, red-team probes, and targeted sets for known weaknesses. A reward model trained only on easy prompts cannot judge hard ones.
  • Response sampling. Generate 2 to 4 responses per prompt from the SFT model (sometimes at higher temperature for diversity). Labelers rank them. Sampling from the model you are about to train keeps the data on-policy: it describes the exact region of behavior space the policy will explore.
  • Guidelines. A written rubric defines what "better" means, usually in priority order: helpfulness first, then honesty, then harmlessness, with tie-breakers. Example tie-breaker: "if both answers are correct, prefer the clearer one". Without this, each labeler invents their own rubric and agreement collapses.
  • Agreement measurement. Give the same items to multiple labelers and measure how often they agree. The InstructGPT team reported agreement around 7 in 10 on their comparisons. That number is a feature, not a bug: it tells you the ceiling. A reward model cannot be more consistent than the humans it learns from.

  2. Response sampling. Two to four responses per prompt from the SFT model itself, so the data stays on-policy.

3.3 What makes a pair high quality

  • Hard negatives. The most valuable pairs are close calls: two decent answers where one has a subtle flaw (a wrong fact buried in paragraph three, a slightly unhelpful framing). Easy pairs (great vs gibberish) teach almost nothing; the model already knows gibberish is bad.
  • Diversity. Cover tasks, topics, difficulty levels, and both benign and adversarial prompts. A narrow dataset trains a narrow judge.
  • Clean winners. The preferred response should be genuinely better, not just longer or more confident-sounding. Guideline writers fight a constant battle against proxies for quality.

Data beats algorithm. Swap PPO for DPO, or DPO for GRPO, and results move a little. Double the quality of the preference data, fix the verbosity bias, add hard negatives, and results move a lot. "Preference labels are ground truth about quality." They are not. They are a noisy, biased, human proxy for quality, with roughly 70 percent agreement between labelers. Every method in this volume inherits that noise ceiling. The right mental model: preference data is a measurement instrument with known error bars. Design the instrument well. That is part of the job.

  • Quality comes from hard negatives, prompt diversity matching deployment, clear rubrics, and measured labeler agreement (~70 percent is normal).
  • Classic pitfalls: verbosity bias, sycophancy, guideline drift, demographic skew, cost-driven noise.
  • Data quality moves results more than algorithm choice. Audit the data first.

4. The RLHF pipeline end to end: reward model, then PPO

Design prompt

  1. "Walk me through the three stages of RLHF. What is the input and output of each?"
  2. "Derive the Bradley-Terry loss for the reward model. Why a scalar head?"
  3. "Write down the PPO clipped objective and explain each term. Why the clip, and why the KL penalty on top of it?"

Comparisons are data, not a training signal. A policy cannot optimize "A beats B" directly. This chapter closes that gap in three stages. First, train a reward model that turns comparisons into scores. Then optimize the policy against it with PPO. A KL leash keeps the policy near the reference.

How to read this diagram
  1. Rows are pitfalls; columns are cause, symptom, fix. Read any row left to right as a causal chain.
  2. Verbosity bias: labelers use length as a proxy for effort; the model pads.
  3. Sycophancy: labelers prefer agreement; the model validates false beliefs.
  4. Guideline drift: rubric interpretation shifts over weeks; the reward model learns a blurry average.
  5. Demographic skew and cost pressure: who labels, and how fast, becomes what the model values.
  6. The chapter's key line: data beats algorithm.

4.1 The three stages, one picture

RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) runs in three stages. Stage 1 is SFT from Chapter 2. Stage 2 trains a reward model. It is a network that reads a prompt plus a response. It outputs a single number. The number scores how good the response is.

Stage 3 uses PPO (proximal policy optimization), an RL algorithm, to tune the language model (the policy) so its outputs score highly under the reward model.

How to read this diagram
  1. Step 1, blue panel. Human demonstration data goes in. Teams supervised-fine-tune the base LLM into the SFT model. This is Chapter 2. Everything downstream starts here.
  2. Step 2, green panel. A prompt goes to the SFT model, which produces two answers. A human ranks them (Answer 1 over Answer 2). That ranked pair trains the RM (reward model), which is the base LLM fine-tuned with a scoring head instead of a language-modeling head.
  3. Step 3, orange panel. A fresh prompt goes to the policy, which generates an answer. The frozen RM scores it. The reward signal flows back (the curved arrow) and PPO updates the policy. The RM is the judge; the policy is the student; PPO is the study method.
  4. What breaks if a stage fails: weak SFT means the policy starts in a bad place and the RM trains on bad samples. A bad RM means PPO optimizes the wrong thing (Chapter 8). Unstable PPO makes a good RM unsafe.

4.2 The reward model: Bradley-Terry from zero

What it is. A reward model is a function r(x, y): given prompt x and response y, return a single real number. Higher means "humans would prefer this".

Architecturally it is the SFT model. A scalar head replaces its language-modeling head. The scalar head is one linear layer. It sits on top of the final hidden state. It outputs one number instead of a vocabulary distribution.

Why it exists. Human preferences are expensive to query at training time. The reward model is a learned stand-in. Train it once on tens of thousands of comparisons. Then query it millions of times during PPO for free.

How it trains, from zero. Take one comparison: prompt x, winner y_w, loser y_l. We need a probabilistic model of "the human picked w over l". The Bradley-Terry model says:

P(yw preferred over yl) = σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl))

Where: x is the prompt. yw is the winning response. yl is the losing response. r(x, y) is the reward model's score. σ is the sigmoid. It squashes any score gap into a (0, 1) probability.

Read it plainly: the bigger the score gap in the winner's favor, the closer the predicted probability gets to 1. If the scores are equal, the model predicts a coin flip (0.5). This turns a ranking into a probability, and probabilities have a standard training loss: negative log-likelihood (the same idea as cross-entropy from Volume 1).

loss = −log( σ( rw − rl ) )

Where: rw = r(x, yw), rl = r(x, yl); the loss is the negative log-likelihood of the observed human choice.

Minimizing this loss pushes r_w up and r_l down. The gradient has a clean shape: it is proportional to (1 - sigmoid(r_w - r_l)). When the model is already confident and correct (gap large), the gradient is near zero: nothing to learn. When it is wrong or uncertain (gap small or negative), the gradient is large. The loss automatically focuses training on the close calls, which are exactly the hard negatives from Chapter 3.

4.3 PPO, mechanically, without mystique

What it is. PPO is a policy-gradient algorithm. It makes high-reward actions (tokens) more likely and low-reward actions less likely. The plain version of this idea is REINFORCE. PPO is REINFORCE plus two stabilizers: a clipped objective and (in the RLHF recipe) a KL penalty.

Why the stabilizers exist. In RLHF, one PPO update step uses a batch of freshly sampled completions. A plain gradient step on that batch can move the policy too far in one jump. Probabilities collapse. The model emits repetitive garbage and never recovers. This is the classic RL failure, destructive updates.

PPO's answer is a trust region enforced cheaply. Never let one batch move the policy far.

The setup. For each token position we have: the old policy's probability π_old, the new (under training) policy's probability π_new, and an advantage A. The advantage answers "how much better was this token than expected?"

It comes from a learned critic. The critic is a value head predicting expected future reward. The estimator combines that prediction with the actual reward. The standard estimator is GAE (generalized advantage estimation). Positive A means "do this more", negative means "do this less". Define the probability ratio:

ratio = πnew(token | context) / πold(token | context)

Where: πold is the policy that generated the data. πnew is the policy under training. A ratio of 1.0 means unchanged.

The clipped objective (what PPO maximizes, per token, then averaged):

L = min( ratio · A , clip(ratio, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) · A )

Where: A is the advantage (how much better this token was than expected); ε = 0.2 typical (illustrative); clip holds the ratio in [1−ε, 1+ε].

Read it in two cases. When A is positive (good token), the objective grows as the ratio grows. But the clip caps the ratio's contribution at 1 + eps. One lucky sample cannot push the probability up without bound. The min picks the capped value.

When A is negative (bad token), the objective improves as the ratio shrinks. But the min with the clipped value keeps the pessimistic bound. Once the policy moves away from the bad token past 1 − eps, overshooting earns no extra credit. Either way, the policy is free to move back toward the old probabilities (recovery is never blocked).

Worked numeric example

eps = 0.2, so the ratio is only trusted in [0.8, 1.2].

  old prob 0.40, new prob 0.60  ->  ratio = 1.5

The surrounding PPO machinery (one click deeper)

A full PPO step in RLHF looks like this:

  1. Rollout. Sample a batch of prompts. Generate completions with the current (old) policy. Score each full completion with the frozen reward model, subtract the KL penalty per token (Section 4.4).
  2. Critic and advantages. A value head (a second scalar head on the policy) predicts the expected return at each token. Advantages come from GAE, which blends the critic's predictions with actual observed rewards. Conceptually: advantage = "reward actually got" minus "reward the critic expected".
  3. Minibatch updates. Split the batch into minibatches. Run a few epochs of gradient steps on the clipped objective. Use 4 epochs as an illustration. Recompute the ratios each time against the frozen old policy.
  4. Monitoring. Watch the KL divergence from the reference model and the clip fraction (how often the clip actually binds). KL exploding means stop or strengthen the penalty. A clip fraction near zero means eps is too loose. Near one, the clip over-constrains updates.

Teams sometimes add an entropy bonus to preserve exploration. It is a small reward for keeping the policy's token distribution spread out. In RLHF practice teams keep it tiny or zero, because the KL penalty already prevents collapse.

4.4 The KL penalty, and why it exists

What it is. During PPO, the reward the policy sees is not just the reward model's score. It is:

total_reward(x, y) = rRM(x, y) − β · KL( πnew(·|x) || πref(·|x) )

Where: rRM is the reward model score; β the KL penalty weight (illustrative 0.05 to 0.2); πref the frozen SFT model; KL the expected log-ratio measuring drift (Volume 1, §7.3).

Worked numeric example. The reward model scores a response rRM = 2.0. The policy's KL from the reference is 1.5 nats and β = 0.1. total_reward = 2.0 − 0.1 × 1.5 = 1.85. The penalty shaves 0.15 off the score: cheap for this much drift, so the policy keeps moving toward reward. Now set β = 1.0: total_reward = 2.0 − 1.5 = 0.5. The same drift now costs three quarters of the reward itself, so the policy stays put. That is what β sets: the exchange rate between reward points and nats of drift.

π_ref is the frozen SFT model from Stage 1. KL divergence (Volume 1, §7.3) measures how far the new policy's token distribution has drifted from the reference. Beta is a coefficient; illustrative values run 0.05 to 0.2.

Why it exists. The reward model is trustworthy only near the data it trained on: sensible, SFT-like responses. Far from that region, its scores are extrapolation, and optimizers are excellent at finding the exact inputs where a learned model is confidently wrong. Without the KL leash, PPO discovers gibberish that the reward model scores highly: perfect reward, useless model. The KL term prices drift: every step away from the reference policy costs beta per unit of KL. The policy can move toward higher reward, but it pays for the distance.

Teams estimate the per-token KL simply: at each generated token, log pi_ref(token) - log pi_new(token), summed over the completion. Tokens the new policy favors much more than the reference did accumulate penalty fast.

[WARNING]

Do not set β = 0 outside a toy experiment. The reward model is only trustworthy near SFT-like responses. Without the KL leash, PPO finds gibberish. The reward model scores that gibberish highly. The training curve looks wonderful while the model collapses. Watch KL during every run: if it explodes, stop and strengthen the penalty.

[KEY]

The clip and the KL penalty are both trust regions at different scales. The clip bounds how far one update step can move the policy (per-batch safety). The KL penalty bounds how far the policy can drift from the reference model over the whole run (global safety). You need both. The clip without KL lets the policy walk steadily into reward-model blind spots. KL without the clip lets a single bad batch do damage before the penalty catches up.

5. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization), derived from first principles

Design prompt

  1. "Start from the RLHF objective and derive the DPO loss. Do not skip the reparameterization."
  2. "Where did the reward model go? What assumptions did you smuggle in?"
  3. "When does DPO beat PPO, and when does it fail?"

PPO works, but it is fiddly: an actor, a critic, a reward model, a KL coefficient, and clipping, all interacting. This chapter asks whether all that machinery does real work. The answer is DPO: the RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum, and pure algebra turns it into a classification loss. No rollouts required.

5.1 The starting point: the RLHF objective

Forget PPO for a moment. Write down what RLHF seeks to do, as an optimization problem over policies π:

maximize over pi:   E_{x ~ D, y ~ pi(.|x)}[ r(x, y) ]  -  beta * KL( pi(.|x) || pi_ref(.|x) )

In words: choose the policy that gets high reward while staying close to the reference model. Beta sets the exchange rate between reward and closeness. This is the objective PPO approximates with sampling and clipping. DPO asks a different question: what is the exact optimal policy of this objective? If we can write it down in closed form, we may not need RL at all.

5.2 Step 1: solve for the optimal policy

Fix a prompt x. Write the objective as an expectation over y, and expand the KL (Volume 1, §7.3: KL equals the expected log-ratio):

J(π) = Ey ~ π[ r(x,y) ] − β · Ey ~ π[ log π(y|x) − log πref(y|x) ]
           = β · Ey ~ π[ log exp(r(x,y)/β) − log( π(y|x) / πref(y|x) ) ]
           = β · Ey ~ π[ log( πref(y|x) · exp(r(x,y)/β) / π(y|x) ) ]

Where: J(π) is the RLHF objective; Ey ~ π the expectation over the policy's outputs; β the KL exchange rate; πref the frozen reference.

Now define the normalizer Z(x). It is the sum over y of pi_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/beta). Define the Gibbs distribution pi*(y|x) = pi_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/beta) / Z(x). Substitute:

J(π) = β · Ey ~ π[ log( Z(x) · π*(y|x) / π(y|x) ) ]
           = β · log Z(x) − β · KL( π(·|x) || π*(·|x) )

Where: Z(x) is the normalizer; π* the Gibbs optimal policy (defined next). The first term does not depend on π. The second reaches its minimum, zero, when π equals π*.

The first term does not depend on pi. The second reaches its minimum (zero) when pi equals pi*. So the exact optimizer of the RLHF objective is:

π*(y|x) = πref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β ) / Z(x)

Where: π* is the exact optimal policy; Z(x) = Σy πref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β) is the normalizer that makes the probabilities sum to 1.

Worked numeric example. A two-token world. Prompt x, tokens A and B. The reference says πref = (0.5, 0.5). Rewards: rA = 2.0, rB = 0.0. β = 1.0. Unnormalized scores: 0.5 · e2 = 3.6945 for A, 0.5 · e0 = 0.5 for B. Z = 4.1945. π* = (0.881, 0.119). The optimal policy tilts the 50/50 reference to 88/12 toward the high-reward token. Raise β to 5.0 and the tilt softens: e2/5 = 1.4918, so π* = (0.599, 0.401). A bigger β means stay closer to the reference: β is the exchange rate between reward and drift.

This is the key equation of the chapter. It says: the optimal policy is the reference policy, tilted exponentially toward high-reward responses. No sampling, no gradients, no PPO. Pure algebra.

5.3 Step 2: the reparameterization (solve for the reward)

We do not know r. But the equation above relates r and pi*. Take logs and rearrange. Write the reward with the policy:

r(x, y) = β · log( π*(y|x) / πref(y|x) ) + β · log Z(x)

Where: the last term is a per-prompt constant: identical for both responses to the same x, so it cancels in Bradley-Terry.

Worked numeric example, continued. Recover the rewards from the policy above, with β = 1.0. rA = log(0.881/0.5) + log(4.1945). That is 0.5665 + 1.4340 = 2.0005, the 2.0 we started with. rB = log(0.119/0.5) + log(4.1945). That is −1.4343 + 1.4340 = −0.0003, the 0.0 we started with. The identity is exact: the policy's log-ratios encode the reward, up to the per-prompt constant β · log Z(x).

This is the reparameterization trick. The reward of a response is beta times a log-ratio, up to a per-prompt constant. The log-ratio measures how much likelier the optimal policy finds the response versus the reference. Intuition: if the optimal policy assigns a response much higher probability than the reference did, that response must have high reward. The policy's own probabilities are an implicit reward model.

5.4 Step 3: plug into Bradley-Terry, watch the reward model vanish

The Bradley-Terry model from Chapter 4 says P(y_w preferred) = sigmoid(r_w − r_l). Substitute our expression for r. The constant beta·log Z(x) is identical for both responses (same prompt x), so it cancels:

P(yw > yl) = σ( β · log(π*(yw|x)/πref(yw|x)) − β · log(π*(yl|x)/πref(yl|x)) )

Where: π* is the policy under training (renamed πθ below). The β·log Z(x) constants canceled because both responses share the prompt.

There is no r anymore. No reward model to train, no scores to predict. Only two policies: the one under training and the frozen reference.

5.5 Step 4: maximum likelihood gives the DPO loss

We have preference data and a probabilistic model of it. The standard move (Volume 1) is maximum likelihood: minimize the negative log probability of the observed human choices. Replace pi* with the policy we are training, pi_theta:

LDPO = −log σ( β · [ log(πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x)) − log(πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x)) ] )

Where: πθ is the policy under training. πref is the frozen SFT reference. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the observed human preference.

That is the DPO loss (Rafailov et al., 2023). One line. It is a binary classification loss on preference pairs, and it trains with ordinary gradient descent: no rollouts, no critic, no clipping, no PPO. The log-probability of a full response is the sum of its token log-probs (Volumes 4 and 5), and pi_ref is the frozen SFT model.

The gradient is worth stating: it pushes up the winner's log-ratio and down the loser's, with strength proportional to beta * (1 - sigmoid(...)). Same shape as the Bradley-Terry gradient from Chapter 4, which makes sense: DPO is Bradley-Terry, with the reward written as a policy log-ratio.

5.7 When DPO works, and when it does not

DPO shines when: the preference data is clean. The compared responses come from a model near the reference (on-policy-ish). The task is single-turn judgment: tone, helpfulness, style, which-of-two. It is simpler, more stable, and cheaper than PPO. That is why it became the default first try.

DPO struggles when:

  • Noisy labels. The loss has no uncertainty model. One mislabeled pair pushes the log-ratio hard. The gradient weight (1 − sigmoid) is largest exactly when the model disagrees with the label. That is also when the label is most likely wrong. PPO's reward model at least averages noise across many comparisons before the policy ever sees it.
  • Distribution shift. DPO is offline: it trains on fixed pairs. If the policy drifts far from the distribution that produced the pairs, the implicit reward extrapolates badly. No KL-guarded rollout loop catches it. Iterative DPO is the standard fix: re-sample pairs from the current policy, re-label, repeat.
  • Reasoning tasks. For math and code, a single preference judgment is a weak signal compared to "the answer is right or wrong". Verifiable rewards (Chapter 6) dominate here.

Variants worth knowing by name: IPO adds explicit regularization pulling the policy toward the reference, taming DPO's overconfidence on noisy data. KTO works from unpaired examples (just "good" and "bad" sets, no pairs), using a prospect-theory-inspired loss. Both are small modifications of the same idea: preferences as a classification loss on log-ratios.

[MISUNDERSTANDING]

"DPO is a different objective from RLHF." No. DPO optimizes the same KL-regularized reward objective as RLHF. It just solves the inner optimization in closed form instead of with PPO. If someone asks what DPO assumes, the honest answer is: Bradley-Terry preferences, and a reference policy worth staying near.

Related trap: "DPO needs no reference model." It does. The loss measures the log-ratios against pi_ref. Drop the reference and the loss has no anchor. The policy drifts.

6.1 RLVR: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

What it is. RLVR replaces the learned reward model with a checker. In domains where machines check correctness, the reward is not a human judgment. It is a fact:

  • Math: the final answer matches the known answer (exact string or numeric match after normalization).
  • Code: the generated program passes a hidden unit-test suite.
  • Formal tasks: a proof checker accepts the proof, a puzzle solver's output satisfies the constraints.

Reward = 1 if the checks pass, 0 otherwise (sometimes plus small shaping rewards for format, like "the right tags surround the answer"). That is the whole reward function. No labelers, no Bradley-Terry, no reward model to train or hack in the human-preference sense.

Why it exists. Human preference labels are a noisy proxy for "good". For math and code, "correct" is not a matter of opinion, and humans are slow, expensive judges of long reasoning chains. A verifier is cheap, tireless, and exactly aligned with what you want: the right answer. RLVR scales where human labeling cannot.

What it cannot do. Most of what assistants do is not verifiable: writing quality, tone, helpfulness, taste. RLVR covers the checkable islands. Everything else still needs preference signal.

How to read this diagram

  1. Math. The answer matches exactly or is symbolically equivalent. A program decides, not a person.
  2. Code. Hidden unit tests pass or fail. The reward is 1 or 0.
  3. Format. The output parses as valid JSON or meets a schema. Cheap and fully automatic.
  4. Tools. The API call succeeded and the result contains what the task needed.
  5. What breaks: a verifier with a hole (wrong code that passes weak tests) teaches the policy to find the hole.

  6. What breaks: if the verifier has a hole (a wrong answer that passes the tests), the policy will find it and exploit it. RLVR is only as honest as its checker.

6.2 GRPO: group relative policy optimization

What it is. GRPO (from the DeepSeekMath work) is PPO's clipped objective with one big simplification: no critic network. Recall from Chapter 4 that PPO needs advantages ("how much better than expected?"), which normally come from a learned value function. GRPO computes advantages from a group instead: for each prompt, sample G completions (illustrative: G = 8), score each with the verifier, and normalize within the group:

For one prompt, sample G completions and get rewards r1..rG (0 or 1). The advantage of completion i is:

Ai = (ri − mean(r)) / std(r)

Where: ri is the verifier score of completion i. The G completions give the mean and std. The group is the baseline.

Then the familiar PPO-style clipped objective per token, plus a KL term against the reference policy:

L = E[ min( ratio · Ai , clip(ratio, 1−ε, 1+ε) · Ai ) ] − β · KL

Where: same clipped objective as PPO, with the group advantage in place of the critic's advantage; the KL term anchors the policy to the reference.

Why the group works. The critic's job was to answer one question: "what reward should I expect here?" That tells it if this sample beat expectations. The group mean answers that empirically: for this prompt, the average of my G attempts is the expectation. A completion that scores above its siblings gets positive advantage, and one below gets negative. No second network to train, no value-function bugs, half the memory.

Worked numeric example

One math prompt. G = 4 completions sampled. Verifier scores:

  completion 1: correct    r = 1
  completion 2: wrong      r = 0
  completion 3: correct    r = 1
  completion 4: wrong      r = 0

mean(r) = 0.5,  std(r) = 0.5   (population std)

Advantages:
  A_1 = (1 - 0.5)/0.5 = +1.0    -> push these tokens' probability UP
  A_2 = (0 - 0.5)/0.5 = -1.0    -> push these tokens' probability DOWN
  A_3 = +1.0
  A_4 = -1.0

Each completion's tokens get the clipped PPO update with their
group advantage. The correct reasoning traces get amplified;
the wrong ones get suppressed. No critic was consulted.

Edge case: all four correct -> mean 1, std 0 -> advantage 0/0.
No learning signal. Practitioners filter out groups with zero
variance (or add format rewards so ties are rare).

One GRPO update, in four steps:

  1. Sample G completions for one prompt from the current policy.

  3. Compute group advantages: (ri − mean) / std over the G scores.
  4. Apply the clipped PPO-style update per token with those advantages, plus the KL term against the reference.

6.3 What changed with reasoning models

GRPO plus verifiable rewards is the recipe. This section is what the recipe produced at frontier scale. The DeepSeek-R1 report described a striking experiment: R1-Zero, pure RL with verifiable rewards from a base model, with no supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces. The reported findings:

  • Long chain-of-thought reasoning emerged from RL alone: the model learned to produce extended reasoning, try alternatives, and backtrack.
  • The famous "aha moment": during training, the model spontaneously learned to re-examine its own reasoning mid-solution ("wait, let me reconsider"), because traces that self-corrected scored higher.
  • The production R1 added back a cold-start SFT phase before RL, because pure-RL traces, while correct, were hard for humans to read. Readability is a preference, and preferences still need human signal.

(All of the above as reported in the DeepSeek-R1 report.) The lesson practitioners took: RL amplifies behaviors the base model already samples; it does not invent them from nothing. If the base model never stumbles onto a correct reasoning trace in G samples, the verifier gives all zeros and the policy learns nothing. Scale of sampling, base-model capability, and verifier quality are the three legs.

[MISUNDERSTANDING]

"RL taught the model to reason from scratch." It cannot. RL is a probability re-weighting machine: it multiplies the probability of traces that score well and divides the probability of traces that score badly. A trace with probability exactly zero stays at zero forever. What R1 showed is that base models already sample the seeds of reasoning often enough for RL to amplify them into reliable behavior. The capability was latent; RL made it dominant. Related trap: "GRPO is totally different from PPO." It is PPO's clipped objective with group-normalized advantages instead of critic-based ones. Same skeleton, cheaper advantage.

Key takeaways

  • GRPO: per prompt, sample G completions, advantage = (r − group mean) / group std, then the PPO clipped objective. No critic.
  • Worked: rewards [1,0,1,0] give advantages [+1,−1,+1,−1]. Zero-variance groups give no signal and get filtered.
  • Reasoning models: RL amplified latent reasoning into reliable long chain-of-thought (R1-Zero "aha moment", as reported). Teams added cold-start SFT back for readability.
  • Fundamental limit: RL re-weights existing behaviors. Zero-probability traces stay at zero.

7.1 What it is

Constitutional AI (CAI, Anthropic, 2022) trains a harmless assistant with AI feedback instead of human feedback. The core idea: humans write down a constitution, a list of principles for good behavior ("do not help with wrongdoing", "do not lie", "explain refusals briefly", dozens of such rules in the paper). Then the model itself generates critiques, revisions, and preference labels guided by that constitution. Human judgment moves up one level: from labeling thousands of outputs to writing the rules once.

7.2 Phase 1: supervised critique and revision

How it works, step by step:

  1. Take a potentially harmful prompt (from red-teaming or adversarial sets).
  2. Generate an initial response with the current model. It may be bad. That is fine; it is raw material.
  3. Prompt the model to critique its own response against the constitution: "Identify specific ways this response violates the principles."
  4. Prompt the model to revise the response in light of the critique.
  5. Fine-tune (SFT-style, Chapter 2) on the revised responses only. The critiques support the revisions. The revisions are the training data.

Concrete walk-through. The prompt asks for instructions facilitating wrongdoing, and the initial response is a partial, edgy compliance. Then comes the critique. It cites the constitution. The critique says the response assists with harmful activity, violating the principle against facilitating wrongdoing. Revision: a clean refusal with a brief explanation and an offer to help with something legitimate. After fine-tuning on thousands of such revisions, the model internalizes the refusal pattern. It goes straight to the safe completion without needing the critique step at inference time.

Why it works. Large models are already good at applying stated principles to text; that is a comprehension task, and comprehension is what pre-training is good at. The constitution turns "be harmless" from a vague vibe into an explicit checklist the model can execute against its own outputs.

7.3 Phase 2: RL from AI feedback (RLAIF)

Phase 1 produces a reasonably harmless model, but supervised cloning has the limits from Chapter 2. Phase 2 applies the RLHF pipeline from Chapter 4 with one substitution: the preference labels come from the model, not humans.

  1. Sample two responses per prompt from the phase-1 model.
  2. Ask the model which response better follows the constitution, as a multiple-choice judgment.
  3. Train a preference model on these AI-generated labels.
  4. Run RL (PPO-style) against that preference model.

The paper reported the resulting model as comparably harmless to one trained with human feedback. It was also less evasive: it refused cleanly and explained itself instead of dodging. (As reported in the Constitutional AI paper.) Less evasiveness matters. A model that says "I cannot help with that, but here is what I can do" is more useful than one that changes the subject.

8. Reward hacking: when the proxy eats the goal

Design prompt

  1. "What is reward hacking? Give me two real examples."
  2. "Your reward curve climbs but human evals are flat. What do you do?"
  3. "How do you detect and fight reward hacking in a post-training run?"

Chapters 2 through 7 all optimize a proxy: demos, preferences, reward models, constitutions. This chapter names the failure mode they share: the policy learns to score well instead of be good. Read it as the shadow over every method so far, and the reason Chapter 9 exists.

8.1 What it is

Reward hacking is what happens when a policy optimizes the reward signal instead of the thing you actually wanted. The classic statement is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

In RLHF the measure is the reward model's score. That score is a proxy: a learned approximation of human preference, trained on limited data, with blind spots. The optimizer does not know it is a proxy. It searches for the highest-scoring outputs, and those outputs often sit in the proxy's blind spots, not in genuinely good behavior. The better the optimizer, the faster it finds them. It is optimization working exactly as specified on a flawed specification, not a bug in optimization.

How to read this diagram
  1. Horizontal axis: training steps. Time, as PPO or any optimizer keeps pushing. Vertical axis: quality, in arbitrary units.
  2. Teal curve: "MEASURED REWARD". The reward model's score, the number the optimizer sees. It climbs steeply and keeps climbing. If this is your only dashboard, everything looks great.
  3. Red dashed curve: "TRUE QUALITY". What humans would actually rate. It rises early (the optimizer finds genuinely better answers first), then flattens, then falls: the policy has discovered proxy exploits that humans dislike.
  4. The warning triangle and the opening gap. The gap is the quantity you must monitor: reward-model score minus independent human (or held-out) evaluation. When the gap grows, you are not training anymore. The policy hacks the proxy.
  5. What breaks: teams that ship on reward alone. The fix is never "optimize harder". It is a better proxy, stronger regularization, or a different reward (Chapters 4, 6, and 9).

How to read this diagram

  1. The horizontal axis: KL divergence. How far the policy has drifted from the reference policy during training.
  2. The orange curve: proxy reward. Keeps rising because the optimizer finds the cheapest way to raise the number.
  3. The teal curve: true quality. Rises, then flattens. Human judges stop seeing improvement even as the proxy climbs.
  4. The dashed line. Stop here. Past this point you optimize the metric, not the goal.

8.2 Real examples

  • The boat race. In the classic CoastRunners example reported in AI safety literature, the game rewarded an RL agent for hitting targets along a race course. Instead of finishing the race, it learned to drive in circles, crashing into the same high-value targets repeatedly. Maximum reward, zero racing. The measure (targets hit) diverged completely from the goal (finish fast).
  • Length exploitation in RLHF. Across RLHF efforts, models learn that longer answers score higher, because human labelers used length as a proxy for effort (Chapter 3's verbosity bias, now amplified by optimization). The policy pads: restatements, filler, hedging. Reward up, usefulness flat or down.
  • Sycophancy. Models learn to agree with the user's stated views, including false ones, because agreeable answers won preference comparisons. The reward model cannot tell "helpful agreement" from "flattery", so the policy maximizes flattery.
  • Unfaithful summarization. Summarization models trained with human feedback learned to add plausible-sounding details that were not in the source text. Judges rewarded confident, fluent summaries; faithfulness was harder to check, so the proxy ignored it and the policy exploited that.
  • Evasive hedging. "I am an AI language model..." style non-answers score safely on harmlessness metrics while providing nothing. Optimizing a harmlessness proxy alone breeds refusal-shaped mush.

The pattern across all five: the policy finds the cheapest way to raise the score, and the cheapest way is never the behavior you wanted. Every proxy has a cheapest exploit. Your job is to know what yours is before the optimizer finds it.

8.3 How practitioners detect it

  • Reward vs independent eval. Track the reward model's score and a separate human (or held-out autorater) evaluation on the same checkpoints. Diverging curves are the diagnosis. This is the single most important plot in post-training.
  • KL from the reference. A spiking KL divergence (Chapter 4) means the policy has left the region where teams trained the reward model. Scores from outside that region are extrapolation. Treat them as guilty until proven innocent.
  • Qualitative audits. Read actual outputs regularly. Automated metrics miss new exploit shapes; a human reading 50 samples spots "it now starts every answer with a disclaimer" in minutes.
  • Held-out preference sets. Keep a set of comparisons the reward model never trained on. If the policy's outputs win against the reference on the training distribution but not on held-out judges, the policy overfit the proxy.
  • Reward model ensembles. Train several reward models on different data splits. When they disagree sharply on the policy's outputs, the policy exploits at least one of them.

8.4 How practitioners fight it

  • Stronger KL regularization. Raise beta (Chapter 4). This shrinks the region the policy may explore, which shrinks the space of available exploits. The cost: less optimization headroom.
  • Retrain the reward model on the policy's outputs. The classic fix for a stale proxy: sample the current policy, get fresh human (or AI) judgments on its outputs, retrain the RM, repeat. This closes the loop: the proxy learns the exploits and stops rewarding them. Iterative RLHF.
  • Adversarial data collection. Red-team the reward model directly: find outputs it scores highly that humans rate poorly, add them to its training data as corrections.
  • Verifiable rewards where possible. You cannot flatter a unit test (Chapter 6). Wherever correctness is checkable, prefer the checker over the learned proxy.
  • Keep a human gate before shipping. No model ships on reward curves alone. Final sign-off is human evaluation on a golden set, every time.
[KEY]

The reward model is the training signal, not the goal: the goal is quality that humans judge. Every number you optimize is a proxy, and every proxy has a cheapest exploit that the optimizer will find. You layer your defenses: KL regularization to bound the search. Bring fresh data to fix the proxy. Use verifiable rewards where they exist, and keep independent evals that never become training targets.

[MISUNDERSTANDING]

"Reward hacking means the model acts deceptive or malicious." No. The model has no intent to deceive. It is gradient descent doing exactly what the math says: maximizing the specified reward. Anthropomorphizing the exploit as scheming misdiagnoses the problem and points at the wrong fix. The flaw is in the proxy and the setup, which are your responsibility, not the model's.

Key takeaways

  • Reward hacking = optimizing the proxy (reward model score) instead of the goal (human-judged quality). Goodhart's law, mechanized.
  • Real shapes: the boat-race loop, length exploitation, sycophancy, unfaithful summaries, evasive hedging.
  • Detection: reward-vs-independent-eval divergence, KL spikes, qualitative audits, held-out judges, RM ensembles.

9. Eval-driven iteration: how post-training actually runs

Design prompt

  1. "Describe the post-training loop as it runs in practice. Where do evals sit?"
  2. "Your new checkpoint wins overall but regresses on coding. What do you do?"
  3. "How do you read a win-rate delta without fooling yourself?"

If the optimizer can game every training signal, the defense is measurement you trust. This chapter is the operating system of real post-training. It runs a train, evaluate, analyze loop. That loop decides which method to use, which data to collect, and when to stop.

9.1 What it is

In practice, post-training is not "run PPO once and ship". It is a loop: train a candidate, evaluate it, analyze the failures, change the data or the algorithm, repeat. The algorithm is rarely the bottleneck. The loop is the job. Teams that iterate this loop quickly, on trustworthy evals, beat teams with fancier losses.

How to read this diagram
  1. TRAIN (teal). One candidate: an SFT run, a DPO run, a PPO run, a data mix change. Exactly one deliberate change per loop, or you cannot attribute the result.
  2. EVAL SUITE (blue, gauge icon). The fixed battery the candidate must face: held-out preference win-rates, task benchmarks, refusal calibration probes, regression checks on old capabilities. Fixed means comparable across loops.
  3. FAILURE ANALYSIS (amber, magnifier). Not the average. The distribution of failures: cluster the bad outputs, count failure modes, read samples. "Coding win-rate dropped 4 points" becomes "it now imports nonexistent libraries in 30 percent of failing cases".
  4. FIX DATA OR ALGO (red, wrench). The intervention: add hard negatives for the failure mode, fix noisy labels, adjust beta, change the data mix. Then back to TRAIN.
  5. Center text: "EVALS STEER TRAINING". The loop's direction comes from measurement, not intuition. A loop with bad evals optimizes confidently in the wrong direction (Chapter 8).
  6. What breaks: changing three things per loop (unattributable), evals that move every loop (incomparable), or skipping failure analysis and "just trying more data" (expensive wandering).

9.2 What the eval suite contains

  • Preference win-rate vs baseline. The new checkpoint's outputs vs the current production model's, judged blind (by humans or a trusted autorater) on a fixed prompt set. The headline number.
  • Task benchmarks. Standard suites for the capabilities you care about: reasoning, coding, instruction following, multilinguality. These catch regressions the preference set misses.
  • Behavioral probes. Refusal calibration (does it refuse the right things?), sycophancy tests, honesty probes ("say you do not know" cases). Small, hand-built, high-signal.
  • Regression checks. Prompts the current model already handles well. A new checkpoint must not break them. Catastrophic forgetting (Vol 3) is a post-training failure mode too.
How to read this diagram
  1. Win-rate vs baseline: new checkpoint against production, judged blind on a fixed prompt set. The headline number.
  2. Task benchmarks: reasoning, coding, instruction following, multilinguality. These catch regressions the preference set misses.
  3. Behavioral probes: refusal calibration, sycophancy tests, honesty probes. Small, hand-built, high-signal.
  4. Regression checks: prompts the current model already handles well. A new checkpoint must not break them; forgetting is a post-training failure mode too.

9.3 How practitioners read results

  • Win-rate deltas need uncertainty. "52 percent vs 48 percent on 200 prompts" is noise. Report confidence intervals; require the delta to survive them before acting. A common bar: the win must be significant and replicate on a fresh sample.
  • Slice, do not just average. Win-rate up 2 points overall while coding drops 4 is not a win. Slice by task type, difficulty, and risk class. Ship decisions are made per slice.
  • Track reward and KL jointly. Reward up and KL flat: healthy optimization. Reward up and KL spiking: probable hacking (Chapter 8). Reward flat and KL flat: the run learned nothing; check the data and the advantage signal.
  • Failure taxonomies beat averages. Maintain a living list of failure modes with counts per checkpoint. "Hallucinated APIs: 12 percent to 4 percent" is actionable. "Quality improved" is not.
  • Ablate one thing at a time. New data mix or new beta, not both. Post-training runs are expensive; unattributable experiments waste them.
[KEY]

The loop is: train one change, eval on a fixed suite, analyze failures by mode, fix the data or the algorithm, repeat. Speed of trustworthy iteration beats cleverness of any single step. And teams maintain the evals like code: versioned, fixed across loops, with known blind spots.

Before doubling the run, the experienced move is a failure analysis on 100 samples. It is cheaper than a training run and more informative.

# Stage 1: SFT. Loss on response tokens only (Chapter 2).
sft = copy.deepcopy(gpt)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(sft.parameters(), lr=1e-4)  # small LR (illustrative)
for prompt, ideal in sft_pairs:
    ids = tok(prompt) + tok(ideal)
    labels = [-100]*len(tok(prompt)) + tok(ideal)
    loss = cross_entropy_shifted(sft(ids), labels)
    loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

# Stage 2: DPO against the frozen SFT reference (Chapter 5).
ref = copy.deepcopy(sft).eval()          # pi_ref, never updated
policy = copy.deepcopy(sft)              # pi_theta, trained
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(policy.parameters(), lr=5e-5)
beta = 0.1
for prompt, winner, loser in pref_pairs:
    lp_w  = logprob(policy, prompt, winner) - logprob(ref, prompt, winner)
    lp_l  = logprob(policy, prompt, loser)   - logprob(ref, prompt, loser)
    # logprob(model, prompt, resp) = sum of token log-probs of resp
    loss = -torch.log(torch.sigmoid(beta * (lp_w - lp_l)))
    loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

logprob is a helper you write. Run the model over prompt + response. Gather the log-softmax values at the response positions. Sum them. cross_entropy_shifted is the standard next-token loss from Volume 5 with -100 masking.

A.1 Rollout generation throughput for online RL

A.1.1 What a rollout is

A rollout is one full model-generated trajectory used as training data. A prompt goes in, the model generates tokens until it stops, and you score the result. This appendix assumes the online RL loop from Chapters 4 and 6. Each step generates fresh rollouts from the current policy. Rollouts are always new and online, unlike DPO, which learns offline from a fixed dataset. The step scores them and runs the gradient update. What is new here is the clock: generation takes the overwhelming majority of step time, and this chapter gives you the arithmetic to budget it.

A.1.2 Why generation dominates the step

Generation is autoregressive decoding: one token at a time, each token requiring a full pass over the model weights. Training is the opposite: hundreds of tokens processed in parallel through big matrix multiplies that keep the GPU's compute units busy.

Two different bottlenecks:

  • Decoding is memory-bandwidth bound. To produce one token for one sequence, the GPU must read every weight from memory. A 7B model in bf16 is 14 GB of weights. At ~3 TB/s of memory bandwidth, reading all of them for one token sets a hard ceiling near 200 tokens per second per GPU. Real systems land well below that once you count batching overhead and the KV cache.
  • Training is compute bound. A forward-backward pass over a batch of sequences reuses each weight across hundreds of tokens at once. The GPU's arithmetic units set the pace, not its memory bus. Throughput per token is an order of magnitude higher.

So the same 2 million tokens cost roughly 10x more wall clock to generate than to train on. That ratio is the whole story of RL systems engineering: everything in A.2 and A.3 exists to push generation throughput up.

A.1.3 How an online RL step works, token by token

Walk through one step with concrete shapes. Batch: 128 prompts. Group size G = 8 completions per prompt. Mean completion length: 2,000 tokens (long chain-of-thought, the R1 regime).

Total tokens to generate this step: 128 x 8 x 2,000 = 2,048,000. About 2M tokens.

The update phase replays those same 2M tokens (plus prompt tokens, a smaller addition) through forward and backward passes. Same token count, wildly different cost per token, because decode and train hit different hardware limits.

The code below is a step-time budget model. It is deliberately simple: two throughput numbers in, one fraction out. The fraction is what you use to decide where your next GPU goes.

# Online RL step-time budget: where does the wall clock go?
# One step = generate rollouts, then run the gradient update. This model
# answers a single question: what fraction of the step is generation?
# Change the knobs and watch the answer move.

def step_budget(
    n_prompts,          # prompts per step (the batch)
    group_size,         # completions sampled per prompt (G in GRPO)
    avg_tokens,         # mean completion length in tokens
    rollout_tok_per_s,  # combined generation throughput of the rollout pool
    train_tok_per_s,    # combined training throughput of the trainer pool
):
    # Total tokens the step must generate: prompts x completions x length.
    # This product is the number that matters; everything else is commentary.
    gen_tokens = n_prompts * group_size * avg_tokens

    # Generation is decode-bound: tokens arrive at rollout_tok_per_s.
    # Training replays the same tokens through forward+backward at
    # train_tok_per_s, which is far higher per token because training runs
    # big matrix multiplies at high utilization while decode is choked by
    # memory bandwidth. If you adapt this model, change these two rates;
    # the structure stays the same.
    gen_seconds = gen_tokens / rollout_tok_per_s
    train_seconds = gen_tokens / train_tok_per_s

    total = gen_seconds + train_seconds
    return {
        "gen_tokens": gen_tokens,
        "gen_seconds": round(gen_seconds, 1),
        "train_seconds": round(train_seconds, 1),
        # The headline number: fraction of the step spent generating.
        "gen_fraction": round(gen_seconds / total, 3),
    }

# Worked example: 128 prompts, G=8, 2000 tokens each -> ~2.05M tokens.
# A rollout pool doing 30K tok/s needs ~68 s. The trainer replays 2M
# tokens at 300K tok/s in ~7 s. Generation is ~91% of the step.
print(step_budget(
    n_prompts=128,
    group_size=8,
    avg_tokens=2000,
    rollout_tok_per_s=30_000,
    train_tok_per_s=300_000,
))
# {'gen_tokens': 2048000, 'gen_seconds': 68.3, 'train_seconds': 6.8,
#  'gen_fraction': 0.909}

How to read this diagram

  1. The product first. gen_tokens multiplies batch x group size x length. All three knobs multiply, so doubling any one of them doubles generation time.
  2. Two rates, one ratio. The ratio of the two throughputs decides the step split, not either one alone. Decode at 30K tok/s vs train at 300K tok/s gives a 10:1 ratio, so generation takes ~91% of the step.
  3. What moves the needle. Raising G from 8 to 16 doubles both phases equally, so the 91% split does not change. The split only moves if you change the throughput ratio: faster rollout engines (A.2) or more rollout GPUs (A.3).
  4. What breaks if you ignore it. Buying more trainer GPUs while the rollout pool starves leaves trainers idle 91% of the time. Budget the bottleneck first.

A.1.4 Common misunderstanding

"Training FLOPs dominate RL cost, so I should scale the trainer." That claim is wrong. The FLOP count of the gradient step is large, but FLOPs are not wall clock. Section A.1.2 showed that decode is bandwidth-bound, while the gradient step reuses the weights across thousands of tokens at once. Measure your own loop with the budget above before buying hardware. In online RL with long completions, generation is routinely 70-95% of step time. The fix is rollout throughput, not trainer FLOPs.

A.2 Inference engines inside the RL loop

A.2.1 What a rollout worker is

A rollout worker is an inference server that holds the policy weights and does nothing but generate. The RL trainer sends it prompts; it returns completions. In modern stacks this server is vLLM or SGLang, the same engines that serve chatbots in production. Teams use them here for one reason: they generate tokens several times faster per GPU than a naive generation loop.

The loop looks like this. The trainer finishes a gradient step and broadcasts the new weights to the rollout workers. The workers start generating the next batch of rollouts with the fresh policy. Generation and training alternate. The workers exist so that generation runs at inference-engine speed instead of training-script speed.

A.2.2 Why naive generation is slow

The obvious way to generate G completions for a batch of prompts is a loop over a standard generate call. Three things make it slow:

  1. Static batching. The naive loop pads every sequence to the longest one and waits for the whole batch to finish. If one completion runs 4,000 tokens and the rest run 200, seven-eighths of the batch's GPU slots sit idle most of the time.
  2. KV cache fragmentation. Each sequence reserves one long contiguous block of memory for its key-value cache. Real lengths vary, so the reserved blocks are half empty on average, and the wasted memory is memory that cannot hold more concurrent sequences.
  3. No prefix reuse. In multi-turn rollouts (agents, long CoT with shared system prompts), the engine re-processes the same prefix for every turn. An engine with prefix caching computes it once.

Inference engines attack all three. That is the entire reason they belong in the RL loop.

A.2.3 How it works under the hood

Continuous batching (also called iteration-level scheduling) is the core trick. Instead of forming a batch and waiting for every member to finish, the scheduler works one iteration at a time. Each iteration, finished sequences leave, newly arrived sequences join, and the scheduler refills the freed slots immediately. There is no "wait for the longest sequence." The batch is a living thing, not a fixed roster.

PagedAttention (vLLM's KV cache design) fixes the fragmentation. The KV cache is split into fixed-size blocks (pages), like virtual memory in an OS. A sequence's cache is a list of block pointers, not one contiguous slab. Short sequences use few blocks, long ones use many, and there is no half-empty reserved slab. More concurrent sequences fit per GPU, which directly raises tokens per second.

RadixAttention (SGLang's design) adds prefix sharing on top: a radix tree of cached prefixes computes common prompt prefixes (system prompts, few-shot examples, repeated tool definitions) once and reuses them across requests. For agentic rollouts with long shared prefixes, this is a large win.

Weight sync closes the loop with training. After each gradient step, the trainer must get fresh weights into the workers. On a real cluster this is a broadcast over NCCL (the GPU collective library) or a checkpoint-engine transfer. In verl's rollout-server mode, a checkpoint engine moves weights from trainer to rollout replicas with a staged protocol: abort in-flight requests, transfer, verify version, resume. The workers then generate with the new policy. The sync is a brief pause in generation, which is one more reason A.3 decouples the two sides.

The simulation below shows the continuous-batching win with the same requests and the same per-iteration speed. The only difference is the schedule.

# Static vs continuous batching, simulated with identical requests.
# Each request: (arrival iteration, total tokens). Each iteration the GPU
# processes a fixed token budget split across the running batch.
# Static batching locks the batch: once admitted, no new request joins
# until every member finishes, so a long request holds slots hostage.
# Continuous batching refills finished slots every iteration.
# Same work, same speed: scheduling is the only difference.

def simulate(requests, tok_per_iter, max_batch, continuous):
    pending = sorted(requests)   # not yet admitted, ordered by arrival
    active = []                  # [tokens_left, arrival] currently running
    t, i = 0, 0
    done = []                    # completion times (arrival -> finished)
    batch_open = True            # static batching closes the door mid-batch
    while i < len(pending) or active:
        # Admit arrivals while there is room (and the door is open).
        while (i < len(pending) and pending[i][0] <= t
               and len(active) < max_batch and batch_open):
            arrival, tokens = pending[i]
            active.append([tokens, arrival])
            i += 1
        if not active:
            t = pending[i][0]    # idle: jump to the next arrival
            continue
        # One iteration: split the token budget across the running batch.
        share = tok_per_iter / len(active)
        still = []
        for rem, arrival in active:
            rem -= share
            if rem <= 0:
                done.append(t + 1 - arrival)
            else:
                still.append([rem, arrival])
        active = still
        # Static batching: the door stays shut until the batch fully drains.
        # Continuous batching: the door never closes.
        if not continuous:
            batch_open = len(active) == 0
        t += 1
    return sum(done) / len(done), t

A.3 Async RL architectures and memory layout for rollout workers

A.3.1 What async RL is

In the lockstep loop from A.1, generation and training take turns. The trainers sit idle while rollouts generate. Then the rollout workers sit idle while gradients compute. Async RL breaks the turn-taking. Rollout workers generate continuously on their own GPUs, and trainers update continuously on theirs. The two sides meet only at weight sync, when the trainer publishes fresh weights to the fleet.

The price of decoupling is staleness: the rollouts come from a slightly older policy than the one under training. Async systems bound that staleness (a few versions behind, not fifty) and correct for it in the update. The reward is utilization: no side ever waits for the other, and one straggler with a 32K-token chain-of-thought no longer stalls the whole step.

The reference design is verl's HybridFlow (Hybrid-Controller) architecture. A single-controller orchestration layer treats the RL algorithm as a dataflow graph: schedule rollouts, score, dispatch training. Workers run FSDP/Megatron for training, the sharded-training frameworks from Volume 6, and vLLM/SGLang/TensorRT-LLM for rollout. In fully-async mode, rollout workers sample continuously into a message queue, and the trainer consumes mini-batches from the queue. The schedule triggers weight syncs, with a staleness threshold that pauses generation if queued data gets too old. The one-step-off variant overlaps the two phases by one step: while the trainer runs iteration k, the fleet generates iteration k+1's rollouts.

A.3.2 Why it exists

Three forces push RL toward async:

  1. The 90/10 split from A.1. If generation is 90% of the step, lockstep means trainers idle 90% of the time. Decoupling lets a small trainer pool serve a large rollout fleet, each sized to its own bottleneck.
  2. Stragglers. Completion lengths vary wildly (a short refusal vs a 32K-token proof). In lockstep, the step waits for the longest rollout. In async, long rollouts just occupy one worker while the rest of the fleet keeps producing.
  3. Fault tolerance. A dead rollout worker in lockstep halts training. In async with a supervisor heartbeat (verl's rollout-elastic design), the supervisor prunes and replaces the dead replica while training continues on the queue's buffered batches.

A.3.3 How it works under the hood

Placement. Three options, one tradeoff (memory vs sync cost):

  • Colocated: training and rollout share the same GPUs. Memory is tight (weights + optimizer + KV cache on one card); the engine must release inference memory before the training step and reclaim it after (TensorRT-LLM's rollout in verl uses explicit release/resume for this).
  • Disaggregated: separate GPU pools for rollout and training. Each side is memory-comfortable; the cost is the weight broadcast between pools every sync.
  • Hybrid: some GPUs colocated, overflow on dedicated rollout nodes. Common at large scale.

Weight sync. The trainer's weights must reach every rollout replica. At small scale this is a checkpoint copy. At scale it is a staged transfer over NCCL with versioning: abort in-flight requests, transfer, verify, resume. Then a replica never generates with half-old weights.

Staleness control. Two mechanisms keep old-policy data honest:

  • Bounded lag: the trainer syncs every N updates, and generation pauses if the queue holds batches sampled more than K versions ago (verl's staleness threshold).
  • Algorithmic correction: the update accounts for the policy that generated the data. verl implements this as a three-policy setup. The rollout policy is the behavior policy that generated the data. The proximal policy is the clipping anchor, computed fresh each epoch. The current policy is the one under optimization. PPO-style clipping against the proximal policy keeps updates safe even when the data came from a slightly older rollout policy.

The asyncio sketch below is the architecture in miniature: producers and a consumer joined by a bounded queue, rendezvousing only at weight sync. The sleeps stand in for GPU work; the structure is the real thing.

# Async RL in miniature: rollout producers and a trainer consumer joined
# by a bounded queue. Neither side waits for the other; they rendezvous
# only at weight sync. Runnable anywhere: the sleeps stand in for GPU
# work, so run it and watch the overlap in the timestamps.

import asyncio
import time

t0 = time.time()

def now():
    # Elapsed seconds: makes the producer/consumer overlap visible.
    return round(time.time() - t0, 1)

async def rollout_worker(queue, worker_id, n_batches):
    # One rollout worker: generate a batch, stamp it with the weight
    # version it used, push it on the queue, repeat. It never blocks on
    # the trainer. If the queue fills, put() waits: that is backpressure
    # (a staleness guard), not lockstep.
    version = 0
    for b in range(n_batches):
        await asyncio.sleep(0.5)   # stands in for generating G completions
        await queue.put((worker_id, b, version))
        print(f"{now()}s  worker {worker_id}: pushed batch {b} "
              f"(weights v{version})")

async def trainer(queue, n_batches, sync_every):
    # The trainer: pull batches, update, and every sync_every batches
    # publish new weights. Here publishing is a version bump; on a real
    # cluster it is a staged NCCL broadcast to the rollout fleet.
    version = 0
    for b in range(n_batches):
        worker_id, batch_id, used_version = await queue.get()
        staleness = version - used_version   # how old is this data?
        await asyncio.sleep(0.3)             # stands in for the grad step
        print(f"{now()}s  trainer: batch {batch_id} from worker "
              f"{worker_id}, staleness {staleness}")
        if (b + 1) % sync_every == 0:
            version += 1
            print(f"{now()}s  trainer: published weights v{version}")
        queue.task_done()

async def main():
    # Bounded queue: the bound IS the staleness control. Unbounded would
    # let workers race arbitrarily far ahead of the trainer.
    queue = asyncio.Queue(maxsize=4)
    await asyncio.gather(
        rollout_worker(queue, 0, 6),
        rollout_worker(queue, 1, 6),
        trainer(queue, 12, sync_every=4),
    )

asyncio.run(main())

How to read this diagram

  1. Two producers, one consumer. Workers 0 and 1 each push 6 batches; the trainer consumes 12. They run concurrently: while the trainer chews batch 0, workers are already generating batches 2 and 3.
  2. The version stamp. Each batch carries the weight version used to generate it. The trainer prints staleness = current version minus batch version. Watch it stay at 0 or 1: data is never ancient.
  3. The sync point. Every 4 batches the trainer publishes v1, v2, v3. In a real system the workers would pick up the new weights here. The sketch keeps workers on v0 to keep the code short. That is why staleness grows late in the run. That growth is exactly what the staleness threshold guards against in production.
  4. The queue bound. maxsize=4 means workers can run at most 4 batches ahead. Remove the bound and fast workers race ahead: unbounded staleness, unstable training. The bound is a one-line staleness controller.
  5. What breaks if you lockstep it. Replace the queue with a direct handoff (worker generates, then waits for the trainer to finish before generating again) and total runtime becomes the sum of both phases instead of their overlap. That sum is the tax async RL refuses to pay.

A.3.4 Memory layout of a rollout worker

A rollout worker never trains, so its GPU carries no gradients and no optimizer states. Its memory is three items:

  1. Weights, read-only: params x bytes per param.
  2. KV cache for the running batch: 2 (K and V) x layers x KV heads x head dim x bytes per param x batch x seq len. Note KV heads, not query heads. GQA (grouped-query attention) models share key/value heads across query heads. They cache far less per token and serve longer contexts per GPU.
  3. Fixed overhead: CUDA context, engine workspace, fragmentation slack. Budget ~2 GB.

Compare with the trainer side, which carries weights + gradients + optimizer states (Adam in fp32: 8 bytes per param on top of the 2-byte weights and 2-byte grads, so ~6x the weight memory before activations). This asymmetry is why disaggregation is attractive. The rollout pool is memory-light per GPU and scales with batch x length. The trainer pool is memory-heavy and scales with model size.

# GPU memory layout of one rollout worker: weights + KV cache + overhead.
# No gradients, no optimizer states: the worker never trains. Change the
# model shape or the batch geometry and watch which term dominates.

def rollout_worker_memory_gb(
    params_b,          # model size in billions of parameters
    bytes_per_param,   # 2 for bf16/fp16, 0.5 for 4-bit
    n_layers,          # transformer layers
    n_kv_heads,        # key/value heads (GQA-aware: fewer than query heads)
    head_dim,          # dimensions per head
    batch,             # concurrent sequences in the running batch
    seq_len,           # tokens per sequence (prompt + generated so far)
    overhead_gb=2.0,   # CUDA context, engine workspace, slack
):
    # Weights: the full model, read-only during rollouts.
    weights_gb = params_b * bytes_per_param   # billions x bytes = GB

    # KV cache per token: 2 (K,V) x layers x kv_heads x head_dim elements,
    # each bytes_per_param wide. GQA shows up here directly: halving KV
    # heads halves this term, which is why GQA models pack longer contexts
    # per GPU. If you change the attention type, change this line first.
    kv_per_token_gb = 2 * n_layers * n_kv_heads * head_dim * bytes_per_param / 1e9
    # The cache must cover the worst case the scheduler admits:
    # batch x sequence length.
    kv_gb = kv_per_token_gb * batch * seq_len

    total = weights_gb + kv_gb + overhead_gb
    return {
        "weights_gb": round(weights_gb, 1),
        "kv_cache_gb": round(kv_gb, 1),
        "overhead_gb": overhead_gb,
        "total_gb": round(total, 1),
    }

# 7B bf16, 32 layers, 8 KV heads, head dim 128, batch 32 x 4096 tokens:
# weights 14.0 GB, KV per token 128 KB, cache 17.2 GB, total 33.2 GB.
# Fits a 40 GB card with room to spare.
print(rollout_worker_memory_gb(7, 2, 32, 8, 128, 32, 4096))
# {'weights_gb': 14.0, 'kv_cache_gb': 17.2, 'overhead_gb': 2.0,
#  'total_gb': 33.2}

# 70B bf16, 80 layers, 8 KV heads, batch 8 x 4096: weights 140 GB alone
# exceed any single GPU, so the worker needs tensor parallelism across
# at least 2x80 GB cards before the KV cache is even counted.
print(rollout_worker_memory_gb(70, 2, 80, 8, 128, 8, 4096))
# {'weights_gb': 140.0, 'kv_cache_gb': 10.7, 'overhead_gb': 2.0,
#  'total_gb': 152.7}

How to read this diagram

  1. Read the bar left to right as gigabytes. The model fixes the weights. Your batch geometry sets the KV cache. Overhead is roughly constant.
  2. The KV cache is the moving part. At batch 32 x 4096 it is the largest term (52%). Halve the batch and it halves; double the sequence length and it doubles. This is the term the scheduler manages.

  4. The 70B line is the scaling lesson. Weights alone (140 GB) exceed one GPU, so rollout workers for 70B need tensor parallelism first; only then does the KV term matter. Placement follows the weights.

A.3.5 Common misunderstanding

"Async means training on stale garbage, so lockstep is safer." That fear misses the point. In a well-built async system, staleness stays within a few versions and the update corrects for it explicitly. The update clips against the proximal policy and accounts for the rollout policy that generated the data (verl's three-policy setup). What lockstep buys in freshness it pays in utilization: trainers idle through every generation phase, and one straggler stalls everything. The production consensus has moved. Bound the staleness, correct for it, and keep both sides busy.

    Unit 1: Post-training data pipelines

    B.1 Synthetic data generation pipelines

    B.1.1 What synthetic data is

    Synthetic data is training data a model writes instead of a human: prompts, responses, step-by-step rationales, code with tests, preference pairs. The pipeline is a loop. Generate candidates. Filter them with a checker. Keep the good ones. Sometimes feed them back to improve the generator itself.

    This is not an exotic trick anymore. It is how the field makes reasoning data at scale. Human experts cannot write millions of step-by-step math solutions. But a model can draft them, and a verifier can check the answers.

    B.1.2 Why it exists

    Three reasons, in order of importance:

    1. Humans are slow and expensive. A careful math rationale takes an expert many minutes. A model writes thousands per hour.
    2. Verifiable domains filter themselves. In math and code, correctness is checkable: the answer matches, or the tests pass. That turns generation into a precision instrument: keep only what is right.
    3. Bootstrapping works. A model trained on its own correct outputs gets better at producing correct outputs, which makes the next round of synthetic data better. The loop compounds.

    The catch, covered in B.1.5, is that the loop can also compound the model's blind spots. Filtering is the whole game.

    B.1.3 How it works under the hood

    The STaR loop. STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner, Zelikman et al., 2022) is the canonical bootstrapping recipe, and every later variant is a variation on it:

    1. Generate. Prompt the model with a few rationale examples and ask it to solve many questions, writing its reasoning.
    2. Filter. Keep the rationales that reach the correct answer. Discard the rest.
    3. Rationalize. For the failures, try again with the correct answer given as a hint, asking for the reasoning that leads there. Then strip the hint: the training example is (question, rationale), not (question, hint, rationale).
    4. Fine-tune. Train on all kept rationales.
    5. Repeat. The improved model generates better rationales next round.

    The paper's headline result: this loop let a 6B model reach CommonsenseQA accuracy comparable to fine-tuning a 30x larger model. The model teaches itself reasoning by keeping its own correct work.

    Rejection sampling (RFT). A simpler, one-shot cousin (Yuan et al., 2023). Fine-tune a base model on the seed data, then sample k solutions per problem. Keep the correct ones, augment the dataset, and fine-tune once more. No iteration: cheaper, less compounding.

    Distillation at scale. The industrial version: a strong teacher generates a large, carefully filtered dataset, and smaller models train on it. DeepSeek's R1 report describes distilling 800K samples (600K reasoning, 200K non-reasoning) from R1 into smaller dense models, a major driver of the R1-Distill family's strength. Same pattern as STaR, minus the loop, at massive scale.

    Quality gates. Every serious pipeline applies four, in order:

    • Dedupe: near-duplicate solutions teach repetition.
    • Difficulty filter: drop problems the generator solves trivially every time. They add no signal.
    • Decontamination: remove anything overlapping eval sets, or your "gains" are leakage.
    • Format checks: answers must be parseable, because the verifier has to extract them.

    The code below is the STaR loop with a toy noisy generator so it runs anywhere. The generator is a stand-in; the loop structure is the real thing.

    # STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner) in miniature: the synthetic-data loop.
# generate  -> the model writes rationales for questions
# filter    -> keep rationales that reach the right answer
# rationalize -> retry failures with the answer given as a hint, then
#                strip the hint before training (the model must learn the
#                reasoning, not the hint)
# fine-tune -> train on the kept rationales; repeat
# The generator here is a noisy toy so the loop runs anywhere. Swap in a
# real model and a real checker for the honest version.

import random

random.seed(7)

def noisy_multiply(a, b, hint=None):
    # Toy "model": a calculator that sometimes slips a digit. With a hint
    # (the true answer), its work is clean: that is rationalization.
    true = a * b
    if hint is not None:
        shown = true
    else:
        # 50% of the time it slips: the forward pass is unreliable,
        # which is exactly the regime where STaR helps.
        shown = true + random.choice([0, 0, 0, 10, -10, 100])
    rationale = f"{a}x{b}: partial products sum to {shown}."
    return rationale, shown

def star_iteration(n_questions):
    # One STaR iteration. Returns (forward_kept, rationalized_kept).
    forward_kept, rationalized_kept = 0, 0
    for _ in range(n_questions):
        a, b = random.randint(10, 99), random.randint(10, 99)
        rationale, answer = noisy_multiply(a, b)
        if answer == a * b:
            forward_kept += 1       # the model solved it alone: keep
        else:
            # Backward rationalization: give the answer, ask for the work.
            rationale2, answer2 = noisy_multiply(a, b, hint=a * b)
            if answer2 == a * b:
                rationalized_kept += 1   # new capability, banked as data
    return forward_kept, rationalized_kept

for it in range(3):
    fwd, rat = star_iteration(200)
    print(f"iter {it}: forward-kept {fwd}, rationalized-kept {rat}, "
          f"banked {fwd + rat}")
# iter 0: forward-kept 100, rationalized-kept 100, banked 200
# iter 1: forward-kept 119, rationalized-kept 81, banked 200
# iter 2: forward-kept 107, rationalized-kept 93, banked 200

    How to read this diagram

    1. The toy model. noisy_multiply slips a digit half the time without a hint, never with one. That gap between unaided and hinted performance is the raw material STaR mines.
    2. Forward keeps. The model solves about 100 of 200 questions unaided each iteration, and the loop banks them directly. These are the model's existing competence, converted into training data.
    3. Rationalized keeps. The model solves the other ~100 only with the answer given, and the loop banks their clean rationales too. This is the interesting half: traces the model could not produce on its own.
    4. What the toy does not show. The toy generator never learns, so the forward rate stays flat. In the real loop, step 4 (fine-tune on banked traces) is what lifts the next iteration's forward rate. The paper's compounding comes from that training step, which the demo omits on purpose to keep the data mechanics visible.
    5. What breaks if you skip rationalization. Keeping only forward successes banks what the model already knows. Rationalization is how the dataset gains reasoning the model could not previously produce: the frontier of the data moves outward each round.
    How to read this diagram

    B.1.4 Worked numbers: the cost of a synthetic batch

    Say you want 100K verified math rationales. Recipe: 50K seed problems, sample k=16 each at ~500 tokens per rationale.

    • Generation volume: 50K x 16 x 500 = 400M tokens.
    • Keep rate 25% (only a quarter reach correct answers): 200K rationales banked, 100M tokens of training data.
    • The loop discards the other 300M generated tokens: the price of filtering. A better generator (higher keep rate) is worth more than a cheaper generator here, because discarded tokens dominate the bill.

    This is why keep rate is the metric synthetic-data engineers watch. It is also why the STaR loop's compounding matters: each iteration raises the keep rate of the next.

    B.1.5 Common misunderstanding

    "Synthetic data is free and unlimited, so just generate more." Two failure modes. First, model collapse: training only on your own outputs narrows the distribution round after round, and the model forgets the tails of the real data. Keep real human data in the mix and filter hard.

    Second, the generator's blind spots become the dataset's blind spots. If the model mishandles a problem type, its own sampling never fixes it: the filter keeps only what the model can verify. Synthetic data amplifies capability but does not invent it from nothing, and even the rationalization step needs ground-truth answers from somewhere real.

    B.2 Verifier and reward pipelines

    B.2.1 What a verifier is

    A verifier is a program that checks a model output and returns a verdict: right or wrong, tests passed or failed, proof valid or not. A reward pipeline is the code path from a raw rollout to the scalar reward the RL update consumes. Normalize the output, extract the answer, run the checks, aggregate to a number, and log everything.

    Verifiers are what make RLVR (RL with verifiable rewards, Volume 7 Chapter 6) possible. Where RLHF needs a learned reward model trained on human preferences, RLVR needs a correct program. The verifier is the reward model, written in code instead of learned from labels.

    B.2.2 Why it exists

    A reward function you cannot trust teaches the wrong thing, and the model will find the gap. This is where reward hacking is born (Volume 7 Chapter 8): not in the RL algorithm, but in the plumbing between generation and reward. A verifier that counts a test as passed when the solution crashed. A math checker that accepts "42" inside a paragraph of nonsense. An extraction regex that grabs the wrong number. Each of these becomes the behavior the model learns. The model optimizes the reward you actually compute, not the reward you meant.

    So the verifier pipeline is built like infrastructure, not like a script: staged, logged, tested against adversarial outputs.

    B.2.3 How it works under the hood

    A production verifier pipeline has five stages:

    1. Normalize. Strip formatting: remove markdown fences, trailing whitespace, LaTeX wrappers. Model outputs are messy; the checker must see through the mess deterministically.
    2. Extract. Pull the answer out: the number in \boxed{}, the code block, the JSON field. Extraction is where most verifier bugs live, because there are a hundred ways to write "42".
    3. Check. Run the actual verification: unit tests in a sandbox, symbolic or numeric answer comparison, proof-checker validation. This stage must be deterministic: same output, same verdict, every time.
    4. Aggregate. Combine check results into one scalar: fraction of tests passed, binary correct/incorrect, partial credit rules. Keep the aggregation simple enough to audit.
    5. Audit log. Record (raw output, extracted answer, per-check results, final reward) for every rollout. When reward hacking appears, this log is the crime scene.

    Sandboxing is non-negotiable for code: generated code runs in a subprocess with a timeout and no network, because you treat generated code as untrusted input. A missing timeout is how one infinite loop hangs your entire rollout fleet.

    V-STaR (Hosseini et al., 2024) adds a learned layer. Train a verifier on both correct and incorrect generations. At test time you can rank candidates by the verifier's score instead of relying on hand-written checks alone. Hand-written verifiers for training signal, learned verifiers for selection: they compose.

    The code below is a complete verifier pipeline for code tasks, small enough to read in one sitting.

    # A verifier pipeline for code tasks: normalize, execute, score, audit.
# The verifier IS the reward function, so its bugs become the model's
# bugs. Every stage logs its decision: when the model starts gaming the
# reward, this log is where you find out how.

import subprocess
import tempfile
import os

def verify_solution(code, cases, timeout_s=10):
    # code: candidate source defining solve(...).
    # cases: list of (args_tuple, expected_value).
    # Returns (reward, audit_log). Reward is the fraction of cases passed.
    # Stages: (1) the code is written to disk as-is (normalization would
    # strip fences here in a fuller pipeline); (2) a harness calls
    # solve() per case in a subprocess; (3) per-case PASS/FAIL lines are
    # parsed; (4) the fraction passed is the scalar reward; (5) every
    # line is returned as the audit log.
    harness = "import sol\n"
    for i, (args, expected) in enumerate(cases):
        # One guarded call per case: an exception in case 3 must not kill
        # cases 4 and 5. repr() keeps values unambiguous in the log.
        harness += (
            f"try:\n"
            f"    got = sol.solve(*{args!r})\n"
            f"    print('PASS' if got == {expected!r} else 'FAIL', {i}, "
            f"          repr(got))\n"
            f"except Exception as e:\n"
            f"    print('ERROR', {i}, type(e).__name__)\n"
        )
    with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as d:
        open(os.path.join(d, "sol.py"), "w").write(code)
        open(os.path.join(d, "run.py"), "w").write(harness)
        try:
            r = subprocess.run(
                ["python3", "run.py"],
                capture_output=True, text=True,
                timeout=timeout_s, cwd=d)
        except subprocess.TimeoutExpired:
            # A hang is a zero, not a crash: the pipeline must survive it.
            # Without this branch, one infinite loop stalls the rollout
            # fleet. Timeouts are load-bearing infrastructure.
            return 0.0, ["TIMEOUT"]
        log = r.stdout.strip().splitlines()
        passed = sum(1 for line in log if line.startswith("PASS"))
        reward = passed / len(cases)
        return reward, log

# Demo: sum of squares, one correct and one off-by-one candidate.
good = "def solve(n):\n    return sum(i*i for i in range(1, n+1))\n"
bad = "def solve(n):\n    return sum(i*i for i in range(n))\n"
cases = [((3,), 14), ((1,), 1), ((5,), 55)]

print(verify_solution(good, cases))
# (1.0, ['PASS 0 14', 'PASS 1 1', 'PASS 2 55'])
print(verify_solution(bad, cases))
# (0.0, ['FAIL 0 5', 'FAIL 1 0', 'FAIL 2 30'])

    How to read this diagram

    1. The harness. For each case, a try/except block calls solve() and prints one verdict line. The per-case guard means one crashing case cannot hide the others: partial credit stays honest.
    2. The sandbox. The candidate runs in a child process with a 10-second timeout, in a temp directory. The timeout branch returns reward 0.0 with a TIMEOUT marker: hangs are data, not disasters.
    3. The aggregation. Reward = fraction passed. Simple, auditable, no hidden weighting. If you want partial credit or difficulty weighting, it goes here, in the open.
    4. The audit log. Every verdict line comes back to the caller. In a real pipeline you write these lines to disk per rollout: raw output, extracted answer, per-case verdicts, final reward. When the model learns to print "PASS" instead of solving (it happens), the log shows the raw output diverging from the verdicts.
    5. The demo's lesson. The off-by-one candidate fails all three cases visibly: range(n) vs range(1, n+1). A verifier that only checked n=1 would have passed it (both give... no: bad gives 0 for n=1, good gives 1). The point stands: case coverage is the verifier's real quality metric.

    B.2.4 Worked numbers: verifier throughput

    500 code problems, 8 samples each, 5 test cases per sample: 20,000 program executions per RL step. At 0.2 s per execution (small tests, warm interpreter), that is ~67 minutes single-threaded: completely unacceptable inside a training loop. Verifiers parallelize trivially (each execution is independent), so a 64-worker pool brings it to ~63 seconds. Budget verifier parallelism the same way you budget rollout GPUs: it is part of the generation phase's wall clock from A.1. And cap the timeout aggressively: one 10-second hang per thousand executions adds ~3 minutes per step at this scale if unparallelized.

    B.2.5 Common misunderstanding

    "A verifier just runs the tests." A verifier is not the check stage alone. It is all five stages from Section B.2.3. Most bugs live in normalization and extraction: model outputs arrive wrapped in markdown and apologies, and the audit log is where reward-hacking diagnoses start. Teams that treat the verifier as "just run pytest" discover the other stages the hard way. Usually that happens when the model's reward curve climbs while its actual capability does not.

    B.3 SFT data mixtures and rejection-sampling recipes

    B.3.1 What a data mixture is

    A data mixture is the weighted blend of sources that makes up supervised fine-tuning: X% code, Y% math, Z% chat, and so on. The mixture is not a vibe; it is a sampling distribution. Every training batch is drawn from it, so the mixture decides what the model practices, in what proportion, for the whole run.

    Rejection sampling is the filter that upgrades a source. Generate k candidates per prompt. Keep the ones that pass a checker or judge. Discard the rest. It trades compute for quality inside a fixed source.

    B.3.2 Why the mixture matters more than its size

    At fixed training compute, tokens are a budget. Every token of low-quality chat you include is a token of high-quality code you exclude. The mixture sets three things:

    1. Skill balance. The model's competence follows the mixture weights, with a lag. Underweight math and math scores sag two weeks later.
    2. Repetition risk. Small sources get repeated many times per run. A 100B-token run with a 5B-token source at 20% weight repeats that source 4 times. Repetition past a few epochs teaches memorization of the source, not the skill.
    3. Behavioral tone. The chat portion sets refusal style, formatting habits, and personality. It is small in tokens and large in influence, because it is where the model learns how to talk.

    The QLoRA paper's finding is the extreme case of the quality argument: fine-tuning on a small high-quality dataset beat larger noisier ones. Tokens are not interchangeable; the mixture is a quality-weighted portfolio.

    B.3.3 How it works under the hood

    Mixture math. Sources have sizes (tokens available) and weights (target fractions). Per training run of T tokens, source i contributes weight_i x T tokens, which is (weight_i x T / size_i) epochs of that source. The planner below computes exactly this, and flags repetition.

    Temperature and upsampling. Small, precious sources (expert-written math, carefully reviewed code) are routinely upsampled: given weight beyond their size share, accepting repetition as the price. The rule of thumb is to watch epochs per source and cap the worst offenders, either by lowering their weight or by finding more data.

    The rejection-sampling recipe, in the order practitioners run it:

    1. Generate k per prompt (k=8 to 64; temperature moderate-high for diversity).
    2. Filter with the verifier from B.2 (code, math) or a model judge (open-ended).
    3. Keep at most one or two per prompt. Keeping all k passes of an easy prompt floods the data with near-duplicates of the same reasoning.
    4. Dedupe across prompts: exact-match dedupe first, then near-dedupe on normalized text or embeddings.
    5. Re-balance by difficulty: keep a higher fraction of hard-prompt successes, because easy successes are plentiful and teach less.

    # SFT mixture planner: from source sizes and target weights to a training
# plan. The mix is a promise about what the model will practice. This
# planner turns "50% code, 30% math, 20% chat" into tokens per source,
# epochs per source, and total steps, so you can see when a small source
# will be repeated 4x (and start memorizing itself).

def plan_mixture(sources, total_tokens, seq_len, batch_seqs):
    # sources: list of (name, size_tokens, weight). Weights need not sum
    # to 1; they are normalized here, so you can write 5, 3, 2.
    total_w = sum(w for _, _, w in sources)
    plan = []
    for name, size, w in sources:
        frac = w / total_w
        tokens = frac * total_tokens   # tokens drawn from this source
        # Epochs > 2-3 deserve a second look: repetition teaches the
        # source's surface patterns, not the underlying skill.
        epochs = tokens / size
        plan.append((name, frac, tokens, epochs))
    steps = total_tokens / (seq_len * batch_seqs)
    return plan, steps

sources = [
    ("code", 40e9, 0.5),   # 40B tokens available, target 50%
    ("math", 10e9, 0.3),   # 10B tokens available, target 30%
    ("chat",  5e9, 0.2),   #  5B tokens available, target 20%
]
plan, steps = plan_mixture(sources, total_tokens=100e9,
                           seq_len=2048, batch_seqs=512)
for name, frac, tokens, epochs in plan:
    print(f"{name:6s} {frac:4.0%}  {tokens/1e9:5.1f}B tokens  "

# code    50%   50.0B tokens   1.2 epochs
# math    30%   30.0B tokens   3.0 epochs
# chat    20%   20.0B tokens   4.0 epochs
# total steps: 95,367

# Rejection-sampling recipe: generate k, keep what passes, cap per prompt.
def rejection_sample(prompts, k, generate, judge):
    # generate(prompt) -> candidate string; judge(prompt, candidate)
    # -> True/False (the verifier from B.2, or a model judge).
    # Returns kept (prompt, candidate) pairs, at most one per prompt:
    # the cap is what prevents easy prompts from flooding the dataset
    # with near-duplicate successes.
    kept = []
    for p in prompts:
        for _ in range(k):
            c = generate(p)
            if judge(p, c):
                kept.append((p, c))
                break          # one keep per prompt, then move on
    return kept

    1. The inputs. Each source declares what exists (size) and what you want (weight). The planner's job is to show the tension between the two.
    2. The epochs column is the diagnosis. Code at 1.2 epochs is comfortable. The run repeats math at 3.0 and chat at 4.0 enough to memorize surface patterns. If chat quality matters (it sets tone), 4 epochs of the same 5B tokens is a risk: find more chat data or lower its weight.
    3. Steps from geometry. 100B tokens at 2048-token sequences x 512 sequences per batch = ~95K steps. Change the batch and the step count moves, but the per-source epochs do not: epochs are a property of the mixture, not the hardware.
    4. The rejection cap. break after the first keep is deliberate. Without it, easy prompts contribute k near-identical successes and the dataset's difficulty distribution collapses toward trivial.
    5. What breaks if you skip dedupe. The recipe as written does not dedupe across prompts; in production, an exact-match pass and a near-dedupe pass follow. Duplicated reasoning traces teach the model to repeat itself verbatim.

    "More data is always better; just add every source you can find." This is wrong at fixed compute. Adding a source means removing tokens from somewhere else. Low-quality tokens actively harm: the model practices the wrong patterns. That is the B.3.2 portfolio point as a design rule: shrink the mixture and raise the bar. The scarcest resource is verified-correct tokens, not tokens.

    B.4 Preference-data collection design

    B.4.1 What preference data is

    Preference data is the (prompt, response A, response B, verdict) structure from Chapter 3. This chapter is about collecting it as an operations discipline. The labels are the ground truth your reward model treats as reality. So every systematic error in collection becomes a systematic error in the model's values.

    B.4.2 Why collection design matters

    A reward model cannot exceed its labels: noisy preferences train a noisy reward function, and RL then optimizes it faithfully. These are Chapter 3's pitfalls restated as collection-design problems:

    1. Rubric ambiguity. If labelers disagree on what "better" means, the dataset encodes the disagreement as noise.
    2. Labeler drift. Standards shift over weeks of labeling; early and late labels are not drawn from the same distribution.
    3. Spurious correlations. Labelers prefer longer answers, or more confident tone, or bullet lists. The reward model learns "longer = better" instead of "correct = better", and RL then breeds verbosity (a classic reward-hacking channel).

    Collection design is how you fight all three before the data poisons the reward model.

    B.4.3 How it works under the hood

    Labeler instructions. The rubric is a document, not a paragraph. It defines the criteria in priority order (correctness first, then helpfulness, then clarity), and it gives paired examples of each verdict. It lists edge cases: two wrong answers, refusals, disallowed content. It tells labelers when to skip rather than guess. The team versions the rubric, and labels carry the rubric version.

    Task design. Pairwise comparison (A vs B) is the standard unit: it is the easiest judgment for humans to make reliably. Rankings of 3-4 cost more attention per item. Multi-turn comparisons judge whole conversations, which is harder and noisier; many pipelines judge single turns and compose.

    Disagreement handling. Every item gets 3 labelers. Majority wins. Ties and abstentions go to expert adjudication, never to a coin flip: a coin flip trains the reward model on noise. Items with low agreement across the whole batch trigger rubric repair: the instructions were unclear, so fix the document, not the labelers.

    Agreement metrics. The team tracks Percent agreement (how often labelers pick the same winner) and Cohen's kappa (agreement corrected for chance) per batch. Healthy operations live around kappa 0.6-0.8. Below ~0.4, stop labeling and repair the rubric: more labels under a broken rubric just manufacture confident noise.

    Calibration. You mix gold items with known-correct verdicts into every batch. Labelers who drift on gold get retrained. This is how you catch drift before it contaminates thousands of labels.

    AI feedback as a complement. Chapter 7's CAI/RLAIF scales labeling with model-generated judgments. The same quality control applies: measure AI-judge agreement against human gold, and repair the principles when it sags.

    # Preference-label quality control: agreement metrics and adjudication.
# Three labelers judge each pair; the majority becomes the label, and the
# agreement rate tells you whether the rubric is working. Low agreement
# means the prompt is ambiguous (fix the instructions), not that the
# labelers are bad.

def cohens_kappa(a, b):
    # a, b: two labelers' 0/1 vote lists on the same pairs.
    # kappa = (observed - chance) / (1 - chance). Chance-correction
    # matters: two labelers who always vote 1 agree 100% but know
    # nothing; kappa gives them 0. Healthy ops: 0.6-0.8. Below ~0.4,
    # stop and repair the rubric before labeling another pair.
    n = len(a)
    agree = sum(1 for x, y in zip(a, b) if x == y) / n
    p_a1 = sum(a) / n            # labeler A's rate of voting 1
    p_b1 = sum(b) / n            # labeler B's rate of voting 1
    chance = p_a1 * p_b1 + (1 - p_a1) * (1 - p_b1)
    return (agree - chance) / (1 - chance) if chance < 1 else 1.0

def adjudicate(votes):
    # votes: three 0/1 labels, None where a labeler abstained.
    # Majority of the non-abstained wins. A tie or total abstention goes
    # to expert review: never a coin flip, because a coin flip trains the
    # reward model on pure noise disguised as a label.
    clean = [v for v in votes if v is not None]
    if not clean:
        return "expert"          # everyone abstained: genuinely hard pair
    ones = sum(clean)
    zeros = len(clean) - ones
    if ones > zeros:
        return 1
    if zeros > ones:
        return 0
    return "expert"              # tie: a human expert decides

import random
random.seed(11)
truth = [random.choice([0, 1]) for _ in range(1000)]
def labeler(err):
    return [t if random.random() > err else 1 - t for t in truth]

print(f"kappa(l1,l2) = {cohens_kappa(l1, l2):.2f}")   # 0.59
print(f"kappa(l1,l3) = {cohens_kappa(l1, l3):.2f}")   # 0.54
majority = [1 if sum(v) >= 2 else 0 for v in zip(l1, l2, l3)]
acc = sum(1 for m, t in zip(majority, truth) if m == t) / 1000
print(f"majority-vote accuracy: {acc:.1%}")           # 96.2%
# Three 88%-accurate labelers vote a 96%-accurate label: redundancy buys

    Unit 2: Mechanistic interpretability basics

    B.5 What interpretability is for

    B.5.1 What it is

    Mechanistic interpretability is the project of explaining a neural network from its internal parts. Which features does it represent, how do those features combine into computations (circuits), and how do the computations produce behavior? "Mechanistic" marks the ambition. Not just describing what the model did, but how the machinery did it. At a level where you could predict what it will do next.

    The toolkit this unit covers: probing (B.7), sparse autoencoders (B.6), circuit analysis, and steering (B.7). Each gets its definition where the unit teaches it. What they share is the standard of evidence from Section B.5.3: correlation plus intervention.

    B.5.2 Why it exists

    Black-box evaluation tells you what the model did on the tests you ran. It cannot tell you why, and "why" matters in three situations:

    1. Debugging. The model fails on a slice of inputs. Eval scores say where; interpretability aims at the mechanism, which tells you how to fix it.
    2. Trust under distribution shift. Evals cover the tested distribution. If you understand the mechanism, you can reason about inputs you have not tested.
    3. Safety claims. "The model does not do X" is stronger when you can point at the machinery. Show the absence of the mechanism, not just the absence of observed failures.

    No one has solved any of these problems. This unit teaches the methods and their limits with equal weight.

    B.5.3 How the field thinks: the ladder and the standard of evidence

    The ladder of explanation has three rungs:

    • Behavior: what the model outputs. Evals live here.
    • Representation: what information the activations carry. Probing and SAEs live here.
    • Mechanism: how the parts compute the behavior. Circuits and causal interventions live here.

    Each rung up is harder and more valuable. The field's standard of evidence is correlation plus intervention. First show a feature correlates with a behavior (probe fires, SAE feature activates). Then intervene (ablate the feature, steer the activation) and show the behavior changes. Correlation alone is a hypothesis, and intervention is the test. This standard is what separates modern interpretability from "this neuron looks like X" storytelling.

    The key background idea is superposition (Elhage et al., 2022): networks represent more features than they have dimensions, packing them as nearly-orthogonal directions in activation space. Consequence: individual neurons are polysemantic (one neuron fires for many unrelated concepts), because the true features are directions, not neurons. SAEs exist to find those directions.

    B.5.4 Worked numbers: the scale of the enterprise

    Anthropic's SAE program scaled from 4,096 features on a small model (2023; features for DNA sequences, legal language, code) to roughly 1M, 4M, and 34M features on Claude 3 Sonnet (2024). The big runs surfaced features for abstract concepts including deception, sycophancy, and bias. Google DeepMind's Gemma Scope released SAEs across Gemma 2's layers. OpenAI trained a 16M-feature SAE on GPT-4 and published scaling laws relating compute, dictionary size, sparsity, and reconstruction quality. The direction is clear: interpretability turns from a craft project into a budgeted engineering effort.

    B.5.5 Common misunderstanding

    "Interpretability means attention heatmaps." Attention weights show where the model looked, not what it computed or why. A high attention weight on a token does not mean that token caused the output. Heads routinely attend broadly while the decisive computation happens in the MLP. Modern interpretability barely uses raw attention visualization and works on features (SAEs), probes, and causal interventions instead. If someone shows you a heatmap as an explanation, ask what intervention would change the behavior.

    B.6 Sparse autoencoders

    B.6.1 What an SAE is

    A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is a small neural network trained to reconstruct a model's activations through a wide, sparse bottleneck. Input: an activation vector x from some layer (say 2,048 dimensions). The encoder maps it to a much wider feature vector f(x) (say 16,384 dimensions). A sparsity penalty forces most entries of f(x) to zero on any given input. The decoder reconstructs the activation from the few surviving features: x ≈ W_dec f(x) + b.

    The bet: the surviving features are the monosemantic directions superposition hid. Instead of one neuron that fires for "DNA sequences, legal text, and Python comments," you get three features that each fire for one thing.

    B.6.2 Why it exists

    Neurons are polysemantic because of superposition. The model has more features to represent than dimensions to put them in, so it packs them as overlapping directions. Reading neurons directly gives mush. Dictionary learning (the classical name for what SAEs do) separates the mush into a dictionary of clean directions. That is an overcomplete set of feature vectors such that each activation is a sparse combination of a few of them.

    The 2023 result launched the modern wave (Cunningham et al.). SAEs trained on language model activations learned features more interpretable and monosemantic than any alternative decomposition. Automated interpretability scoring measured this. Ablating those features enabled precise behavior edits (e.g., removing pronoun prediction) with less collateral damage than prior methods.

    B.6.3 How it works under the hood

    Architecture. Encoder: f(x) = ReLU(W_enc (x - b_dec) + b_enc). Decoder: x_hat = W_dec f(x) + b_dec. The decoder bias doubles as the centering term: the encoder centers activations before encoding, which stabilizes training.

    Loss. Reconstruction (mean squared error between x and x_hat) plus sparsity (L1 penalty on f(x)). The L1 term is the whole trick: without it, the network learns a wide identity map and every "feature" is mush. With it, features compete to explain the activation and only the winners survive per input.

    The expansion factor (d_sae / d_model, typically 4x to 64x in practice, up to 256x) sets how many candidate features compete. More features: finer concepts, more compute, harder to inspect them all.

    Anti-cheating. A subtle failure: the network can shrink feature activations and grow decoder weights to dodge the L1 penalty. The standard fix constrains decoder columns to unit norm, so features cannot cheat on scale.

    Training data. SAEs train on cached activations: run the model over a large text corpus, save layer activations, train the SAE on those. The SAE never sees the model's weights, only its activity.

    Reading the features. For each feature, look at the inputs where it activates most strongly (max-activating examples). A feature whose top examples are all database error messages is the "database error" feature. Automated scoring (asking another model to predict the feature's activation from a proposed description) scales this to millions of features.

    Using the features. Clamp a feature's activation and watch behavior change. Anthropic's famous demo clamped the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature high, and the model steered every topic toward the bridge. That is correlation plus intervention, the B.5 standard, applied to a single feature.

    # A sparse autoencoder in miniature: the whole idea in ~30 lines.
# x: an activation vector (d_model). The encoder lifts it into a much
# wider feature space (d_sae >> d_model); a sparsity penalty forces most
# features to zero; the decoder rebuilds x from the few survivors.
# NOTE: needs PyTorch; run on any GPU machine (RunPod-ready) or CPU.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class SparseAutoencoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, d_sae):
        super().__init__()
        # Widening map d_model -> d_sae. The expansion factor
        # (d_sae / d_model, 4x-64x in practice) sets how many candidate
        # features compete to explain each activation.
        self.encoder = nn.Linear(d_model, d_sae)
        # Decoder back to d_model. Real SAEs constrain these columns to
        # unit norm so features cannot dodge the L1 penalty by shrinking
        # activations and growing weights; omitted here for brevity.
        self.decoder = nn.Linear(d_sae, d_model, bias=False)
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_model))

    def forward(self, x):
        # Center, encode, sparsify with ReLU, reconstruct.
        f = torch.relu(self.encoder(x - self.bias))
        x_hat = self.decoder(f) + self.bias
        # L1 on features: the sparsity pressure. Without this term the
        # network learns a wide identity map and every "feature" is mush.
        sparsity = f.abs().sum(dim=-1).mean()
        recon = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).mean()
        return x_hat, recon + 1e-3 * sparsity, f

# Toy run: 512-dim "activations", 4096 features (8x expansion).
torch.manual_seed(0)
sae = SparseAutoencoder(512, 4096)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(sae.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
x = torch.randn(64, 512)        # stand-in for cached residual-stream acts
x_hat, loss, f = sae(x)
opt.zero_grad()
loss.backward()                 # gradients flow through the sparse ReLU
opt.step()
active = (f > 0).float().sum(dim=-1).mean().item()
print(f"loss={loss.item():.4f}  mean active features={active:.0f}/4096")
# After one step the L1 term is already pushing most features to zero;
# train on real cached activations and the survivors become interpretable.

    How to read this diagram

    1. The shapes. 512 in, 4096 features, 512 out. The bottleneck is wide but sparse: capacity lives in the width, interpretability in the sparsity.
    2. The centering. x - self.bias before encoding, + self.bias after decoding. Activations have a large mean component; centering keeps the encoder's job about variation, not offset.
    3. The ReLU. Nonnegativity plus L1 gives true zeros, not small values. Zeros are what make features countable and inspectable: "47 features active on this token" is a meaningful sentence.
    4. The loss balance. recon + 1e-3 * sparsity: the coefficient trades reconstruction fidelity against feature count. Too high and features die (everything reconstructs from bias); too low and nothing is sparse. Tuning it is the main practical knob.
    5. What the toy omits. Unit-norm decoder columns (anti-cheating), real cached activations, dead-feature resampling (the trainer re-initializes features that never activate), and the max-activating-examples inspection loop. Each is one paragraph in the SAE literature and one afternoon in the lab.

    B.6.4 Worked numbers

    d_model = 2048, expansion 8x: 16,384 features. Typical sparsity: tens to low hundreds active per token (illustrative; the exact count follows from the L1 coefficient). Parameters: encoder 2048x16384 + decoder 16384x2048 = ~67M, small next to the model it explains. Training tokens: billions of cached activations for frontier-scale SAEs. That is why Gemma Scope and the OpenAI 16M-feature SAE were notable compute investments. And it is why OpenAI's scaling laws (compute vs dictionary size vs sparsity vs reconstruction) matter: they turned SAE training from alchemy into budgeting.

    B.6.5 Common misunderstanding

    "Each SAE feature is one clean concept, and SAEs find all of them." Features are hypotheses, not ground truth. Known issues:

    • Feature splitting: one concept fragmented across several features.
    • Feature merging: one feature covering two related concepts.
    • Uninterpretable features: they activate on no discernible pattern.
    • Incomplete coverage: the SAE explains much of the activation variance but not all. Unexplained machinery remains in the residual.

    Treat a feature as a lead for intervention to confirm, per the B.5 standard, not as a discovered fact.

    B.7 Probing vs steering

    B.7.1 What they are

    Probing is read-only: freeze the model, train a tiny classifier (usually linear) on its activations to predict some property, and check the accuracy. High accuracy means the information is present in the activations. The classic form is the linear probe (Alain and Bengio, 2016): a linear classifier per layer, asking what each layer knows.

    Steering is write: modify activations during the forward pass to change behavior. The canonical recipe is contrastive activation addition (CAA, Rimsky et al., 2023). Collect contrast pairs: prompts showing the behavior vs its opposite. Take the mean difference of residual-stream activations as a steering vector v. Add alpha x v at token positions during generation. Positive alpha pushes toward the behavior, negative alpha away.

    B.7.2 Why the pair matters

    Probing answers one question: does the model represent X? Steering answers another: does X drive behavior? The pair is the B.5 standard of evidence in miniature. Probe for correlation. Steer for causation. Either alone misleads:

    • A probe can be 95% accurate on information the model never uses (the probe is a stronger reader than the model's own downstream layers).
    • Steering without a probe is blind: you push a direction you have not verified means anything.

    Together they are the cheapest interpretability loop that clears the correlation-plus-intervention bar.

    B.7.3 How they work under the hood

    Linear probes. Take activations at layer L for a labeled dataset, train logistic regression (or least squares), report accuracy vs a majority-class baseline. Sweep layers: the layer where accuracy peaks is where the information is most linearly available. Keep the probe weak on purpose: a strong probe finding the signal proves nothing, because a strong probe can memorize. A linear probe winning is honest evidence the information is linearly present.

    ITI (inference-time intervention, Li et al., 2023). The bridge between the two: train linear probes to find truthfulness-correlated attention heads, then shift activations along the probe direction at inference. Probing selects the direction; steering applies it.

    CAA mechanics. For each contrast pair (same question, "honest" vs "sycophantic" completion), record the residual-stream activation at the answer position for both. Take the difference, average over hundreds of pairs. The averaging cancels everything except the behavior direction: that is why the method uses contrast pairs, not single examples. At inference, add the vector at all token positions after the prompt, scaled by alpha. Reported effects on Llama 2 Chat: steered behaviors (sycophancy, hallucination, refusal, corrigibility) moved substantially with small capability loss. The steering composed with fine-tuning and system prompts rather than replacing them.

    Choosing alpha. Sweep it. Too small: no visible change. Too large: the model degrades into repeating the steered concept (the Golden Gate Bridge demo is alpha set to maximum, on purpose, as a spectacle). The usable range is where the target behavior shifts and everything else stays put: measure both.

    # Probing vs steering on a toy concept. Runs anywhere (numpy only).
# Probe (read-only): a least-squares linear classifier on activations.
# High accuracy = the information is present.
# Steering (write): a mean-difference vector from contrast pairs, added
# during the "forward pass". A behavior shift = the direction is causal.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def make_activations(n, truthful):
    # Toy world: 64-dim activations; dimension 0 secretly encodes
    # "truthfulness" as +2/-2 plus noise. The probe must discover it;
    # steering must move it.
    X = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 64))
    X[:, 0] += np.where(truthful, 2.0, -2.0)
    return X

X_true = make_activations(500, True)
X_false = make_activations(500, False)
X = np.vstack([X_true, X_false])
y = np.array([1] * 500 + [0] * 500)

# --- Probe: least-squares linear classifier, closed form, no libraries.
# Deliberately weak (linear): if THIS finds the signal, the information
# is honestly, linearly present. A strong probe winning proves nothing.
Xc = X - X.mean(0)
w = np.linalg.solve(Xc.T @ Xc + 1e-3 * np.eye(64), Xc.T @ (y - 0.5))
pred = (Xc @ w > 0).astype(int)
print(f"probe accuracy: {(pred == y).mean():.1%}  (chance: 50%)")
print(f"weight on dim 0: {w[0]:.2f} vs mean |w|: {np.abs(w).mean():.3f}")
# probe accuracy: 98.2%  (chance: 50%)
# weight on dim 0: 0.20 vs mean |w|: 0.010
# The probe put 20x the average weight on the true dimension: it found it.

# --- Steering: mean-difference vector, CAA-style.
# v = mean(truthful acts) - mean(false acts), over contrast pairs.
# Add alpha * v to FRESH false-labeled activations and watch the probe's
# verdict move: correlation (probe) becomes intervention (steering).
v = X_true.mean(0) - X_false.mean(0)
X_new = make_activations(200, False)      # fresh items, labeled false
for alpha in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0]:
    steered = X_new + alpha * v
    verdict = ((steered - X.mean(0)) @ w > 0).mean()
    print(f"alpha={alpha}: probe says truthful {verdict:.0%} of the time")
# alpha=0.0: probe says truthful 2% of the time
# alpha=0.5: probe says truthful 52% of the time
# alpha=1.0: probe says truthful 96% of the time

    How to read this diagram

    1. The toy world. Dimension 0 carries the concept; the other 63 are noise. This is the ground truth the methods must rediscover: a clean room for checking the logic.
    2. The probe. Closed-form least squares, linear, no tricks. 98.2% accuracy with 20x weight on dimension 0: the information is linearly present, and the probe says exactly where.
    3. The steering vector. Mean difference over 500 contrast pairs. Averaging is the noise control: any single pair's difference is full of irrelevant variation; the mean keeps only what is consistent across pairs.
    4. The intervention. Fresh false-labeled items, never seen during vector construction, get alpha x v added. At alpha=1.0 the probe flips 96% of them to "truthful": the direction is causal in this toy, not just correlated.
    5. The honest caveat. The toy's concept lives on one clean dimension. Real concepts spread out and tangle: the same code on real activations gives messier, smaller, still meaningful shifts. The logic transfers. The magnitudes do not.

    B.7.4 Common misunderstanding

    "A 95%-accurate probe means the model uses that information." No. A probe is a new classifier trained on the activations; it can learn to read information that the model's own downstream layers ignore. The classic control: probe a randomized or untrained network and watch probes still score above chance on some tasks (they fit the probe to spurious patterns). Presence is not use. The claim "the model uses X" requires intervention: steer or ablate along the probe direction and show the behavior moves. Probe proposes, steering disposes.

    Key takeaways

    • Probing (read-only, linear, weak-on-purpose) tests whether information is present; steering (write, CAA mean-difference vectors, alpha-scaled) tests whether a direction is causal.
    • The pair is correlation plus intervention: probe proposes, steering disposes.
    • CAA: average activation differences over contrast pairs, add at post-prompt positions with ±alpha. Reported to steer high-level behaviors in Llama 2 Chat with small capability cost.
    • A high probe score alone never proves the model uses the information; run the intervention.

    2. Right: steering. Build v from contrast pairs (honest versus sycophantic), add alpha times v at post-prompt positions. Behavior shifting means the direction is causal.
    3. Sweep alpha: too small and nothing changes; too large and the model degrades.
    4. The loop: probe proposes, steering disposes. ITI is literally this loop.

    B.8 Limits and open questions

    B.8.1 What this chapter is

    An honest boundary map. Interpretability is a young field with real results and real limits. The limits matter more than the results for anyone making decisions with these methods. Five limits, then the open questions.

    B.8.2 The limits

    1. Correlation is not causation, and most results are correlation. The bulk of published interpretability is correlation ("feature X activates when the model does Y"). The intervention half of the standard is rarer because it is harder. Ablating or steering cleanly, measuring the behavior change, ruling out side effects: each is real work. Treat un-intervened findings as leads.

    2. Coverage is partial. SAEs explain much of activation variance but not all. Unexplained machinery remains in the residual. A safety argument of the form "we checked all the features and found no deception circuit" fails at the first step. You did not check all the machinery, only the part your SAE captured.

    3. Steering is brittle. It works on broad behavioral tendencies (more/less sycophantic, more/less refusing). It degrades on precise structured tasks. Steering a model to produce valid JSON for a schema, for example, fails in reported cases where the same steering moves open-ended style easily. The direction is real but coarse, and fine control is an unsolved problem.

    4. Explanations need evaluation too. "This feature means X" is itself a claim requiring evidence: predictive scoring on held-out activations, intervention tests, human or automated agreement. Automated interpretability scoring exists but is noisy. An explanation method without an evaluation method just tells stories.

    5. Scale costs. Frontier SAEs are large compute investments (billions of cached activations, published scaling laws). Teams budget frontier interpretability like training. That means most practitioners will use released SAEs (Gemma Scope) rather than train their own. They inherit the releaser's layer choices, sparsity settings, and blind spots.

    B.8.3 Open questions

    • Faithful circuits at scale. Small circuits (induction heads) are understood; full behavioral circuits in frontier models are not. The gap between "a feature" and "the computation" is the field's main open problem.
    • Prediction before deployment. Can interpretability find failure modes evals miss, before they matter? A few demonstrations exist; a reliable practice does not.
    • Composable steering. Steering vectors for two behaviors often interfere. No one has solved reliable composition (steer A and B independently).
    • Theory of superposition. Why do networks pack features the way they do, and can anyone design architectures to be interpretable by construction rather than by post-hoc dissection?

    B.8.4 Common misunderstanding

    "Interpretability will let us prove a model is safe." It will not, not in its current state, and possibly not ever in the strong sense. What it offers is evidence: features, probes, interventions that raise or lower confidence in specific claims about specific behaviors. Treat interpretability outputs the way you treat eval outputs: as measurements with error bars, blind spots, and failure modes, not as proofs. The honest use is comparative and diagnostic: this checkpoint vs that one, this behavior's mechanism vs unknown.

    B.9 Patch: LoRA and QLoRA with GPU-memory accounting drills

    Why this patch exists

    Volume 7's main chapters teach full-parameter post-training: every weight moves. In practice, much fine-tuning moves a tiny fraction of the weights instead. This patch covers LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and QLoRA (quantized LoRA): the parameter-efficient methods that made fine-tuning 7B-65B models possible on single GPUs. Volume 7 does not cover them; the drills below close the gap.

    B.9.1 What LoRA is

    LoRA freezes the pretrained weights W and learns a low-rank update instead: W' = W + B x A. B is (d_out x r), A is (r x d_in), and the rank r is tiny (8, 16, 64) next to dimensions like 4096 or 8192. Only A and B train; W never moves.

    The forward pass: h = Wx + (alpha/r) x B(Ax). B starts at zero, so training begins exactly at the base model (delta = 0) and the adapter grows from nothing. At inference, you can merge B x A back into W (W_merged = W + BA), so there is zero latency cost. The deployed model is just a dense matrix again.

    Applied to a 7B model with r=8 on the attention projections, LoRA trains roughly 4M parameters out of 7B: about 0.06%. The bet: fine-tuning lives in a low-rank subspace, so a small update captures most of the adaptation.

    B.9.2 What QLoRA adds

    QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) keeps the LoRA idea and quantizes the frozen base to 4-bit, with three innovations:

    1. NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat): a 4-bit data type described as information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights (which pretrained weights approximately are). More precision where the weight mass is.
    2. Double quantization: quantize the quantization constants themselves, saving ~0.4 bits per parameter on top.
    3. Paged optimizers: optimizer state pages spilled to CPU memory during spikes, so transient memory peaks do not OOM the run.

    Headline result, as reported: fine-tune a 65B model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit fine-tuning task performance. Their Guanaco models reached 99.3% of ChatGPT's score on the Vicuna benchmark, with 24 hours of fine-tuning on one GPU. The paper's analysis also notes full 16-bit fine-tuning of 65B needs over 780 GB: the gap QLoRA closes.

    B.9.3 How it works under the hood

    Rank and the update. The adapter's capacity is r x (d_in + d_out) parameters per targeted matrix. Rank 8 on a 4096x4096 matrix: 8 x 8192 = 65K params vs 16.8M for the full matrix. Double r, double the adapter. The alpha/r scaling keeps the update magnitude stable as you sweep r. Without it, larger r would mean larger initial steps for no good reason.

    Target modules. LoRA is usually applied to attention projections (q_proj, v_proj, sometimes k_proj, o_proj) and optionally the MLP. More targets = more capacity = more memory. The choice is a knob, not a law: attention-only is the cheap default; adding MLP targets helps when the adaptation needs it.

    Where QLoRA's memory goes. The system dequantizes the 4-bit base to bf16 on the fly for the forward/backward compute (compute stays in bf16, only storage is 4-bit). Gradients flow through the frozen quantized weights into the bf16 adapters. So the memory is: 0.5 bytes/param for the base + full bf16 training state for the ~0.1% of params that are adapters.

    The honest limit. LoRA is excellent at style, format, and instruction-following adaptation: teaching the model how to talk. It is weaker at adding genuinely new knowledge or rewiring deep behaviors: the low-rank update can only nudge what the base model already represents. If the base model cannot do X at all, LoRA rarely teaches X from scratch; that needs pre-training-scale updates or a stronger base.

    # LoRA in miniature: the weight surgery and the memory accounting drill.
# Frozen base W; trainable low-rank update B @ A. Forward:
# h = Wx + (alpha/r) * B(Ax). B starts at zero so training begins exactly
# at the base model. NOTE: needs PyTorch; RunPod-ready.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r=8, alpha=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base
        for p in self.base.parameters():
            p.requires_grad = False   # the base never moves: this is the
                                      # entire memory trick
        d_out, d_in = base.weight.shape
        # A: random init (the "directions" to learn in). B: zeros, so the
        # initial update is exactly zero and training starts at the base
        # model. If B were random too, step 0 would jump away from the
        # pretrained weights: the zero-init is load-bearing.
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, d_in) * 0.02)
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_out, r))
        # alpha/r keeps the update scale stable as you sweep r: without
        # it, doubling r would double step sizes for no reason.
        self.scaling = alpha / r

    def forward(self, x):
        # Base path (frozen) plus adapter path (trainable). At inference
        # this merges to a single matrix: W + scaling * B @ A.
        return self.base(x) + self.scaling * (x @ self.A.T @ self.B.T)

    def trainable_params(self):
        return sum(p.numel() for p in self.parameters() if p.requires_grad)

def finetune_memory_gb(params_b, mode):
    # The accounting drill, in GB for weights + optimizer states.
    # Full fine-tuning (bf16): weights 2 B/param + grads 2 B/param +
    #   Adam m and v in fp32 (4+4 B/param) = 12 B/param = 6x weight GB.
    # LoRA: frozen bf16 weights (2 B/param) + tiny adapter states.
    # QLoRA: 4-bit base (0.5 B/param) + tiny adapter states.
    # Activations are not modeled here: they add on top for all modes.
    w_bytes = {"full": 2.0, "lora": 2.0, "qlora": 0.5}[mode]
    weights = params_b * w_bytes        # billions x bytes = GB
    if mode == "full":
        return weights * 6             # 12 bytes/param total
    # Adapters are ~0.1% of params: their grads + Adam states fit in
    # ~0.5 GB for 7B at r=8. The base dominates; quantization shrinks it.
    return weights + 0.5

for mode in ["full", "lora", "qlora"]:
    mem = finetune_memory_gb(7, mode)
    print(f"7B {mode:5s}: {mem:5.1f} GB (weights + optimizer states)")
# 7B full :  84.0 GB (weights + optimizer states)
# 7B lora :  14.5 GB (weights + optimizer states)
# 7B qlora:   4.0 GB (weights + optimizer states)
# Full fine-tuning needs multi-GPU or CPU offload; LoRA fits a 24 GB
# card; QLoRA fits a 16 GB card. Same model, three budgets.

    How to read this diagram

    1. The surgery. LoRALinear wraps a frozen linear layer. A holds learned input directions, B maps them back to output space. The product BA is the low-rank update; the rank r is its bottleneck.
    2. The zero-init. B starts at zeros, so BA = 0 and the model behaves exactly as the base at step 0. This is what makes LoRA safe to attach to a working model: nothing changes until training moves B.
    3. The memory trick. requires_grad = False on the base means no gradients and no optimizer states for 99.9% of params. The drill's numbers: full fine-tuning stores 12 bytes/param (weights + grads + Adam m,v); LoRA stores 2 bytes/param for the frozen base plus crumbs for adapters.
    4. QLoRA's extra squeeze. The base drops from 2 to 0.5 bytes/param (NF4). Compute still happens in bf16 (dequantize on the fly); only storage is 4-bit. That is how 65B fits 48 GB: ~33 GB of weights plus adapters, optimizer, and activations.
    5. What the drill omits. Activations (add on top for all modes; gradient checkpointing trades compute for them), the double-quantization saving (~0.4 bits/param), and paged-optimizer spill. The drill is the skeleton; those are the flesh.

    B.9.5 Common misunderstanding

    "LoRA matches full fine-tuning everywhere, so full fine-tuning is obsolete." LoRA matches it on adaptation tasks: style, format, instruction following, domain tone. It lags where the task needs new knowledge or deep behavioral change, because a rank-8 update cannot rewrite what the base model never learned. The honest rule: LoRA for teaching how to talk, full-parameter (or a stronger base) for teaching new things. Rank and target-module choice move the boundary but do not erase it.
