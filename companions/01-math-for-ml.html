Unit 1 · The map

Chapter 1 - Why math first: the map

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 1

1.1 One volume, four pillars. Machine learning wears a software costume. Remove the frameworks and four pillars remain. Linear algebra is the language where data lives: vectors, matrices, and transformations. Probability and statistics is the language of uncertainty: every model output is a bet. Calculus is the language of change: the field shows how fast a loss moves when a weight moves. Optimization turns calculus into a train loop: walk downhill, one step at a time. Information theory sits on top and gives meaning to the loss.

1.2 Which math each later volume builds on. The map names a real dependency for each arrow. Linear algebra feeds Volumes 4, 6, and 8: attention scores, KV cache sizing, and tensor parallelism. Probability and statistics feed Volumes 2, 7, and 11: eval metrics, reward signals, and ablation confidence. Calculus and optimization feed Volumes 3, 5, and 6: backprop is the chain rule, and every optimizer is gradient descent with memory. Information theory feeds Volume 7: the DPO loss rests on a KL divergence penalty.

1.3 How to study this volume. The plan is one chapter a day at four hours a day. Run every lab. Type the code by hand. Break one variable at a time and watch the result. The autograd lab in Chapter 8 is the first link of the build thread that runs through Volumes 1 to 8. The chart below shows the study plan minutes for this volume.

Unit 2 · Linear algebra

Chapter 2 - Linear algebra from zero

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 2

2.1 Vectors. Vectors are arrows with arithmetic. A vector is an ordered list of numbers and an arrow from the origin to a point. Addition adds the entries that match. A scalar product stretches, shrinks, or flips the arrow. A word embedding is a vector of 4096 entries. The norm of u = [3, 1] is the square root of 10, about 3.16, and the unit vector in the same direction is about [0.949, 0.316].

2.2 Dot products: similarity as geometry. The dot product sums the entry products and equals the product of the norms times the cosine of the angle. For u = [3, 1] and v = [1, 2] the dot product is 5 and the cosine is about 0.707, so the angle is about 45 degrees. Attention scores are dot products between a query vector and key vectors. Raw dot products grow with dimension, so attention divides by the square root of d before the softmax.

2.3 Matrices as transformations of space. A matrix moves every vector at one time and keeps grid lines straight and the origin fixed. The columns of a matrix show where the basis vectors land, and that fact fixes the whole map. Composition is multiplication, and order matters: AB is not BA in general. The determinant is the volume scale factor. A determinant of zero flattens space and kills the inverse.

2.4 Rank: the number of dimensions that survive. Rank counts the independent columns. The matrix with rows [1, 2] and [2, 4] has rank 1 because column two is two times column one, and every output lands on one line. A chain of maps cannot create dimensions, so the rank of a product caps at the smaller rank of the factors. LoRA rests on this fact: a fine-tune update can live in a low-rank subspace with rank 8 or 16.

2.5 Eigendecomposition: the favorite directions of the transformation. An eigenvector only stretches under the map: Mv equals lambda times v. For the matrix with rows [3, 1] and [0, 2] the eigenvalues are 3 and 2, and repeated use of the map grows the lambda 3 direction faster and faster. The largest eigenvalue magnitude rules iterated systems. A recurrent weight with spectral radius above 1 explodes gradients, and a radius below 1 makes gradients vanish.

2.6 SVD: the master decomposition. SVD factors any matrix as a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and a second rotation. The singular values price each dimension. The Eckart-Young theorem states that the top k singular values give the best rank-k approximation, and the error equals the sum of the squares of the dropped values. For the diagonal matrix with entries 3 and 1, the rank-1 fit drops a dimension that holds 1 of 10 parts of the squared energy, 10 percent.

Unit 3 · Probability

Chapter 3 - Probability from zero

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 3

3.1 Sample spaces, events, and the rules. The sample space lists every outcome. An event is a subset of the sample space. Three rules do the work: the complement rule, the addition rule with the overlap removed, and the multiplication rule with a conditional probability. Two fair coins give four outcomes at 1/4 each. The event first coin heads has probability 1/2, and the event at least one heads has probability 3/4.

3.2 Random variables and distributions. A random variable is a number with an uncertain value. A discrete variable uses a mass function, and a continuous variable uses a density, where probability is area under the curve. The core list is Bernoulli, Binomial, Uniform, Normal, and Categorical. A language model output is a categorical distribution over the vocabulary, about 50,000 numbers that sum to 1. The normal curve puts about 68 percent of mass within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95 percent within two.

3.3 Expectation and variance: the two numbers that summarize a distribution. Expectation is the long-run average. Variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Expectation stays linear with no independence condition. The variance of an average of n independent draws is the variance of one draw divided by n. A fair die has mean 3.5 and variance about 2.917. The average of 100 rolls has variance about 0.0292, so the average pins near 3.5. Correlated draws break the 1/n rule: near-duplicate eval items keep variance high.

3.4 Bayes theorem: update beliefs with evidence. Bayes turns the probability of evidence given a hypothesis into the probability of the hypothesis given the evidence: posterior equals likelihood times prior, divided by evidence. The worked case sets prevalence at 1 percent, sensitivity at 0.90, and the false positive rate at 0.05. The numerator is 0.90 times 0.01, equal to 0.009. The denominator is 0.009 plus 0.05 times 0.99, equal to 0.0585. The posterior is about 0.154, near 15 percent. Most positive results come from healthy people because healthy people outnumber sick people 99 to 1.

15%posterior chance of sickness after a positive test
Nugget 3 · The base rate rules the test From the lesson Section 3.4 of the volume
The lesson

A positive result on a test with 90 percent sensitivity and a 5 percent false positive rate means sickness only about 15 percent of the time at 1 percent prevalence.

Why it matters. Most positive results come from the healthy group because that group is 99 times larger. A test score alone never answers a belief question.

Transfer. Ask for the base rate before any conditional claim. No base rate, no posterior, no verdict.

Unit 4 · Calculus

Chapter 4 - Calculus for ML

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 4

4.0 Integrals. Area under the curve. An integral adds an infinite count of thin slices under a curve. Probability for a continuous variable is area under the density, and the total area under any density is 1. Expectation is the same area with each slice at a weight equal to its value. For the Uniform case on 0 to 2 the density is 1/2, the total area is 2 times 1/2, equal to 1, and the mean is the midpoint 1.

4.1 Derivatives: the speedometer. The derivative is the limit of secant slopes as the gap shrinks. For x squared near x = 1 the slopes run 2.1, 2.01, and 2.001 and settle at 2. The sign of the derivative gives the downhill direction, and the size gives the steepness. At a flat point the derivative is zero, and the point can be a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle.

4.2 The chain rule: the most important rule in deep learning. A nested function takes the product of the local slopes. For Y equal to the square of (3X + 1), the total slope is 2 times (3X + 1) times 3, equal to 18X + 6. The one-neuron case in the source gives a gradient near minus 0.212 for W, so a step up in W cuts the loss. Backpropagation is this rule plus bookkeeping: the forward pass stores values, and the backward pass multiplies local slopes.

4.3 Gradients: the derivative in many dimensions. The gradient packs one partial derivative per input into one vector. The gradient points in the steepest uphill direction and stands perpendicular to the level curves. For f(x, y) equal to x squared plus 2 y squared, the gradient at (1, 1) is [2, 4]. The y part is double the x part, so plain descent zigzags in a narrow valley, and per-coordinate rescale rules exist for that reason.

4.4 Jacobians and Hessians: the shape of the map. The Jacobian is the matrix of all partial derivatives of a vector map, and each layer backward pass multiplies by one Jacobian. The Hessian holds the second derivatives and reads the local shape: all eigenvalues positive means a bowl, and mixed signs mean a saddle. For x squared plus 2 y squared the Hessian eigenvalues are 2 and 4, and the condition number is 2. A network with a billion parameters would need a billion by billion Hessian, so first-order rules rule practice.

0.25cap on one sigmoid slope factor
Nugget 4 · The 0.25 cap From the lesson Section 4.2 of the volume
The lesson

Unit 5 · Statistics

Chapter 5 - Statistics: learning from data

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 5

5.1 Estimators. Estimators are rules for guessing from samples. An estimator turns data into a guess at an unknown parameter, and the guess varies from sample to sample. Judge an estimator by its sampling distribution: bias is the average miss, and variance is the spread of guesses. A coin with true bias 0.6 and 10 flips gives an unbiased guess with standard deviation about 0.155, so one small experiment lands far from the truth as a routine event.

5.2 Bias and variance of estimators: the trade-off. Expected squared error splits into bias squared plus variance, and the cross term vanishes. More data shrinks variance. More capacity shrinks bias and grows variance. The source case shows the sting: a constant guess of 0.5 for a 0.6 coin totals 0.01 error, and the unbiased sample mean totals 0.024. Deliberate bias can win on small data, and that fact is the root of regularization.

5.3 MLE and MAP: two ways to fit parameters. Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the data most probable. MAP adds a prior and picks the parameters that are most probable given the data and the prior. Three heads in three flips give an MLE of 1.0 and a MAP value of 0.8 under a Beta(2,2) prior. A Gaussian prior gives L2 regularization, and a Laplace prior gives L1 regularization.

5.4 Hypothesis testing and p-values. A test states a null hypothesis of no effect and asks how surprising the data look under that null. The p-value is that surprise measure. A result at p below 0.05 rejects the null under the standard cutoff. Model B wins 58 of 100 items: z is 1.6 and p is about 0.11, so the win is not significant. The same rate at 580 of 1000 gives z about 5.06 and p about 0.0000004. Sample size is power.

5.5 Confidence intervals. An interval reports a range in place of one number. The standard form is estimate plus or minus z times the standard error, with z equal to 1.96 for 95 percent. An accuracy of 0.82 on 200 items has standard error about 0.027 and an interval of about 0.767 to 0.873. The 95 percent figure names the long-run hit rate of the method across repeats. The value does not name a chance for one fixed interval.

Transfer. Quote the count with every rate. A rate with no count hides the power of the test.

Unit 6 · Optimization

Chapter 6 - Optimization from first principles

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 6

6.1 Gradient descent, traced by hand. The update moves each parameter against the gradient, at a scale set by the learning rate eta. On f(x) = x squared from x = 4 with eta = 0.3, the trace runs 4, 1.6, 0.64, 0.256, 0.102, and 0.041. Each step multiplies x by 0.4. Eta = 1.1 flips the sign and grows the size, so the walk diverges. Eta = 0.05 converges at one tenth of the speed.

6.2 SGD: the gradient is a noisy estimate. A minibatch gradient has no bias: its average equals the full gradient, and its variance scales as 1 over batch size. Three batch gradients in the source average to [2.03, 0.1] against a true gradient of [2.0, 0.1]. The noise helps escape sharp minima and shallow saddles, and the same noise stops the walk from a full settle near the optimum, so the plan decays eta over time.

6.3 Momentum: Give the ball mass. Momentum keeps a velocity, an exponential average of past gradients with beta near 0.9. Parts across a valley cancel in the average, and parts along the valley add up. In a steady direction the velocity grows to about ten times the raw gradient, so eta drops to match. Nesterov momentum reads the gradient at the lookahead point and cuts the overshoot.

6.4 Adam, derived step by step. Adam keeps two averages per parameter: m for the gradient mean and v for the squared gradient scale. Bias correction divides each average by one minus beta to the power t, because both averages start at zero. For a steady gradient the step lands near eta times the sign of the gradient. For a noisy gradient that flips sign, the mean stays near zero and Adam shrinks the step about tenfold. AdamW moves weight decay out of the gradient and applies decay to the weight in a direct step.

6.5 Convexity: when optimization is easy. A convex function keeps every chord above the graph, and every local minimum of a convex function is the global minimum. Linear regression and logistic regression are convex. Deep networks are not convex, and their stationary points are saddles far more often than bad minima. Practice works because good minima are common and because SGD noise plus momentum escape saddle plateaus.

6.6 Learning-rate schedules: the step-size plan. Warmup ramps eta from zero across the first steps, because early gradients are large and chaotic. Cosine decay then lowers eta in a smooth curve to a floor near 10 percent of the peak. The worked plan runs 100,000 steps with peak rate 0.0003, a warmup of 2,000 steps, and a floor of 0.00003. At step 50,000 the rate sits near 0.000165.

Step-through: three steps of gradient descent

Step 1: start at x = 4 on f(x) = x squared. The slope is 8. With eta = 0.3 the step is 0.3 times 8, and x moves to 1.6.

Step 2: at x = 1.6 the slope is 3.2. The step is 0.3 times 3.2, and x moves to 0.64.

Step 3: at x = 0.64 the slope is 1.28. The step is 0.3 times 1.28, and x moves to 0.256.

Each step multiplies x by 0.4. The walk nears the minimum at x = 0 and never overshoots.

10xstep shrink for a sign-flip gradient under Adam
Nugget 6 · Adam damps the noisy coordinate From the lesson Section 6.4 of the volume
The lesson

For a gradient that flips sign between steps, the mean stays near zero and Adam shrinks the step about tenfold.

Why it matters. Per-coordinate scale is the reason one global step size fails across layers with different gradient sizes. Adam prices each coordinate on its own record.

Transfer. Sort parameters by signal steadiness in a debug pass. Wild coordinates need small steps, and Adam applies that rule per coordinate.

Unit 7 · Information theory

Chapter 7 - Information theory: the loss function of language models

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 7

7.1 Entropy: How surprised should you be? Entropy is expected surprise in bits: the entropy of p equals minus the sum of p(x) times log base 2 of p(x). A fair coin holds 1 bit. A coin with heads at 0.9 holds about 0.469 bits. A four-side die with probabilities [0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125] holds 1.75 bits, below the 2-bit cap for four outcomes. The log form makes surprise add across independent events.

7.2 Cross-entropy: pay for wrong beliefs. Cross-entropy scores a model distribution q against the truth p. For a one-hot target the sum falls to one term: minus log of the probability on the true token. A model that gives the true token 0.7 pays about 0.357 nats. A uniform model over four tokens pays about 1.386 nats. A model that gives the true token 0.01 pays about 4.605 nats. Confident error has no cap on the penalty.

7.3 KL divergence (how far one probability distribution sits from another): the extra cost of being wrong. Cross-entropy splits into the entropy of the truth plus the KL divergence. Train work can shrink only the KL part. KL is directional. For p = [0.5, 0.5] and q = [0.9, 0.1], the KL from p to q is about 0.737 bits and the KL from q to p is about 0.531 bits. RLHF uses a KL penalty to hold a tuned policy near a reference model, and Volume 7 derives DPO from that penalty.

The lesson

For p = [0.5, 0.5] and q = [0.9, 0.1], KL from p to q is about 0.737 bits and KL from q to p is about 0.531 bits.

Why it matters. The direction decides which errors pay the most. A flip of the two terms in an RL penalty punishes the wrong mistakes.

Chapter 8 - Capstone labs

From the lesson 01-math-for-ml.html, chapter 8

8.1 Lab: autograd from scratch. The lab builds a scalar Value engine with a forward pass that records the graph and a backward pass that walks the graph in reverse order. Three checks agree to 8 decimal places: the engine gives dL/dw equal to minus 0.40049735, the hand chain rule gives the same value, and finite differences give the same value. Two hundred steps at eta 0.5 move the loss from 0.0568 to 0.000126. A wrong local derivative corrupts every upstream gradient in silence, so the triple check carries the weight.

8.2 Lab: Adam versus SGD on a toy landscape. Both optimizers run 400 steps from the same start with the same noise seed on a narrow curved valley with a second basin. SGD at rate 0.002 ends at f = 0.1529. Adam at rate 0.05 ends at f = 1.2144 near the second basin. SGD at rate 0.02 diverges to a value near 1.7 times 10 to the power 49. The lab lesson is narrow and honest: optimizer rank on a toy problem does not transfer to transformers.
