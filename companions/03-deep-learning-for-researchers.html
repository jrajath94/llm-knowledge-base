Unit 1 · The neuron

Chapter 1: The Neuron

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 1

1.1 What a neuron computes. A neuron is the smallest learnable unit. The neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds the products plus one bias, and passes the sum through an activation. The worked neuron takes inputs [2.0, -1.0, 0.5], weights [0.8, -0.4, 1.2], and bias 0.3. The sum is 1.6 + 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.3 = 2.9, and ReLU passes 2.9 as the output. Multiply, add, bend: every neuron in every network runs this one computation.

1.2 Why depth needs the nonlinearity. Two linear layers in a row compute one matrix product, so a stack of linear layers collapses to a single layer. A hundred linear layers hold the power of one. The activation breaks the collapse with a bend. ReLU bends at zero: negatives turn to zero and positives pass. Each neuron adds one kink, and the network builds curves from kinks.

1.3 What the weights store. All knowledge in a trained network lives in the weights and biases. No other memory exists. A large language model is a few hundred billion of these numbers in layers. Chapter 3 measures the error of the current numbers. Chapter 4 assigns blame to each number. Chapter 6 moves the numbers downhill. The chart below shows the study plan minutes for this volume.

Unit 2 · Layers

Chapter 2: Layers and the Forward Pass

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 2

2.1 From one neuron to a layer. A layer is many neurons side by side, all on the same inputs, each with a row of weights. The weight rows form one matrix W, the biases form one vector b, and the layer computes z = W x + b with the activation on each entry. A hidden layer sits between input and output, and its activations are features that later layers read.

2.2 The forward pass, end to end. The forward pass feeds the input through each layer in turn to the output. The worked 3-2-1 network takes input [1.0, 0.5, -1.0]. Hidden neuron one gives z = 0.2 and hidden neuron two gives z = 0.7, and ReLU passes both values. The output layer gives 0.9 times 0.2 plus 0.6 times 0.7, equal to 0.6. Inference is this same pass with the weights frozen.

2.3 Why it is all matrix multiplication. A layer is a matrix product plus an elementwise bend, and a deep network is a chain of such products with kinks between them. The uniform shape is the engineering fact of the field: the whole model reduces to the operation GPUs run fastest. Scale grows the matrices and adds layers, and the computation stays the same.

Unit 3 · Loss

Chapter 3: Loss: How the Network Knows It Is Wrong

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 3

3.1 One number for "how wrong". A loss function turns a prediction and its answer into one number. Regression predicts a number and uses mean squared error: predictions [2.0, 3.0, 4.0] against targets [3.0, 3.0, 6.0] give squared errors [1.0, 0.0, 4.0] and a mean of about 1.67. Classification picks a class and uses cross-entropy: minus the log of the probability on the true class. A true-class probability of 0.7 gives a loss of 0.357, and a probability of 0.1 gives 2.303.

3.2 The loss landscape. The loss as a surface over the weight space: each point is one full weight setting, and the height is the loss. The surface is non-convex, with many valleys, flat plateaus, and sharp cliffs. Train work walks downhill on this surface. A minimum traps the walk in every direction. A saddle holds a zero gradient and still offers a downhill exit, and high-dimensional losses hold far more saddles than minima.

44xpenalty ratio of a 0.01 miss to a 0.9 hit
Nugget 2 · The confident miss pays 44 times From the lesson Chapter 3 of the volume
The lesson

Cross-entropy charges 0.105 for a true-class probability of 0.9 and 4.605 for a probability of 0.01, about 44 times the penalty.

Why it matters. The steep price on confident error is the pressure that repairs bad beliefs first. A flat penalty would waste steps on small errors.

Transfer. Read a loss as a price list. Ask which errors pay the most, and check that the price matches the cost in the product.

Unit 4 · Backprop

Chapter 4: Backpropagation

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 4

4.1 Blame assignment. The loss depends on the output, the output depends on the last layer, and the chain runs back to the first weights. The gradient holds one derivative per weight and points uphill. The chain rule splits a derivative through the middle steps: dL/dw equals dL/dy times dy/dw. Each factor is local, so blame flows backward one multiplication per hop.

4.2 The backward pass, worked. The tiny graph sets x = 2, w = 3, and target t = 4. Forward: y = 6 and L = 4. Backward: dL/dy = 2 times (6 - 4) = 4, and dy/dw = x = 2, so dL/dw = 8. The gradient is positive, so w must decrease. One backward pass yields every gradient at one time, where a forward method would need one pass per weight.

4.3 Gradient descent: the update. The update rule sets the new w to w minus eta times dL/dw. On L = w squared with eta = 0.3 from w = 4.0, step one gives 1.6 and step two gives 0.64. Practice runs the gradient on mini-batches in place of the full dataset, and the name for that method is SGD. One epoch is one full pass over the train data. An optimizer is the rule that turns gradients into updates, and plain gradient descent is the simplest rule.

Why it matters. Each factor is local, so one backward sweep prices every weight at one time. A forward method would need one pass per weight.

Unit 5 · Activations

Chapter 5: Activation Functions

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 5

5.1 The four you will meet. Sigmoid squashes any input into the range 0 to 1. Tanh squashes into the range minus 1 to 1 and centers on zero. ReLU is max(0, x): positives pass and negatives turn to zero. Softmax turns raw scores, named logits, into probabilities that sum to 1. Logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1] exponentiate to [7.389, 2.718, 1.105] with sum 11.212, and the probabilities read [0.66, 0.24, 0.10]. Softmax plus cross-entropy is the standard output pair for a classifier.

5.2 Why ReLU won, and what it costs. The ReLU derivative is 1 on the positive side and 0 on the negative side, so gradients pass at full strength through live neurons. Sigmoid and tanh saturate at the extremes, the derivative falls near zero, and a 20-layer sigmoid stack shrinks the gradient about 4 times per layer, to roughly a trillion times smaller at layer 20. The cost sits on the negative side: a dead ReLU takes only negative inputs, outputs zero on every input, and receives no gradient, so the neuron never recovers.

4xgradient shrink per sigmoid layer
Nugget 4 · Fourfold shrink per layer From the lesson Section 5.2 of the volume
The lesson

Each sigmoid layer shrinks the gradient up to 4 times, so a 20-layer sigmoid stack leaves a gradient roughly a trillion times smaller.

Why it matters. Saturation compounds through depth. The bend choice in one layer sets the fate of gradients in every layer below it.

Transfer. Multiply the per-layer shrink across the depth of a stack before a build. A dead gradient shows in the product early.

Unit 6 · Optimizers

Chapter 6: Optimizers

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 6

6.1 Adam, decoupled: the AdamW story. Adam joins two ideas: momentum averages past gradients so steady directions gain speed and sideways moves cancel, and adaptivity scales each step by the gradient history of that parameter. In plain Adam, weight decay enters as L2 inside the gradient and then passes through the adaptive divide, so each parameter decays at a rate the user never set. AdamW decouples the two moves: the Adam step comes from the pure gradient, and a direct shrink of eta times lambda times w follows.

6.2 Tuning and schedules: warmup and decay. Warmup ramps the learning rate from near zero across the first steps, because random weights give large and chaotic gradients and the Adam moments need time to settle. Decay then lowers the rate, most often on a cosine curve, so late steps squeeze the last quality near a minimum. Lab 3.3 Part A races four rules on a stiff quadratic for 500 steps: SGD ends at 2.338e-02, SGD with momentum at 3.912e-14, Adam at 1.868e-20, and AdamW at 4.200e-03. AdamW trails here because decay is a regularizer and this problem has no overfit to punish.

6.3 Lab 3.3: the optimizer laboratory. Part A is an optimization story: one valley 100 times steeper in one direction than the other, and Adam crosses fastest. Part B is a generalization story: same data, same decay 0.1, same 2000 steps, and only the placement of the decay moves. Adam with L2 in the gradient reaches test MSE 0.2103, and AdamW reaches 0.1007. A reader who mixes the two stories builds optimizer myths, so the lab keeps the verdicts apart.

Step-through: one AdamW update

Step 1: the state. One weight w = 1.0, with bias-corrected momentum m_hat = 0.5 and bias-corrected scale v_hat = 0.25. The rate eta = 0.01 and the decay lambda = 0.1.

Step 2: the Adam step. Eta times m_hat over the square root of v_hat: 0.01 times 0.5 over 0.5, equal to 0.01.

Step 3: the decay step. Eta times lambda times w: 0.01 times 0.1 times 1.0, equal to 0.001.

Step 4: the new weight. 1.0 minus 0.01 minus 0.001, equal to 0.989. Each weight decays by the same eta times lambda per step, apart from its gradient history.

Transfer. Test optimizer claims with one variable moved at a time, and judge on test error, not on the loss curve alone.

Unit 7 · Normalization

Chapter 7: Normalization

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 7

7.1 BatchNorm, LayerNorm, RMSNorm: three answers to one problem. Signal scale drifts through a deep stack, and normalization re-centers and re-scales activations at each layer. BatchNorm takes mean and variance for each feature across the batch and uses run averages at test time. LayerNorm takes the statistics across the features of one example, so train and test behave the same. RMSNorm drops the mean step and divides by the root-mean-square alone, and the source reports a cost cut of 7 to 64 percent for the norm step. The worked values [2, 4, 6] have mean 4 and standard deviation about 1.633, and the normalized values read [-1.22, 0, 1.22].

7.2 Pre-norm vs post-norm: where the norm sits. Post-norm applies the norm after the residual addition, so the main stream passes through a norm at every layer. Pre-norm applies the norm inside the branch, before the sublayer, and the residual stream stays a clean path for gradients. Deep post-norm stacks often need warmup and care at the start. Pre-norm trains in a far wider stable region, and modern LLMs use pre-norm for that reason. Post-norm can reach a small edge in final quality when a run stays stable.

7.3 Lab 3.1: the normalization shootout. A 16-layer MLP trains 300 steps four ways. No normalization stalls at loss 3.33. BatchNorm reaches 1.33. LayerNorm reaches 2.65, and RMSNorm reaches 2.65. BatchNorm wins this toy because the batch statistics stay clean here. The regime decides the winner: small batches and sequences favor per-example statistics.

Nugget 6 · No norm, no motion From the lesson Section 7.3 of the volume
The lesson

Why it matters. Signal scale drifts through depth until gradients stall or explode. The norm holds each layer input in a range the next layer can use.

Transfer. Check activation scale layer by layer when a deep stack stalls. A flat loss with wild scale is a norm problem first.

Unit 8 · Initialization

Chapter 8: Initialization

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 8

8.1 The variance problem: signals compound through depth. Zero init fails because every neuron computes the same value, takes the same gradient, and updates in lockstep forever, so symmetry never breaks. Random init needs the right scale: each layer multiplies signal variance by fan_in times the weight variance. With fan_in 512 and weight variance 1, ten layers grow the signal by 512 to the power 10, about 1.2 times 10 to the power 27.

8.2 Xavier and He, derived. Xavier sets the weight variance to 2 over fan_in plus fan_out, a fit for symmetric activations like tanh. ReLU zeroes about half its inputs and halves the variance that survives a layer, so He doubles the scale: the weight variance equals 2 over fan_in. At fan_in 512 and fan_out 512, the Xavier standard deviation is 0.0442 and the He standard deviation is 0.0625.

8.3 Dead networks and diagnosis. The cheap diagnosis runs one batch at init and reads per-layer statistics. Healthy signs: activation variance near 1.0 and about half the ReLUs live. Lab 3.2 reads dead fractions at layer 30 near 41.6 percent for three inits and 53.6 percent for Xavier, so the dead fraction alone does not diagnose a network. The variance path does.

8.4 Lab 3.2: the initialization sweep. A 30-layer ReLU network takes four inits. Unit-variance init grows activation variance from 43.8 to 1.48e+54 and explodes. Small init at 0.02 shrinks variance from 0.0175 to 1.00e-30 and vanishes. Xavier decays from 0.341 to 6.10e-10. He holds the line from 0.685 to 0.965. Only He keeps the signal near order 1 across all 30 layers.

Why it matters. Variance compounds per layer, so a small scale error at init grows into a dead or wild network before the first step.

Transfer. Forward one batch at init and read the variance path. Fix the scale before any GPU hours burn.

Unit 9 · Mixed precision

Chapter 9: Mixed Precision

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 9

9.1 FP16 vs BF16: range against precision. Mixed precision runs the heavy compute in 16 bits and keeps master weights and sensitive steps in FP32. FP16 uses 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, and 10 mantissa bits, with max normal 65,504 and min normal 6.10e-5. BF16 uses 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 7 mantissa bits, with max normal 3.38e38, the FP32 range. Gradients live near 1e-6 to 1e-8, below the FP16 floor, and activations can pass the FP16 cap. Range wins over fine steps in train work, so LLM train runs standardized on BF16.

9.2 Loss scaling: rescuing the small gradients. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a scale S before the backward pass, so each gradient grows by S into the FP16 window, and a divide by S restores the true values before the step. A true gradient of 1.0e-8 reads as exactly 0.0 in FP16. With scale 1024 the stored value is 1.025e-5, and the unscale step returns 1.0e-8. Dynamic scaling shrinks S and skips the step on any overflow, and grows S after clean runs. BF16 needs no scaler because the exponent range matches FP32. The master weights stay in FP32 because an update below the FP16 step size near 1.0 rounds to no change at all.

9.3 Lab 3.4: the numerics laboratory. The range test shows 1e5 and 1e30 as infinity in FP16, and as 9.984e4 and 9.953e29 in simulated BF16. The precision test adds 0.001 near 1.0: FP32 gives 1.001000, FP16 gives 1.000977, and BF16 rounds back to 1.000000 because the step size near 1.0 is 2 to the power minus 8. FP16 fails at the extremes, BF16 fails near 1.0, and train work lives at the extremes.

Unit 10 · Checkpointing

Chapter 10: Gradient Checkpointing

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 10

10.1 The memory wall: activations dominate. The backward pass needs forward values, so the system saves activations for every layer. Activation memory grows with layers times sequence length times batch size times hidden size, and the weights enter the count one time. The lab model, with 24 layers, hidden size 1024, sequence 2048, batch 8, and FP16, needs about 14 GB for weights and 36.24 GB for activations with attention scores stored, or 10.47 GB with FlashAttention. The lab marks these counts as illustrative, and the ratio between strategies is the lesson.

10.2 The tradeoff: forget and recompute. Checkpoint steps save only every k-th layer activation set and recompute the rest during the backward pass. The reference result gives memory near the square root of the layer count. The price is one extra forward pass, about 33 percent more compute. A checkpoint at every 4 layers cuts activation memory 5.8 times without FlashAttention, from 36.24 GB to 6.24 GB, and 5.4 times with FlashAttention, from 10.47 GB to 1.95 GB. The method saves activations alone: parameters, gradients, and optimizer state stay in place.

10.3 Lab 3.5: the memory-math lab. The lab counts bytes per layer at 1.51 GB with attention scores stored and 0.44 GB with FlashAttention. A checkpoint at every layer drops activation memory to 0.81 GB in both modes at the same 1.33 times compute. The two methods multiply: FlashAttention removes the score matrix, and checkpoint steps forget the rest in a controlled way.

5.8xless activation memory at 1.33x compute
Nugget 8 · Forget on purpose, at a price From the lesson Section 10.2 of the volume
The lesson

Checkpoint steps at every 4 layers cut activation memory from 36.24 GB to 6.24 GB, a 5.8-fold cut, for about 33 percent more compute.

Why it matters. Activations, not weights, set the memory wall at long sequences. A planned recompute buys room that no weight trick can buy.

Transfer. Budget activations first in any capacity plan: layers times sequence times batch times hidden size.

Unit 11 · The bridge

Chapter 11: Bridge to Volume 4

From the lesson 03-deep-learning-for-researchers.html, chapter 11

11.1 The fixed-window wall. A fixed-window model sees only the last W tokens. Content before the window is not hard to use. The content is absent. No optimizer, norm, or init scale restores information the model never receives. Past the cutoff, accuracy is chance by construction.

11.2 The road to Volume 4. Volume 4 builds the transformer and removes the window with attention, where each position reads every other position in a direct step. This volume slots into that build in full: neurons and layers become the MLP blocks, loss through optimizers becomes the train loop, norm and init become signal control, and precision plus checkpoint steps become the scale budget.

11.3 Lab 3.6: the copy task. The lab sets a window of 8 tokens and a label that depends on the first token. At sequence lengths 4 and 8 the score is 100 percent. At length 12 the score falls to 54.06 percent, and at lengths 16, 24, and 32 the score stays near 50 percent, the chance line. The target token sits outside the window, and no tune step can fix an absent input.
